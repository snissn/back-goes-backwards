## Relation, timing, response, and inference audit

The draft keeps four operational objects separate.

1. **Instability is a relation claim.** The selected relation must leave an accepted set or fail a recovery criterion under a defined demand. The accepted set can be asymmetric, phase-dependent, support-dependent, and person–device specific; it is not one ideal trajectory.
2. **Discoordination is a timing claim.** It requires an event marker and reliable participant timing. The timing difference matters only if it predicts relation loss, correction, exposure, or outcome.
3. **Correction is an action.** It can answer relation loss, altered timing, uncertainty, anticipated demand, learning, or ordinary error. It is not itself evidence of a failure family.
4. **Bracing is a strategy.** It changes stiffness or available motion around stated boundary conditions. It can occur before a perturbation and without demonstrated instability.

Instability and discoordination are neither exhaustive nor mutually exclusive. The prose permits a timing change to precede relation loss, relation loss to provoke a timing change, or successful correction to preserve the relation despite altered timing. It also separates Chapter 11's route availability from Chapter 14's timed participation: an available route can be mistimed, and an unavailable route can prompt a well-timed substitute.

Measurements retain their identities. Motion, contact, force, electromyography, calculated joint moment, modeled stiffness or exposure, pain, uncertainty, and effort do not silently become one control variable. Surface electromyography is not muscle force; a net moment is not one muscle's contribution; visible motion is not an internal control instruction.

## Affirmative variability and bracing audit

Capable variability is part of the argument before instability is interpreted. The draft uses task-tolerance, covariation, alternate joint configurations, similar damping with different recovery actions, and direction-dependent walking strategies to show that a stable task variable can coexist with variable contributors. It explicitly includes learning, anatomy, training, aging, disability, prostheses, canes, rails, altered environments, and another person as legitimate sources of capable solutions rather than deviations from a default body.

The required null is substantive: when the declared relation remains controlled, the strategy adapts to relevant perturbations, and the selected exposure does not meaningfully rise, visible variability is not demonstrated instability. The reciprocal limit is also retained. Endpoint success does not prove equal internal exposure among strategies; separate quantities require separate measurement.

Bracing appears first through preserved function. Anticipatory stabilization, reactive protection, learned precision, chosen safety, and continued participation all remain legitimate jobs. The Nielsen ankle experiment supports a bounded co-contraction–stiffness relation with participant and condition variation. The Granata–Marras study supports simultaneous modeled stability benefit and modeled compression cost in selected lifting tasks. Neither is turned into a universal activation rule.

Potential cost always has a denominator: modeled force, compression, activation time, effort, fatigue, recovery, restricted options, or another declared result. Greater activation does not establish fear, pathology, inefficiency, or sensory deficit. Lower activation does not establish improvement. Stiffness remains direction-, state-, timescale-, task-, and boundary-dependent rather than a body-wide scalar.

## Repeated-correction audit

The repeated-correction section applies Chapter 10's temporal grammar without reteaching generic dose. Each event can retain perturbation, relation departure, response, recovery, and the state before the next event. Later events are not treated as identical copies because expectation, activation, contact, fatigue, confidence, and strategy may change.

The Wang and Xu studies are used only for selective repeated-task adaptation. The draft does not infer injury from repeated correction, transfer Xu's chronic-ankle-instability group label to the hypothetical participant, or treat one co-contraction pattern as a general clinical signature.

Count, time outside relation, impulse, work, activation, excursion, and other summaries remain noninterchangeable. A small corrective event becomes mechanically relevant through repetition only when a named cumulative quantity changes. The prose explicitly keeps the resulting record at the exposure-history level. Receiver-specific local exposure, capacity, biological response, chronology, and alternatives would be required before fatigue, pain, injury, or degeneration could enter. Chapter 19 retains that bridge.

Learning, stable adaptation, reduced correction, reorganized correction, assistance, and deliberate precision remain equal branches. The chapter therefore gives repeated control activity mechanical status without making it presumed damage.

## Sensory-loop audit

The project-native feedback hypothesis is stated directly and then decomposed. Candidate initiators include position, motion, tissue state, pain, expectation, and instruction. The selected sensory measure can change, remain unchanged, or be unmeasured. Bracing can change, remain unchanged, or precede the sensory finding. Mechanical options can improve, worsen, or remain adequate. Protection can resolve, persist, be replaced, or have no demonstrated consequence.

The experimental-pain evidence supplies positive and null branches without being made to support the full loop:

- Weerakkody and colleagues support site- and measure-specific changes plus nearby-control and other-perception nulls, with the sample correctly reported as twelve total, ten to twelve per condition, and nine completing all four;
- Matre and colleagues support a high-intensity, two-antagonist-muscle movement-detection result with preserved position recognition; and
- Summers and colleagues supply a sustained-pain null for measured wrist joint-position error in twenty-eight healthy participants.

The draft distinguishes movement-direction detection, movement-detection threshold, and position matching rather than treating proprioception as one scalar. A positive result for one measure does not become a generic sensory deficit; a null result for one measure does not establish that every sensory or motor process is unchanged.

Reverse and common-cause directions remain explicit. Pain, instruction, expectation, task demand, fatigue, inflammation, habit, learning, environment, or measurement error can initiate or explain the observed sequence. The loop gains support only through time order, covariation, manipulation, and weaker competing accounts. No autonomic, immune, metabolic, chronic-symptom, or body-wide regulatory conclusion enters the draft; Chapter 16 owns broader scale-transition questions, and Chapter 21 owns the fuller pain, fear, behavior, and causal analysis.

## Internal decision and next action

Chapter 14 passes its controlled first-draft gate. It makes the archive's control-history thesis concrete without making unusual movement, variability, correction, or bracing into pathology. The draft distinguishes relation failure from timing mismatch, gives capable variability and protective stiffness full mechanical standing, converts repetition into a named-quantity history rather than presumed damage, and retains the sensory–bracing proposal as a testable multi-directional hypothesis.

The chapter is ready for Chapter 15's compensation work and later Part III integration. It is not publication-approved. Chapter 15 may now begin from what the strategy preserved, follow only persistent solutions into the next task state, and ask whether they remain useful, are replaced, or change later demand.
