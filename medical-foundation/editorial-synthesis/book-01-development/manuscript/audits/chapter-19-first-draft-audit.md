## Exposure, quantity, dose, and recovery audit

The opening uses three direct but deliberately noncommensurable measurements. Tibial surface axial strain, three-dimensional Achilles deformation, and cartilage intratissue strain remain tied to their sites, tasks, participants, methods, and frames. The manuscript does not call body weight, ground reaction, exercise name, or joint moment a tissue dose.

The demand-to-tissue scale stack separates observation and measurement from calculation, modeling, inference, and unknown response. Local strain retains direction, sign, reference configuration, spatial distribution, rate, sampling, and model-dependence where material. A partial measurement remains useful without becoming a complete field.

Dose is treated as a history rather than a repetition count. The bone rest-insertion example preserves periosteal response, fewer cycles in the mouse rest condition, and selected endocortical nulls. The tendon examples keep acute synthesis, longer-term stiffness or modulus change, cross-sectional-area null, and mechanical-deformation recovery on different clocks. Cartilage deformation and recovery remain separate from ex-vivo biosynthesis and injury protocols.

The draft does not derive a human interval, readiness measure, tissue threshold, exercise prescription, or universal recovery time. Mechanical homeostasis remains organizing vocabulary for dynamic ranges and changing capacity, not one body-wide set point.

## Receiver-state and mechanotransduction audit

Bone, tendon, and cartilage are represented as different material receivers rather than three examples of one generic tissue law. Their candidate inputs, state modifiers, response families, nulls, and principal transfer limits remain distinct. Parallel matrix columns are explicitly organizational and do not imply commensurability.

The state discussion locates variation in geometry, material state, prior history, task execution, exposure estimate, assay, and observation time. Age, endocrine or metabolic context, medication, inflammation, prior injury, hydration, and recovery are introduced only as named modifiers of a receiver or relation. They do not function as a late whole-person disclaimer.

Mechanotransduction is defined as a family of physical-to-biological processes and is never used as a universal proof engine. The draft distinguishes deformation from sensing, sensing from response, acute assay change from retained adaptation, signaling from harm, remodeling from degeneration, and no detected response from no event anywhere.

The response fork includes object, direction, time, and alternative tests. A branch receives a useful or adverse label only under a declared outcome. The draft does not infer net matrix accumulation from synthesis, universal benefit from stiffness, degeneration from signaling, or absence of response from one null measure.

Study layers remain visible. Human in-vivo exposure measurements do not inherit cell response from explants. Animal fatigue or overuse studies do not become human clinical histories. Cross-sectional state/deformation association does not establish chronology. The IL-1β compression experiment remains a biology-first cell-culture interaction rather than a disease or exercise conclusion.

The adverse mechanical thesis is preserved where the evidence directly supports it. Repeated or high-rate exposure can alter tissue organization, viability, material behavior, signaling, and repair within named models. The draft does not dilute those observations into “many factors matter.” It also preserves the positive, null, ordinary, and recovery branches needed to prevent adverse evidence from becoming deterministic.

## Feedback and clinical-separation audit

The reciprocal model requires at least an exposure state, a response or material state, and a later mechanical state. Increased or reduced stiffness, altered geometry, remodeling, hydration, injury, recovery, task selection, and assistance can change the mechanics of a later task. The draft includes stabilizing, amplifying, recovery, biology-first, behavior- or context-first, common-cause, and null possibilities.

No source is made to prove a complete self-amplifying loop. Evidence for exposure-to-response does not prove response-to-redistribution, and evidence that an altered tissue deforms differently does not identify the initiating cause. If the return measurement is absent, the return arrow remains a hypothesis.

The clinical ladder keeps five transitions separate: exposure to response, response to finding, finding to function or symptom, symptom and examination to diagnosis, and diagnosis with context to outcome. Asymptomatic spine and knee findings and the tendon prediction review support non-equivalence without implying structural nihilism. The manuscript stops at the measured object while preserving that object's legitimate significance.

No diagnosis, differential, prognosis, prevention claim, treatment, self-test, exercise cue, tissue threshold, referral rule, or individualized recommendation enters the chapter.

## Internal decision and next action

Chapter 19 passes its controlled first-draft gate. It supplies the biological middle that Chapters 10–18 deliberately withheld: a named local exposure enters a living receiver whose state, dose history, and recovery conditions shape several possible responses. Bone, tendon, and cartilage make the argument concrete without being reduced to one material law or one adverse sequence.

Its distinct contribution is reciprocal receiver specificity. Mechanical exposure can produce a measured biological response, and biological change can alter the mechanics of a later task. Both directions are substantive; neither identifies a complete clinical history by itself. The clinical-separation ladder preserves the valid mechanical and biological result while preventing it from silently becoming a finding, symptom, diagnosis, or outcome.

The chapter is ready for controlled Part IV integration. It is not publication-approved. Chapter 20 may now ask what changes when the receiver's ordinary mechanical job depends on deformation, patency, excursion, glide, containment, conduction, or pressure rather than assuming that a vessel, nerve, airway, sliding interface, or cavity behaves like bone, tendon, or cartilage.
