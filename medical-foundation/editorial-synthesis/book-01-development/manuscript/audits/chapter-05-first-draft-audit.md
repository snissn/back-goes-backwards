## Skeletal-first accounting and hard/soft audit

The authorial preference for beginning the accounting with skeletal geometry is present rather than hidden. It is explicitly separated from Book II's formal skeletal-primary routing assumption and given survival conditions:

1. the declared skeletal graph must help close force-and-moment balance;
2. its nodes and interfaces must correspond to anatomical contacts rather than a decorative line;
3. a changed configuration must yield an observable change in moment, contact, or candidate sharing;
4. omitted active, tensile, pressure, and external-support terms must remain visible, while mass and acceleration stay in dynamic accounting; and
5. a simpler or competing model is allowed to replace it when the skeletal graph adds no explanation or prediction.

The chapter recalls Chapter 3's TCM-motivated hard/soft distinction once and applies it rather than redefining it. Bone performs a comparatively shape-preserving role under declared task conditions while remaining deformable over smaller spatial or longer temporal scales. Ligament can perform a firmer tensile role in one configuration without becoming permanently hard. Neither bone, mineralization, force transmission, posterior anatomy, strength, health, nor benefit is equated with the hard role.

Form-receiving systems remain present even in the skeletal reduction. Cartilage deformation, pressure, interface behavior, active force, tendon, capsule, and external support are named where they change the route. Chapter 6 receives their positive component mechanics. Chapter 13 continues to own adverse substitution and constraint-maintained deformation.

## Quantity, contact, and inference audit

The draft preserves the quantity stack at every approach to local exposure:

```text
task and phase
    -> observed geometry in a declared frame
    -> external contacts and external force-and-moment inventory
    -> mass, acceleration, and dynamic balance
    -> modeled active force and joint resultant
    -> modeled contact distribution
    -> candidate local pressure, stress, or strain
```

No later node is inferred directly from an earlier appearance. Contact location, contact area, contact pressure, local stress and strain, and tissue response are defined as separate objects. The in-vivo contact study supports moving locations, not a pressure field. Instrumented implants support task- and support-dependent joint force, not a universal value. The ligament studies remain separated by measured object and experimental design.

The only use of *alignment* declares a room-fixed frame and one bounded consequence: a forearm–object relation may alter a modeled elbow moment. The sentence explicitly excludes optimality, local tissue stress, capacity, symptoms, and health. Every other comparison uses configuration, geometry, contact, or relation rather than visual alignment shorthand.

The draft also preserves a legitimate null. If a skeletal graph cannot close balance, cannot distinguish an observation, is reversed by an omitted term, or adds nothing beyond an ordinary free body, it should be weakened or set aside. Mechanical consequence is not coded as adverse; changed moments, contacts, supports, and strategies require separately selected endpoints before they can be ranked.

## Recurring-task and stability audit

The primary example remains the ordinary transfer rather than a lifting lesson. It uses the canonical phase labels:

- phase 1 for changing acceptance while the first support remains;
- phases 2–3 for departure and redirection;
- phase 4 for changing foot contact during carry; and
- phase 5 for transfer onto the second support.

The draft redraws the event at object, person-plus-object, selected-segment, joint, and local-interface boundaries. It includes two visibly different viable configurations, one retained-support or assisted variant, and a matched-looking but mechanically different support comparison. No panel is labeled correct, safe, symmetrical, normal, efficient, or healthy.

The stability section treats maintenance and recovery as task-specific relations. It permits motion, asymmetry, assistance, yielding, and changing recruitment to participate in a stable solution. It separates whole-object stability, hand correction, joint excursion, and ligament recruitment by scale. It also specifies what a later perturbation study would need: direction and magnitude of disturbance, initial state, support, selected relation, observation interval, and recovery criterion.

## Internal decision and next action

Chapter 5 passes its controlled first-draft gate. It converts Chapter 4's spatial grammar into anatomy, uses skeletal-first accounting as a useful but defeasible opening reduction while reserving formal skeletal-primary routing for Book II, establishes bone as living and deformable, replaces fixed hinges with moving distributed contact, separates ligament length from force, and traces a cooperative skeletal graph across the recurring transfer. It shows why configuration matters without converting appearance into exposure or quality, and it defines stability as an achieved relation under declared conditions.

The draft is ready for the Chapter 5 → Chapter 6 seam and later Part II integration. It is not publication-approved. Specialist review may narrow individual bone, knee, support, or stability passages without removing the chapter's valid job: to show how skeletal geometry creates changing shape-preserving possibilities that active, tensile, compliant, sliding, pressure-bearing, and control systems must make usable.
