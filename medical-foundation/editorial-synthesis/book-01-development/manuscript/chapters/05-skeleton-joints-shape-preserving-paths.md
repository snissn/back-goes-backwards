# Chapter 5 — Skeleton, joints, and shape-preserving paths

The object is leaving its first support. A moment earlier, the table carried most of its weight. Now the hands are accepting the contact, the object is accelerating, and the person's contacts with the floor help complete the external balance. This is phase 2—departure—in the ordinary, non-prescriptive transfer that has followed us through Part I.

Chapter 4 ended by asking which anatomical structures make a three-dimensional load path possible. The skeleton is the clearest place to begin. Bones preserve gross relations more readily than many neighboring tissues over the timescale of an ordinary task. Joint surfaces let one bone move relative to another while still exchanging force. Ligaments and capsules constrain which relations remain available. Together, these structures supply geometry through which demand can be organized.

But the word *skeleton* can encourage two opposite mistakes. One is to picture an inert rack on which the active body hangs. The other is to treat visible skeletal arrangement as a complete code for mechanical quality. Neither picture survives the object transfer. Bone deforms. Joint contact moves. Ligaments are recruited differently as configuration changes. Muscle, tendon, pressure, cartilage, sensory control, and external support all help determine whether any proposed skeletal route exists. A still image does not tell us how those contributions are shared.

We will therefore use a deliberate first-order assumption: for gross gravitational and externally applied structural demand, skeletal geometry can be treated as a principal route of resolution. *Skeletal-primary routing* is a modeling choice, not a conclusion that every internal force travels through bone or that other tissues are secondary. It earns its place only when a skeletal representation clarifies the contacts, moments, and candidate distributions in a defined task. Where active force, pressure, or external support changes the answer, those terms must remain in the model; mass and acceleration must remain in dynamic accounting.

This chapter follows the object from hand to ground at several boundaries. Its question is not which posture is correct. It is how bones, joints, and recruited restraints create relatively shape-preserving possibilities in a body that must keep moving.

## Geometry establishes possibilities, not solutions

A bone's length, curve, cross-section, orientation, and relation to neighboring structures affect what can happen around it. Articular surfaces determine where contact can occur. The distance from an external force's line of action to a joint reference changes the external moment about that joint. A curve or change in cross-section changes how a simplified beam model represents bending or torsion. A surface orientation changes its normal direction and, together with the contact model, the tractions that the interface can support. These are mechanical constraints, but they do not select one response by themselves.

Consider the object during departure. Its position relative to the hands and upper limbs helps set external moment arms. The relative positions of the hands, forearms, upper arms, trunk, pelvis, and feet establish a linked geometry. Yet many internal solutions can remain compatible with the same visible arrangement. Muscle forces can differ. Contact can be shared differently between two hands or two feet. A table edge, rail, mobility aid, or another person's assistance can add an external support. Acceleration can differ even when two images look similar. Geometry defines a feasible set; contact, active state, material state, and time select among the possibilities.

The same distinction applies locally. A joint surface may permit a range of rotations and translations, but muscles, ligaments, capsule, pressure, contact, and the applied task determine which part of that range is used. A long bone may be represented as a rigid segment for one whole-body calculation, but that simplification does not turn the living bone into a rigid material. A planar linkage may answer a question about one external moment while being inadequate for an out-of-plane contact question.

This is the anatomical form of Chapter 4's configuration rule. Skeletal geometry is part of configuration, not a substitute for it. It tells us which contacts, moment arms, and movements are possible under a declared frame and phase. It does not tell us which force-sharing solution occurred, what the local tissue stress was, or whether the result was desirable.

Thinking in terms of a feasible set changes what an anatomical explanation should try to do. It should not begin by naming one position as the answer. It should first identify the contacts and movements that the anatomy permits, the external interactions that must be reconciled, and the alternatives still compatible with those facts. Only then can a model ask whether one configuration changes a moment, brings a restraint into tension, relocates contact, or removes an available route. The observation becomes informative through the difference it predicts.

The skeletal-primary assumption is useful at this stage because it offers a tractable first graph. Bones become segments or deformable structural members; joints become changing interfaces; external supports become boundary contacts. If that graph helps close the selected force-and-moment balance and predicts a change that can be observed, it has done real work. If the same result follows from a simpler free-body diagram, or if an omitted active, pressure, or support term reverses the conclusion, the skeletal graph should be weakened or set aside for that question. “Primary” cannot mean immune to comparison.

Nor does a first graph have to be a unique graph. One analyst might divide the upper limb into hand, forearm, and upper arm; another might combine the hand and object or isolate one joint interface. Both can be valid if their boundaries answer different questions and the transitions between them are explicit. The danger is not simplification. It is allowing a convenient anatomical line to acquire conclusions that its boundary and measurements cannot support.

## Bone preserves gross relation while deforming

Chapter 3 used *hard role* for a comparatively shape-preserving or structurally resolving relation under declared conditions. Bone is the clearest anatomical example, but it is useful precisely because it corrects the idea that shape preservation requires perfect rigidity. In one direct experiment, strain gauges attached to the surface of a living person's tibial shaft recorded deformation during activity.[^05-01] The study involved one person and one sampled region. It does not provide a population norm or the full three-dimensional field inside the bone. It establishes the narrower point we need: a human load-bearing bone can measurably deform while continuing to function as a gross structural route.

Bone also does not respond identically to every direction and mode of loading. Experimental work on compact and cortical bone has found differences across tension, compression, torsion, and specimen orientation, with measured properties also related to tissue composition, porosity, age, sex, and sampling context.[^05-02] These findings justify direction- and mode-specific material descriptions. They do not make the whole body one orthotropic material, and they do not select the preferred directions proposed for later books.

At the whole-bone level, external and active forces can produce combinations of compression, tension, bending, shear, and torsion. The relative contribution of each depends on geometry, contacts, force directions, and the boundary chosen for analysis. Calling a femur or tibia a compression member may be a useful first reduction in a bounded model; calling it a pure compression strut is usually a stronger claim. The muscle forces that stabilize or move a segment can add bending or compression even when the external task appears simple.

Material behavior and structural behavior must also remain separate. A small cortical specimen can be tested along a selected orientation to estimate a material relation under specified conditions. A whole bone includes several tissues, irregular geometry, curvature, internal cavities, attachments, and spatially distributed demand. Its response depends on both the tested materials and the structure they form. A material modulus cannot be assigned unaltered to the effective stiffness of an entire limb, just as the visible curve of a bone cannot reveal its complete stress field.

This distinction explains why an analyst may legitimately treat a bone as rigid in one model and deformable in another. For a gross transfer calculation over a short phase, measured bone deformation may be small enough that including it would not change the selected external result. For a question about local strain, fatigue, fracture, remodeling, or implant interaction, the same assumption could erase the quantity of interest. “Rigid segment” describes a model decision. It is not an anatomical claim.

The living state also matters. Longitudinal observations have related changes in femoral geometry and estimated strength to development, lean mass, sports participation, and sex-steroid measures, while laboratory fatigue experiments have shown accumulation of microdamage and changing compliance in cortical specimens under specified cyclic loading.[^05-03] These are different forms of evidence. The first does not isolate mechanics as the sole cause of structural development. The second does not show that ordinary posture produces inevitable damage in a living person. Together they prevent two errors: treating bone as unchanging architecture, and treating every repeated exposure as injury.

Bone can therefore be comparatively shape-preserving over a step, a carry, or a perturbation while remaining deformable, history-dependent, and biologically changeable over other scales. A rigid-segment model is often legitimate when its deformation would not change the answer. The model must be withdrawn when local strain, material failure, healing, or long-term structural change becomes the question.

This relative account also clarifies the TCM-motivated hard/soft polarity introduced in Chapter 3. Bone does not occupy a permanent metaphysical class called hard. In a declared task relation, its material and geometry often make it an effective shape-preserving carrier. At a smaller scale or over a longer interval, its geometry and material state can change under demand. That change does not by itself assign a soft role; role still follows the selected relation and conditions.

## Joint surfaces make the path move

A stack of separate bones does not create a route. Demand must cross the interfaces between them, and an articular interface is not a frictionless pin. Its surfaces have geometry. Cartilage is present and deformable. Contact is distributed over an area, and both the location and distribution can change as the joint moves. Ligaments, capsule, muscle, and external demand help determine the relation between the surfaces.

Measurements from instrumented knee implants make the task dependence concrete. In a small, selected post-arthroplasty cohort, measured tibial-component force and moment components differed across walking, stair use, standing, sitting, and knee bending.[^05-04] These values are not universal reference loads. They show why “the load at the knee” is incomplete without task and phase. A joint interaction is a changing force-and-moment relation, not one constant number attached to an anatomical name.

Contact location changes as well. In one study, magnetic-resonance-based knee models and dual fluoroscopy were used to estimate tibiofemoral contact kinematics in five people during a quasi-static lunge. Contact points moved through flexion, and the medial and lateral sides did not follow identical paths.[^05-05] This is enough to reject the picture of one fixed hinge point. It is not enough to infer the full contact patch, pressure distribution, cartilage stress, biological response, or a healthy trajectory.

Those distinctions matter because the words can otherwise slide together:

- **contact location** identifies where an interaction is estimated to occur;
- **contact area** describes the extent of the engaged interface under a stated definition;
- **contact pressure** describes how normal traction is distributed over the engaged interface;
- **material stress** and **strain** describe local states under a selected continuum model; and
- **tissue response** is a later biological question.

Changing joint configuration can affect all of them, but each step requires additional measurement or modeling. A shift in estimated contact location does not by itself reveal pressure. An estimated pressure field does not by itself reveal tissue response. A visible segment relation does not identify either one.

*Congruence* is likewise a geometric relation, not a verdict. It describes how contacting surfaces correspond under a stated position and representation. Greater geometric correspondence may increase a candidate contact area in one model, but cartilage deformation, active forces, restraints, and task phase still affect the actual interaction. A less congruent appearance is not automatically unstable, and a more congruent one is not automatically low-stress, healthy, or preferred.

A simple hinge can remain useful when the question truly needs only one dominant rotation and an aggregate moment. The richer contact model becomes necessary when migration, distributed pressure, secondary motion, restraint, or local exposure carries the conclusion. This is another survival test: anatomical realism is valuable when it changes an inference, not merely when it makes the diagram look more bodily.

The joint therefore changes the meaning of continuity. A skeletal route does not pass through a series of fixed centers. It crosses moving, distributed interfaces whose active and material conditions vary by phase. During the object transfer, the route available at initial contact is not identical to the one available during acceleration, carry, or placement, even if the same bones remain involved.

## Ligaments guide through changing recruitment

Ligaments are often introduced as passive restraints that stop a joint at the edge of motion. The description catches one possible job but hides the more useful one. A ligament can help guide an articulation because its fibers and bundles become tensioned differently as position, direction, and demand change. Recruitment can be gradual and nonuniform. Some fibers may be comparatively slack while others contribute more strongly to constraint.

An in-vivo knee study reconstructed the lengths of collateral-ligament bundles through flexion in five healthy knees. Different bundles lengthened, shortened, or changed little rather than behaving as one uniform rope.[^05-06] The reported object here was reconstructed length, not force. Length change can help identify a possible recruitment pattern, but force additionally depends on reference length, material behavior, boundary conditions, and surrounding structures.

A separate cadaveric robotic experiment estimated forces in selected ligamentous and capsular structures during a simulated pivot-shift test. Relative contributions changed with flexion angle under that provocative laboratory protocol.[^05-07] This supports the narrower claim that restraint forces can be configuration-dependent. It does not describe ordinary carrying, every knee, or every ligament. Keeping the two studies separate prevents a common evidentiary shortcut: a length observation in living motion cannot borrow the force status of a different cadaveric experiment.

The mechanical role is nevertheless clear enough for this chapter. A recruited ligament can limit one motion, guide a contact relation, and share demand while other motion remains possible. In Chapter 3's language, it may become comparatively shape-preserving along a selected tensile relation while remaining deformable and form-receiving in other directions. The role can change across the range. Neither slackness nor tension is inherently good; each has meaning only relative to the task and needed excursion.

Ligamentous guidance is never the whole stability account. Articular geometry, capsule, muscle force, pressure, sensory information, and external support participate. The provocative experiment does not turn this chapter into a diagnostic or treatment argument. Its positive point comes first: changing restraint helps turn separate bones into a guided, moving architecture.

## Configuration changes the mechanical problem

We can now compare configurations without making either one an ideal. Take the same object, task phase, and room-fixed side-view frame. In one viable transfer, the object is kept nearer the elbow and trunk. In another viable transfer, it is held farther forward while the person changes segment positions and active force. The object's weight is the same, but its external moment arm about the selected elbow or trunk reference can differ. A model that balances that external moment will generally require a different combination of muscle, joint, and support interactions.

The reasoning chain must remain explicit:

```text
declared task and phase
    -> observed segment and object geometry in a named frame
    -> external contacts and external force-and-moment inventory
    -> mass, acceleration, and dynamic balance
    -> modeled active forces and joint resultant
    -> modeled articular contact distribution
    -> candidate local pressure, stress, or strain
```

The first four levels do not uniquely determine the last three. More than one muscle-force solution can balance a joint moment. A joint resultant does not specify how pressure is distributed across cartilage. Material stress requires geometry, boundary conditions, and constitutive assumptions. The chain tells us why configuration can matter without permitting a leap from appearance to tissue exposure.

Now add a third variant. The person keeps part of the object in contact with the table while sliding or tilting it, or receives assistance from another person. The visible limb relation may resemble one of the unsupported configurations, but the external boundary has changed. The table or helper carries part of the interaction. In a related empirical comparison, instrumented hip and knee implants showed that water depth, movement speed, and added drag altered measured joint forces during aquatic tasks; buoyancy reduced force in some conditions, while speed or resistance could increase it.[^05-08] The selected implant cohort and aquatic tasks do not provide a rule for object transfer. They establish the general counterexample: similar visible segment relations can coexist with different support and internal demand.

The converse is also important. Two visibly different configurations may solve the same external task successfully. Different body dimensions, available motion, disability, fatigue, training history, object geometry, or environmental support can make different strategies mechanically viable. Descriptive difference is not evidence of failure.

The term *alignment* is therefore useful only when its frame and consequence are stated. For example, an observed forearm–object relation in a room-fixed frame may alter the modeled moment about the elbow. That is a bounded mechanical statement. It does not identify an optimal alignment, local tissue stress, capacity, symptom, or health state. When those intermediate terms are absent, alignment becomes a visual verdict rather than an analysis.

The comparison also shows why a mechanical consequence need not be negative. A changed moment arm may increase one active-force requirement while improving another task constraint. A shifted contact can redistribute an interface without producing an adverse tissue response. Assistance can reduce one internal demand while introducing a different contact, timing problem, or dependence on the helper. “Changed mechanics” is therefore the beginning of the comparison, not a synonym for deterioration.

To rank two configurations, the analyst must name an endpoint: task success, peak or time-varying force, contact distribution, energy, fatigue, available excursion, comfort, confidence, or another selected measure. Those endpoints can disagree, and this chapter does not compress them into one efficiency score. Book II may ask whether a broader preference relation can be defined. Here the stricter conclusion is enough: configuration changes specified mechanical relations, and those relations must be measured before they can be valued.

## A skeletal route is linked and cooperative

Return to the whole transfer. We can draw a time-varying graph whose nodes are objects, skeletal segments, joints, and external supports. The graph is a first-order description of mechanical possibility, not a wire diagram hidden inside the person.

During phase 1, contact and acceptance, the table still supports part of the object while the hands establish another interaction. During phase 2, departure, that table branch disappears and the object accelerates. During phase 3, redirection or stabilization, hand contacts and segment moments change. During phase 4, carrying, foot contacts alternate as the person and object move. During phase 5, placement, the second support progressively accepts the object and the previous route is unloaded or redirected.

At the person-plus-object boundary, internal joint and muscle forces do not appear in the external inventory. Gravity, ground contact, and any retained table or helper contact do; their resultant forces and moments must satisfy the linear and angular momentum balances. At a forearm boundary, elbow and hand interactions and segment weight enter the external inventory, while segment mass, acceleration, and angular motion enter the dynamic balance. Modeled muscle and tendon forces may also cross the boundary depending on exactly where it is drawn. At a joint-scale boundary, the aggregate joint interaction can be resolved into distributed articular contact and whichever active or tensile forces cross that chosen boundary. Moving inward does not reveal the same force at higher magnification. It creates a new mechanical problem.

Continuity in this graph has a modest meaning. Successive contact and support relations remain available so that the selected task can be completed. The object does not send one conserved force packet through the hand, radius, humerus, spine, pelvis, femur, and foot. At each selected boundary, forces and moments are balanced anew. Contributions branch, combine, and change with active control and phase.

This is why the word *primary* in skeletal-primary routing needs restraint. The skeleton supplies an intelligible graph because bones and articular geometry often preserve gross relations. But the graph functions only because muscle produces force, tendon transmits it, ligament and capsule constrain it, cartilage and fluid shape contact, pressure can support assemblies, and sensory control changes the next action. Furniture, water, tools, assistance, and the ground can bypass or redistribute internal demand. If the skeletal graph cannot close the declared balance or distinguish one observation from an alternative model, it has added a picture rather than an explanation.

## Stability is an achieved relation under conditions

The word *stability* can make the whole chapter collapse back into a picture of stillness. A bone looks stable when it does not move; a joint looks stable when it stays near a selected position; a person looks stable when the silhouette changes little. Those observations may matter, but none defines the mechanical property we need.

Here, stability means maintaining or recovering the relations required for a defined task under a defined perturbation. The definition requires at least a scale, task, state, perturbation, observation interval, and measure of success. A joint may preserve a usable relation during slow support but show greater excursion after a rapid disturbance. A person may move substantially and still keep the object under control. An asymmetrical strategy may remain stable. An external support may make a previously unavailable solution stable for the selected task.

Several quantities can help operationalize the comparison: task completion, displacement or angular excursion, contact retention, time to recover a selected relation, applied force or perturbation, active response, and current capacity. Which one matters depends on the question. Minimal motion is not a universal objective. Excessive constraint can interfere with movement that the task requires, while controlled yielding can be part of recovery.

Scale can change the answer without contradiction. The object may remain stable relative to the room while the hands make small corrections. The whole person may recover the task while one joint undergoes more excursion than in another trial. A joint may maintain articular contact while a ligament bundle changes recruitment. Each statement concerns a different relation. Calling the entire event stable or unstable without naming the selected relation hides precisely the organization the term is meant to describe.

Perturbation must be specified as carefully as scale. A slow change in object position, a brief impact, a loss of one hand contact, and an unexpected shift of the receiving surface place different demands on the assembly. A useful experiment would declare the perturbation, measure its magnitude and direction, record the starting configuration and support, and identify the time and criterion for recovery. Without those conditions, stability becomes an after-the-fact compliment for whichever strategy succeeded.

Two trials can therefore reverse their ranking when the task changes. A configuration that manages a slow carry with little visible motion may not recover as effectively from an unexpected change in object momentum. A more variable configuration may use movement to absorb and redirect that perturbation. Or the opposite may occur. The chapter does not predict the winner from appearance. It states what would have to be measured.

Capacity also prevents stability from becoming a permanent label. Active state, recent loading history, available motion, confidence, assistance, and environment can differ when the system meets the same external event. Those differences can change the available strategy without proving structural failure.

Chapter 14 will examine instability and discoordination as possible adverse patterns. The positive baseline established here is narrower: a stable solution is sufficient for its conditions, not universally correct. Motion, asymmetry, assistance, and role exchange can all belong to that solution.

## The architecture is deliberately incomplete

Bones, joint surfaces, and recruited restraints create a moving shape-preserving architecture. Bone carries combined demand while deforming. Articular contact changes location and distribution as configuration changes. Ligament bundles are recruited rather than switched on as uniform end stops. Across linked segments, the skeleton supplies a useful first graph of how gross structural demand can be reconciled with the environment.

That graph is mechanically consequential without being a code of ideal posture. Geometry changes moment arms, available contacts, and candidate force sharing. It does not directly reveal muscle force, contact pressure, tissue stress, capacity, symptoms, or quality. Two configurations can both be viable; similar appearances can conceal different internal states; external support can change the problem without greatly changing the silhouette.

The result is a positive account of skeletal organization. A shape-preserving path is not a motionless frame. It is a changing set of usable relations sustained through contact, restraint, active force, material deformation, and control. Stability is the maintenance or recovery of those relations for a selected task, scale, perturbation, state, and capacity.

The deliberate omission now becomes the next question. What actuates the skeletal possibilities, carries tension into and across them, deforms at contact, permits glide, contains pressure, and prepares the system before movement begins? Chapter 6 adds muscle, tendon, cartilage, capsules, fascia, sliding interfaces, fluids, pressure-bearing boundaries, baseline state, and the candidate tension-network models that try to integrate them.

Only after that cooperative architecture is in view will Chapter 7 map the posterior chain as a particular anatomical system. Nothing in the present skeletal reduction establishes posterior necessity or a preferred whole-body configuration; those remain separate propositions with later work to do.
