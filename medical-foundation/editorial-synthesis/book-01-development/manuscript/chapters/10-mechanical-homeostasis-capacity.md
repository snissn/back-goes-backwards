# Chapter 10 — Mechanical homeostasis, capacity, dose, time, and environment

A person moves the same manageable object to the same shelf four times. The first transfer follows an ordinary period of rest. The second comes late in a long sequence of similar transfers. The third follows an interval too short to assume that every relevant variable has recovered. Before the fourth, familiarity, a changed hand contact, a cart, or another person's assistance has changed how the task is shared.

This is a hypothetical comparison, not a test of lifting technique. Object, destination, and approximate external route remain similar. The four trials might also look similar. Yet the mechanical problem is not fully specified by appearance, object mass, or task completion. Contact forces, speed, joint moments, muscle activity, internal sharing, perceived effort, and subsequent recovery can differ. A person may finish all four transfers while the state in which each transfer ends is not the same.

The point is not that repetition hides damage. Repetition may improve coordination, leave the measured state materially unchanged, produce transient fatigue, alter symptoms, contribute to longer-term adaptation, or meet a limit. Assistance may lower one exposure while adding another or may simply make participation possible. Before any of those interpretations is chosen, the system, exposure, capacity, environment, response, and timescale have to be named.

This chapter calls that time-dependent problem *mechanical homeostasis*. The term does not identify one pose or one whole-body set point. It names a proposed ability to maintain and recover specified mechanical relations within viable, changing ranges. Capacity is the corresponding present ability of a defined system to meet and recover from a defined exposure. Neither term is useful as a hidden score. Together they provide the neutral baseline needed before later chapters ask how demand can become poorly distributed, concentrated, constrained, unstable, or compensatory.

## A viable range is not a pose

Homeostasis is often pictured as constancy: a variable departs from a target and is returned to it. Mechanical systems complicate that picture because useful function involves movement, changing contact, changing force, and temporary deformation. A hand must conform to an object. A joint must traverse a range. Muscle force changes from approach to acceptance and release. Cartilage, tendon, and other tissues deform as part of ordinary support. A viable mechanical state therefore cannot mean that all relevant quantities remain fixed.

Research on mechanical homeostasis commonly examines bounded systems such as cells, engineered tissue equivalents, vessels, or selected tissues. Within those systems, investigators ask how a preferred mechanical state is established, maintained, or restored. Even there, the sensed variable, the target, and the mechanism can remain unsettled, and the relevant range may itself change.[^10-01] Current biomechanical reviews treat mechanical homeostasis as important to tissue and organ modeling while emphasizing that neither the biology nor its mathematical representation is complete.[^10-02]

Book I extends that bounded idea as a project-level synthesis. The question becomes: can a declared task system maintain or recover task-relevant mechanical relations within a viable range as demand, state, and support change? The extension is useful only if each use identifies:

- the system and scale;
- the task and environmental conditions;
- the measured or proposed variable;
- the comparison, target, or viable range;
- the time window and recovery criterion; and
- an observation that would count against the account.

A range satisfying those conditions may include asymmetry, variation, temporary strain, fatigue, assistance, and more than one successful strategy. It may also move with development, training, injury, illness, recent work, or changing resources. Calling the range viable does not mean comfortable, symmetrical, average, optimal, or healthy. It means only that a stated function or mechanical relation remains available under the stated conditions and criterion.

The variables need not be identical at every scale. A cell-seeded construct might be studied through tension or deformation. A joint-level model might track contact, excursion, or response to perturbation. A task model might track whether output, precision, and recovery stay inside declared bounds. Moving from one scale to another requires an explicit bridge. A tissue measure cannot become whole-person homeostasis merely because both are described as mechanical, and successful performance cannot establish that every local variable remained inside a preferred range.

Nor does viability require return to one initial value. Some systems return toward a prior state; others establish a changed but workable range after practice, development, or altered support. The relevant comparison might be within one person across time, between two task conditions, or against a validated material or performance criterion. Each comparison answers a different question. The project term remains useful only when that question is visible.

The object transfer makes this distinction concrete. The system does not return to its initial geometry after every phase. It passes from support by the first surface, through hand contact and object acceptance, into carrying, placement, and release. Contacts, muscle force, pressure, and joint relations change throughout. A cart or handrail can become part of the viable system by changing its boundary. Nothing has to be “corrected” for the system to have changed successfully.

This use of mechanical homeostasis can fail. If its variables cannot be operationalized, if it adds nothing to established concepts such as stability, tolerance, or control, or if it can redescribe every result after the fact, it should be narrowed or replaced. The term earns its place by organizing comparisons that can be observed, modeled, and contradicted—not by supplying a biological preference that has not been measured.

## Capacity belongs to a system and an outcome

Capacity is often spoken of as if each tissue or person carries a fixed amount. That shorthand breaks down as soon as the endpoint changes. The capacity to complete one transfer is not the capacity to repeat it for an hour. A muscle's maximal voluntary force is not a tendon specimen's fatigue life. A joint's usable excursion is not a person's tolerance of symptoms. The ability to preserve task output is not the time required for a selected variable to recover afterward.

Here, *capacity* means the present ability of a defined system to meet and recover from a defined exposure under defined conditions. At minimum, a capacity claim needs five qualifiers:

1. **System:** tissue, interface, region, task assembly, or whole-person performance.
2. **Exposure:** the mechanical quantity, direction, magnitude, rate, configuration, and history being considered.
3. **Outcome:** completion, precision, stability, material integrity, symptom tolerance, recovery, or another declared endpoint.
4. **State:** recent loading, development, training, injury, illness, pain, sleep, medication, assistance, and other modifiers relevant to the question.
5. **Time:** one event, a sequence, a day, a training interval, or a longer trajectory.

These qualifiers do not form a generic causal checklist. Some state variables alter the mechanical system directly. A handrail adds a contact; a joint position changes geometry; active force changes internal sharing. Other variables alter tissue response, perception, behavior, or the goal of the task. Some may be incidental in a particular comparison. A useful account names the modifier and the link it is proposed to change instead of pouring every circumstance into an undefined reserve.

Scale matching prevents several common substitutions. If the outcome is tendon integrity, task completion is at most indirect evidence. If the outcome is balance during a transfer, an isolated maximum-force test does not by itself specify the available strategy. If the outcome is recovery of a measured disc variable, comfort and visible posture answer different questions. A capacity claim becomes informative when its endpoint could, in principle, be observed and when a competing endpoint could change without being mistaken for it.

The same discipline applies to thresholds. A material test can define failure through a stated force, stress, strain, or cycle criterion. A task threshold can be the point at which precision or completion is no longer maintained. A symptom-tolerance threshold concerns reported experience under stated conditions. These thresholds can coexist and need not be crossed together. “The load exceeded capacity” is therefore incomplete until both sides of the comparison have the same system, quantity or outcome, and time window.

Capacity also changes with the chosen boundary. At the boundary around a tendon specimen, failure under a specified test can be a meaningful endpoint. At a joint, the question may concern a resultant moment, local contact, or usable motion. At the person-plus-object boundary, success may mean that the object reaches the shelf. At a wider boundary, a cart, orthosis, mobility aid, or second person is not an afterthought: it is part of the system that makes the task possible.

This matters because a demand–capacity account can otherwise become a disguised judgment about the person. If an environment supplies a poor grip, an inaccessible height, forced timing, or no opportunity for support, the mismatch does not reside only inside the body. Conversely, assistance does not prove deficiency. It changes contacts, available routes, timing, and exposure. Capacity is relational by definition.

The distinction also protects the book's hard/soft model from misuse. A shape-preserving route can be capable for one direction and task while a form-receiving structure remains free to yield, glide, contain, or recover. In another phase, their roles can change. “Capacity” does not mean that soft roles should carry no demand or that hard roles never deform. It asks whether the particular division of mechanical labor preserves the declared function and the relevant freedoms over the chosen time.

## Exposure has a history

Task-level demand and internal exposure are not synonyms. *Demand* names what the task and surroundings require at a chosen boundary: support an object, oppose gravity, accelerate a segment, remain within a workspace, or tolerate a contact. *Exposure* names the resulting mechanical history at the selected tissue, interface, segment, or modeled subsystem.

The distinction is a sequence:

```text
task and environment
    -> body state, configuration, and external contacts through time
    -> measured external forces, motion, and timing
    -> calculated segment forces and moments
    -> modeled sharing among active, passive, pressure-mediated,
       skeletal, and external carriers
    -> estimated local force, stress, strain, pressure,
       deformation, or excursion history
    -> separately evidenced response and outcome
```

The same object mass can create a different moment when its distance, acceleration, support, or orientation changes. The same net joint moment can be shared differently among muscle, tendon, contact, pressure, and passive restraint. A visible activity labeled “light,” “sedentary,” “repetitive,” or “high impact” therefore does not locate a tissue dose.

The exposure descriptor introduced earlier is best treated as a structured record:

>     + rest and recovery interval

>     + measurement and model uncertainty
> ```
>

Two histories can share a peak and differ in duration. They can share nominal work and differ in rate, direction, or recovery interval. The same events can occur in another sequence, so the state at the start of each event changes. Which fields matter is an empirical question for the selected tissue and outcome, not a reason to call every descriptor equally predictive.

The word *cumulative* needs the same care. It can refer to cycle count, elapsed time, impulse, mechanical work, time above a chosen level, a fatigue-damage estimate, or simply an ordered record of prior events. Those objects have different units and assumptions. Summing repetitions ignores their direction and rate; summing work does not necessarily describe peak stress; a fatigue model calibrated for one material and loading mode cannot be transferred to another tissue by changing the label. In this chapter, cumulative exposure means that chronology remains available for analysis. It does not mean that all prior demand has been converted into one conserved quantity stored in the body.

A history can also contain recovery, variation, and learning rather than only accumulation. A later event may begin from a more capable state, a less capable state, or a state that is different along several dimensions. That is why the exposure record includes starting state and uncertainty. The record preserves possible explanatory variables; it does not prejudge their sign.

A small handgrip experiment illustrates the sequence problem. Participants completed ascending and descending submaximal force plateaus. The preceding force history affected measured fatigue accumulation, and some voluntary-force recovery appeared during a later lower-intensity plateau.[^10-03] The protocol does not yield a whole-body dose law or a recovery rule for other tissues. It shows that a list of peaks omits information that can distinguish two histories.

Occupational guidance makes a related point at a different scale. The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health treats intensity, frequency, and duration, together with combinations of force, posture, repetition, contact stress, vibration, and work organization, as distinct exposure features.[^10-04] Guidance for identifying workplace factors is not a local tissue model, but it supports a disciplined refusal to turn “more load” into one undifferentiated quantity.

History also separates performance from state. Muscle fatigue is task-specific: a measured reduction in maximal force or power does not necessarily stop a submaximal task.[^10-05] In a small repetitive-lifting study, participants maintained essentially constant movement performance and modeled lumbosacral torque while fatigue-related muscle input–output characteristics changed.[^10-06] Continued performance did not prove unchanged internal sharing. The measured fatigue response did not prove damage.

For the object transfer, at least three observations should therefore remain in separate columns. *Task output* records whether the object reached the shelf. *Mechanical state* records changes in contact, speed, moment, activation, motion, or a local exposure estimate. *Response* records a declared endpoint such as maximal-force change, perceived effort, soreness, recovery time, or a tissue measure. No one column can silently stand in for the others.

Suppose the shelf is reached at the same time in two trials. In the later trial, a hand remains on a support for longer, speed falls, and the modeled joint moment changes. Those observations establish a changed solution, not its value. The change might preserve output efficiently, reflect fatigue, exploit learning, respond to discomfort, or be ordinary variation. Additional measurements and a declared outcome are what turn the changed solution into an interpretation.

## Recovery is a process, not empty time

Rest means that a declared exposure has stopped or changed. It does not mean that every variable has returned to baseline. To claim recovery, the account must name what changed, the comparison state, the measurement, and the time at which the criterion was met.

A bounded material example makes the point. In cadaveric human lumbar discs exposed to axial creep loading and then unloaded, loading and recovery followed different time courses. Under that protocol, recovery was several times slower than loading, and measured height and stiffness approached their initial values only after long unloading intervals.[^10-07] This is evidence about cadaveric discs under a specified experiment, not a prescription for sleep, work breaks, or whole-person recovery.

Living measurement adds another caution. In a study of seventeen sedentary workers, selected quantitative magnetic-resonance indices of intervertebral-disc composition differed between morning and evening measurements.[^10-08] The study supports attention to measurement time. It does not isolate the mechanical exposure responsible for the difference, show that ordinary daytime loading is damaging, or establish a relationship to symptoms.

The broader inference is modest and useful. A snapshot need not be independent of its history. The relevant history, however, depends on the system. Maximal muscle force, tendon stiffness, disc height, perceived effort, task skill, pain, and material damage are not interchangeable recovery variables. One can return while another remains changed. A kinematic return to the starting pose is especially weak evidence: it shows geometry at one moment, not restoration of material state, capacity, physiology, comfort, or future function.

The comparison state also matters. Recovery can mean return toward a pre-exposure value, movement into a task-acceptable range, restoration of a performance criterion, or establishment of a changed baseline after adaptation. These meanings should not be mixed. If the proposed variable naturally fluctuates across a day, a single earlier measurement may not be an adequate target. If a task has been learned, exact return to the initial performance pattern may be neither expected nor desirable. The criterion must follow the scientific or functional question rather than an assumption that the earliest state was ideal.

Recovery can also occur during changed activity rather than complete inactivity. The handgrip example showed partial recovery of one voluntary-force measure during a lower-intensity phase. That observation remains task- and measure-specific. It does not establish that reducing intensity always restores capacity, that recovery is complete, or that continued task performance is harmless.

This is why universal rest intervals do not follow from the chapter's model. The model asks for a recovery window because chronology matters. The answer must still be supplied by a selected system, exposure, variable, criterion, and evidence.

## Environment changes the mechanical boundary

The environment enters mechanics through contact, constraint, support, available movement, timing, and repetition. Floor compliance changes the ground relation. A handle changes where and how an object can be contacted. A seat and backrest redistribute support. A shelf height changes geometry. A tool, visual target, vehicle vibration, sport rule, or work pace changes the task that must be solved. These features may reduce one demand, add another, or alter how demand is distributed.

They do not acquire one fixed sign. Prolonged sitting, screen use, repetitive work, specialized sport, and constrained visual attention are contexts, not diagnoses. The same chair may support one task and obstruct another. A rigid surface may improve precision while increasing local contact pressure. Variability can distribute exposure in one setting and introduce control demand in another. Consistency can preserve accuracy or maintain the same exposure. The direction of effect depends on the stated system and outcome.

Assistance belongs in this account without becoming evidence of a defective body. A cane, orthosis, exoskeleton, cart, handrail, chair, or second person supplies contact or support, changes the system boundary, and can alter the route by which demand is resolved. The result may be lower exposure at one selected structure, greater exposure elsewhere, slower task performance, improved stability, or access to a task that was otherwise unavailable. Those possibilities require measurement; none is licensed by the device label alone.

Work organization is mechanical in the same conditional sense. Timing, sequence, recovery opportunity, task switching, and control over pace can change an exposure history even when the workstation geometry does not change. Social and institutional conditions can therefore enter a mechanical account through concrete boundary variables without being reduced to mechanics. Conversely, a mechanical change does not account for every way an environment affects participation, stress, expectation, or health.

The order matters. Comparing object mass directly with “whole-body capacity” skips configuration, contacts, routing, and local exposure. Comparing a local tendon strain with a general performance score mixes boundaries and outcomes. A bounded model can relate commensurate measurements, but the model has to show that relation rather than letting a smooth diagram imply it.

Environment is therefore neither background nor culprit. It is part of the defined mechanical system. This framing preserves both responsibility and possibility: task design, social support, resources, accessibility, and available assistance can change exposure without turning every difficulty into an individual deficit or every environmental change into a cure.

## One exposure can lead to several trajectories

Mechanical exposure is not yet biological or clinical meaning. Living tissues can respond differently according to tissue, state, dose, chronology, and the outcome being measured. Even the words used for those responses refer to different observations.

Human tendon training studies provide evidence for one branch. Across healthy lower-limb interventions, a systematic review and meta-analysis found changes in tendon stiffness, modulus, and cross-sectional area, with results depending in part on the loading program and local strain. The evidence was concentrated in Achilles and patellar tendons and did not identify one universal optimum.[^10-09] Repeated exposure can therefore participate in a demonstrated longer-term change without proving that every repeated load is adaptive or that the same program transfers to another tissue.

Experimental tendon-fatigue models establish a different branch. Repeated loading can produce progressive mechanical and microstructural change in selected specimens and protocols, while translation from those models to living tendinopathy remains complex.[^10-10] Controlled animal bone-loading studies likewise distinguish adaptive lamellar responses from injury-associated responses, with magnitude, frequency, inserted rest, age, model, and endpoint affecting the result.[^10-11] Articular-cartilage mechanobiology varies with amplitude, rate, frequency, loading history, and tissue state.[^10-12] These literatures establish that outcome cannot be read from repetition alone. They do not supply a whole-body injury threshold.

The framework therefore needs a fork rather than a single arrow:

The branch labels should not be collapsed. *Accommodation* can mean that the task is completed under current conditions without establishing a lasting change. *Practice* concerns altered performance or coordination. *Adaptation* and *remodeling* require a specified change over time and are not automatically beneficial. *Fatigue* requires a task- and measure-specific decline; it is not material damage by definition. *Injury* requires its own biological or structural evidence. Symptoms are observations with their own mechanisms and context. *Failure* can refer to material failure or inability to meet a declared task criterion, and those are different endpoints.

Chronology alone does not select a branch. A symptom appearing after repeated work establishes temporal order, not the local exposure, biological response, or causal contribution. A material change observed after training does not reveal which element of the program produced it without an appropriate comparison. An absence of symptoms does not measure tissue state. The chapter's sequence is deliberately slower: first describe demand and exposure, then observe the response, then test the bridge to function, findings, symptoms, or a clinical interpretation.

A useful model must retain ordinary nulls. Two people can complete the same task with visibly different strategies and no measured adverse change in the observed period. The same person can repeat a task while skill improves and local exposure stays similar, rises, or falls. A support can change exposure without changing symptoms. Symptoms can change while the selected mechanical measure changes little. These comparisons prevent every difference from being renamed hidden overload.

The adverse branches remain important. The archive that motivated this framework asks how modest but repeated or poorly resolved demands might accumulate, shift among carriers, precede symptoms, or contribute to later consequences. Those questions are preserved, not answered by assertion. The next chapters separate interrupted handoff, concentration, maintained constraint, instability, and compensation so each proposed failure family receives its own mechanics and alternatives. Tissue-specific biology and condition-specific meaning require later bridges.

## A changing margin is not a score

The relation among task demand, selected-system exposure, and present capacity changes through time. That observation invites the language of a margin or reserve. It does not yet justify a number.

For a tested specimen under a stated loading mode, exposure and failure criteria may be expressed in commensurate quantities and a threshold may be relatively sharp. For repeated performance, the criterion may be gradual or multidimensional. For a whole person, “capacity” may include several noncommensurate endpoints, supports, and choices. Subtracting an estimated local strain from a task score, or a body-wide capacity number from object weight, would produce arithmetic without a coherent mechanical object.

*Reserve* can still name a conditional systems concept: the currently available margin to preserve a declared function while accommodating a perturbation or additional demand. The word must carry the same qualifiers as capacity. Reserve for what system, exposure, outcome, state, and time? It cannot become an invisible substance inferred whenever a posture, strategy, or environment is disliked.

A useful graphic may therefore show several bands rather than one subtraction. One band can represent a measured or modeled exposure with uncertainty. Another can represent the observed range in which a declared outcome remained available. Environmental changes can appear as alterations to the demand-to-exposure relation rather than as unexplained jumps in bodily capacity. Recovery can change the later capacity estimate. The bands may overlap without a precise crossing point, and the uncertainty may be too large to identify a margin at all. That honest result is more informative than a precise but dimensionally incoherent score.

A changing margin also does not dictate temporal order. Measured capacity can fall before task output changes. Performance can remain stable through altered sharing. Symptoms can appear before, after, or without the selected mechanical threshold. Training can increase one capacity while leaving another unchanged. Assistance can restore task availability without changing the person's tissue state. A model that permits these possibilities is more useful than a single falling line because it tells us what must be measured to distinguish them.

The opening transfers can now be interpreted without ranking them. Each begins with a task-level demand. Configuration and boundary conditions—including any cart, handrail, altered grip, or assistance—translate that demand into an exposure at the selected system. The system meets that exposure with a present, outcome-specific capacity shaped by its history. Task output, mechanical state, response, and recovery are then observed separately. Only after those records exist can the trial be called unchanged, accommodated, training, fatiguing, compensatory, injurious, or failing.

The durable conclusion is simple: a mechanical snapshot cannot carry a longitudinal judgment. Mechanical homeostasis is the proposed maintenance and recovery of declared relations within viable dynamic ranges, not return to one pose. Capacity is exposure-specific and current, not a permanent trait. Dose is a structured history, not a universal sum. Environment and assistance belong to the system boundary. Repetition can precede benefit, no material change, tradeoff, or harm, and each branch needs its own evidence.

The wider series will ask whether some whole-body organizations manage demand more efficiently than others. That hypothesis is not needed here and receives no support merely from the existence of a viable range. Book I first needs the more basic account developed in this chapter: what the task required, how the system was configured and supported, what exposure reached the selected structure, what capacity and recovery criterion were relevant, and what actually changed.

That baseline gives the next chapter its question. If demand can be distributed among several active, tensile, skeletal, pressure-mediated, and external routes, what counts as a successful handoff among them—and what observations would show that a handoff was interrupted, bypassed, or reassigned?

The review supports progressive mechanical and microstructural changes in selected tendon-fatigue models while emphasizing translational limits. Model damage does not establish a deterministic pathway to living tendinopathy.

[^10-11]: Sarah H. McBride and Matthew J. Silva, [“Adaptive and Injury Response of Bone to Mechanical Loading,”](https://doi.org/10.1038/bonekey.2012.192) *BoneKEy Reports* 1 (2012): 192. The review concerns controlled animal loading and supports dependence on magnitude, frequency, inserted rest, age, model, and endpoint. It does not provide a human whole-body threshold.

[^10-12]: Johannah Sanchez-Adams et al., [“The Mechanobiology of Articular Cartilage: Bearing the Burden of Osteoarthritis,”](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11926-014-0451-6) *Current Rheumatology Reports* 16, no. 10 (2014): 451. The review supports tissue-specific dependence on amplitude, frequency, strain rate, loading history, and tissue state and distinguishes physiological from injurious experimental regimes. It is not evidence that a visible posture causes osteoarthritis.
