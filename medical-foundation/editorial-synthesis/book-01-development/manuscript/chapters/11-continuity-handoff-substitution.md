# Chapter 11 — Continuity, handoff, leakage, interruption, and substitution

The object has reached the second support. In one version of this familiar hypothetical transfer, the person places it down without another external contact. In a second version, a forearm briefly contacts the support while the object is lowered. The object reaches the same place in both versions. Its weight is transferred to the same final surface. Yet the contacts available to the person, and therefore the possible internal allocation of demand, are not the same.

What can we conclude from the successful endpoint? We can observe the object, the support, the added forearm contact, and the timing of release. With suitable instruments, we could measure external forces and motion. From those measurements and a declared model, we could calculate net segment or joint moments. We still would not know, from the endpoint alone, how muscle force, tendon tension, joint contact, pressure-mediated support, passive restraint, and external assistance shared the task.

Chapter 10 established that task demand, local exposure, present capacity, and outcome must be kept separate. This chapter adds another separation. *Balance* describes the net force-and-moment account required at a selected boundary. *Allocation* describes how candidate structures, contacts, and mechanical roles participate inside that account. Balance constrains allocation, but it rarely selects one unique internal solution.

That distinction is the starting point for a vocabulary of transfer. A task can remain mechanically continuous while its dominant carriers change. One relation can hand a mechanical job to another. A route can be interrupted, bypassed, overloaded, supplemented, or replaced while the visible endpoint remains intact. The words become useful only when they identify what changed and what observation could show that the proposed change did—or did not—occur.

## Continuity means that the needed relations remain available

In ordinary speech, continuity suggests one thing running without a break. That image is too narrow for a living movement. The body does not need one material cable extending from the object to the floor. It needs a workable succession or cooperation of contacts, structures, and controls through which a defined demand can be received, transformed, transferred, opposed, and ultimately resolved.

We use *mechanical continuity* for that task- and phase-specific availability. The definition begins with the job rather than with a named tissue. During the placement phase, the object must decelerate, contact the support, and transfer its weight away from the hands. The person must manage the changing object contact while remaining supported. Several relations can operate in parallel, and their contributions can rise or fall over the phase. Continuity is present when the selected system retains enough of those relations to complete the declared mechanical job without requiring an unacknowledged substitute.

Availability does not mean participation. A forearm support may be within reach and remain unused. A muscle may be anatomically capable of contributing but produce little force in the selected phase. Participation does not mean dominance, and dominance does not establish that the route is preferable. These are different questions.

The relevant scale must also be stated. A visually smooth whole-body movement does not prove uninterrupted transfer at a selected interface. An abrupt-looking movement does not prove that internal continuity was lost. At the level of a muscle–tendon unit, continuity may concern active force transmitted through a deforming tendon to its attachment. At a joint, it may concern changing active moments, contact, and passive restraint. At the person–environment boundary, it may include a handrail, device, furniture, or another person. A path can be represented as a sequence, a branching network, a distributed field, or a time-varying combination. The representation should match the question rather than forcing the body to resemble a drawn line.

A complete route account also needs somewhere for the selected demand to be resolved. An arrow that begins at the object and disappears at the pelvis is not a continuous mechanical explanation. Depending on the boundary, the terminal relation may be ground contact, another external support, opposition by another segment, a change in momentum, or internal stress and pressure fields that satisfy the stated balance. During placement, the receiving surface progressively accepts the object. During walking, alternating ground contacts and changes in momentum enter. In a smaller tissue system, tractions and constraints at the region's boundary must be declared. The terminal account prevents continuity from becoming a pleasing anatomical trace with no mechanical closure.

Nor must continuity be binary. One candidate relation may have limited excursion yet still participate. Several weak contributors may cooperate. A path may be available for one direction and unavailable for another, or for one interval but not the next. At that point, a continuous numerical description of participation may be more informative than a yes-or-no label. The word remains useful as long as it identifies the relations required by the proposed account and permits partial, shared, and uncertain states.

Bounded measurements make the point concrete. During stimulated contractions of the tibialis anterior in five men, increasing contraction was accompanied by increasing estimated tendon force, stress, displacement, and strain.[^11-01] During one-legged hopping in ten men, the Achilles tendon deformed substantially and returned elastic energy to the task.[^11-02] These are direct series connections at a limited anatomical scale. They show that transmission can include deformation, storage, and return. They do not establish one continuous route through the whole body.

Continuity can also be distributed across regional contributors. In twenty-five adults walking at a self-selected speed, net hip, knee, and ankle moment contributions to a support-moment description changed across stance and covaried with one another.[^11-03] The measurements and calculations do not reveal unique muscle forces or rank the joint strategies. They demonstrate the more basic point: a whole-task support relation can continue while the dominant regional contribution changes.

This definition also leaves room for uncertainty. If the external account closes but the internal distribution has not been measured or defensibly modeled, continuity at the task boundary may be observed while continuity along a proposed internal route remains unresolved. “Unresolved” is not a failure of the task. It is the correct state of the route claim.

## A handoff changes the mechanical job

A *handoff* is a change in carrier, role, direction, scale, contact, or phase through which a task remains mechanically resolved. The term does not imply that force is a packet passed between isolated parts. It is bookkeeping for a change in how a selected quantity or relation is managed.

A muscle can generate active tension that reaches bone through tendon. A joint can redirect resultants as contact geometry changes. A tendon can store energy during one interval and return it during another. A ligament can become more engaged as configuration changes. Cartilage can deform and distribute contact. Adjacent tissues can move together and relative to one another. A pressure-supported assembly can contribute to shape preservation. An external support can accept part of a demand previously carried within the person. Several of these events may occur simultaneously.

The quantity does not have to keep the same descriptive form across the handoff. Muscle activation is not force. Tendon tension is not joint contact pressure. A segment moment is not a tissue stress. Stored strain energy is not a line of action. The relations among them require geometry, material behavior, contact, time, and an explicit model. What remains continuous is the mechanically closed task account, not one scalar label that travels unchanged through every scale.

This matters at biological interfaces. At an idealized bonded boundary, traction compatibility helps close the local mechanical account. A real attachment may grade material properties over a region, change fiber direction, deform, slide elsewhere, and distribute contact. Calling that region a handoff does not replace those mechanics. It directs attention to the interface and asks which quantity entered, which relation emerged, what changed form, and what uncertainty was introduced. If those questions cannot be answered, *handoff* is still a useful observation prompt but not yet a detailed model.

The book's compact formulation gives this changing division of labor a positive center:

> Load wants to travel through the hard body so that the soft body can remain soft.

“Wants” is a heuristic about geometry, relative stiffness, contact, active control, pressure, and available paths—not an intention possessed by force and not a new law of motion. In technical use, *load* must become the particular force, moment, pressure, contact, deformation, or exposure being discussed.

The hard/soft distinction is motivated by traditional Chinese medicine's hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts, but the traditional and mechanical categories are not definitionally identical. Book I uses the motivation to ask a mechanical question: which relations are comparatively shape-preserving, path-establishing, constraining, or structurally resolving while transmitting demand, and which must remain comparatively form-receiving and able to conform, glide, contain, expand, change pressure, or recover for the task? Force transmission alone does not answer that question.

Hard and soft are roles, not materials. They belong to a relation at a stated direction, phase, state, scale, and time. Bone often performs a hard role, but active muscle, tendon, ligament, fascia, cartilage, pressure, coordinated assemblies, and external supports can also help preserve a relation. A tissue performing a soft role is not weak, passive, unloaded, or unimportant. The same structure can transmit substantial force along one direction while receiving form, bending, or sliding in another. A handoff succeeds not by eliminating deformation but by preserving the particular freedoms each participant requires.

Pressure prevents the distinction from becoming a bone-only model. In a coupled finite-element and musculoskeletal model of static lifting, estimated effects of intra-abdominal pressure on spinal loading and stability changed with posture, coactivation, and the modeled pressure level.[^11-04] The result depends on the model and does not establish breathing as a whole-body routing driver. It does show that a deformable, pressure-mediated assembly can contribute to a relatively shape-preserving support job.

Normal deformation supplies the necessary null. Healthy knee cartilage imaged under cyclic compressive loading showed sliding displacement and regionally complex intratissue strain.[^11-05] The hopping study likewise showed tendon deformation and elastic recoil. In both examples, substantial loading and shape change are part of the studied mechanical role. Deformation is not evidence that demand escaped from a proper route. A form-receiving structure can be heavily loaded and still remain available for its work.

## Leakage must name a receiver and a mismatch

The positive hard/soft formulation implies a possible negative case. A relation needed to preserve gross organization may become unavailable or poorly allocated, while a structure that needs to receive form assumes more of a path-establishing, restraining, or load-bearing job. The project's word for this possibility is *leakage*.

Leakage is not literal fluid escape. It is not every force carried by a soft tissue, every deformation, every anterior or lateral exposure, or every departure from a visually preferred line. It is a routing hypothesis: altered allocation produces a named exposure in a named receiver that is poorly matched to the receiver's required freedom, present capacity, task, direction, dose, or recovery context.

That hypothesis must be unpacked. A leakage account needs at least:

1. the receiving structure or relation;
2. the mechanical quantity and direction at issue;
3. its magnitude, duration, repetition, phase, and other relevant history;
4. the freedom or function the receiver must retain;
5. the proposed available route and the proposed change in allocation;
6. a reason the exposure is considered poorly matched rather than merely present;
7. an alternative explanation; and
8. an observation that would disfavor the account.

Possible translations include greater or more sustained deformation, changed pressure, increased active tension, restricted excursion or glide, altered contact, a changed moment, or an exposure that exceeds a declared present capacity. Each translation names a different mechanical object. None becomes adverse merely because it is measurable.

Consider two candidate leakage stories about the added forearm support. In the first, local skin and subcutaneous tissue conform under the contact while the broader person–support relation transmits force. The tissues deform, the endpoint is preserved, and no loss of their required freedom is demonstrated. Nothing in that description qualifies as leakage. In the second story, an interface is proposed to remain deformed after the phase, to lose relative excursion needed in a later movement, and to receive a repeated exposure poorly matched to its present capacity. That account contains a leakage candidate, but only the first two statements are not enough. The maintained state, required excursion, exposure history, capacity comparison, and later result must each be observed or supported separately.

The distinction is especially important when the receiver is active. Increased muscle activation may represent an effective contribution, a chosen stabilization strategy, a response to perturbation, or a substitute carrying more of a task. To call it leakage, the account must identify the intended or required freedom that is lost, show a poorly matched exposure or cost, and compare another plausible allocation. More activation alone does not establish that demand has entered the wrong place.

Location is equally insufficient. An exposure does not become adverse because it is anterior, posterior, medial, lateral, superficial, or deep. Spatial language can identify the receiver. It cannot supply the mismatch. The same anatomical region may be well suited to one direction, phase, and dose and poorly suited to another. Leakage is therefore a receiver-and-relation claim, not a moral map of the body.

Suppose a compliant interface deforms during object placement and returns while its required excursion, task output, and later state remain within the declared comparison range. That is a normal-deformation null, not hidden leakage. Suppose a pressure-supported assembly assumes more of a support job while reducing active demand elsewhere. That may be an effective handoff rather than an improper one. The hard/soft model is not protected by renaming every successful alternative as concealed cost.

*Misrouting* adds an evaluative comparison. It proposes that one allocation is less suitable than another for the stated task, dose, capacity, and required freedoms. The criterion might concern local exposure, reserve, energetic cost, precision, stability, recovery, or another declared outcome. Without such a criterion and a comparison, misrouting means only that the observer expected something else.

Leakage and misrouting therefore remain hypotheses even when the geometry looks compelling. A felt discontinuity can motivate the same hypothesis, but embodied experience is evidence of an experience in a task and phase. It is not, by itself, measurement of the carrier or quantity. The route becomes a mechanical claim only when the proposed receiver, exposure, and alternative path can be inspected.

## Interruption, bypass, overload, substitution, and rerouting differ

A single change in posture, activity, or support is sometimes narrated as if it simultaneously proved a broken chain, excessive demand, compensation, and harm. The terms describe different states and require different comparisons.

An *interruption* means that a relation required by the proposed route is unavailable or insufficient during the stated phase. The missing requirement might be contact, excursion, geometry, force capacity, control, or another declared variable. Interruption does not identify why the relation is unavailable, show that it was necessary to every viable route, or imply total task failure.

A *bypass* means that an alternative route participates while another route that remains plausibly available is used less or not at all. Added hand support can create a bypass. A person may choose it for comfort, access, precision, speed, habit, or no measured reason. A bypass can be beneficial, neutral, costly, or simply different.

An *overload* means that an engaged relation receives an exposure exceeding a specified present capacity criterion. Chapter 10's qualifiers still apply: system, quantity, direction, history, outcome, state, and time. High force is not automatically overload. Fatigue, discomfort, or altered motion may prompt an overload question, but they do not define the required exposure–capacity comparison.

A *substitution* occurs when another structure, contact, carrier, or strategy performs enough of a role to preserve a declared endpoint. The original route may be interrupted, bypassed, reduced, or merely not chosen. Substitution is neutral until its benefit, cost, or consequence is measured. It may make a task newly possible or better suited to a person's anatomy, disability, current symptoms, available device, or environment.

*Rerouting* is the broadest temporal term. It describes a changed distribution among candidate relations over a stated interval or phase. Rerouting can include a changed load share without a categorical interruption or substitution. It should not be used as a permanent whole-body label when only one task comparison has been observed.

These states can coexist. A relation can remain partly available, continue to participate, and receive an exposure that exceeds the selected capacity criterion. An external support can bypass one route and substitute for one part of its job. A strategy can reroute demand before any failure occurs. When measurements cannot distinguish the states, the allocation is unresolved rather than secretly interrupted.

A matched comparison shows why the names matter. Imagine that the same object placement is repeated with a support positioned within comfortable reach. In one trial the support is untouched. In another it accepts forearm force for part of the lowering phase. If the original internal route was still available and the person simply chose the new contact, *bypass* may describe part of the change. If a required relation could not perform its proposed job and the contact made the endpoint possible, *interruption with substitution* is a candidate. If the original relation stayed engaged but a defined exposure exceeded a matched capacity criterion, *overload* is the relevant claim. If force sharing changed continuously without a categorical loss, *rerouting* may be enough.

The visible support contact establishes none of those internal states by itself. A matched study would need to hold the endpoint and other task features sufficiently stable, measure the external contact, estimate or measure the internal quantities that distinguish the candidates, and state the capacity criterion if overload is claimed. Even then, mixed results may be the most accurate answer. The value of the vocabulary is that each term asks for different evidence instead of letting one visible difference carry the entire narrative.

## The same endpoint can conceal different allocations

The endpoint-equivalence null is fundamental: two versions of a task can reach the same visible result through different internal organizations, and the difference can have no demonstrated adverse consequence. The reverse is also true. Similar appearance does not guarantee similar allocation.

Biomechanical redundancy makes the first possibility precise. In detailed model instances matched to the postures of three cats, broad ranges of muscle activation could produce prescribed hindlimb endpoint forces; relatively few modeled muscles had nonzero lower bounds across the tested conditions.[^11-06] The result is a model-based existence proof, not a direct measurement of human whole-body routing and not evidence that every feasible solution has the same cost.

Human reaching provides a complementary observation. Participants stabilized a pointer's endpoint position and orientation while joint configurations varied across trials and target conditions.[^11-07] The experiment does not identify tissue forces. It shows that endpoint stability and internal configuration are separate variables.

External assistance makes the redistribution more visible. In a study of fifteen healthy younger adults, moving robotic handles during standing and sitting increased vertical handle force while reducing peak hip and knee extension moments compared with static handles.[^11-08] The person–device system preserved the chair-transition endpoint while selected demand shifted toward the upper-body contact and away from selected lower-limb moments. This is measured evidence of changed allocation. It does not establish long-term benefit, universal superiority, or what another population would experience.

The inference can also stop in the other direction. In one person with an instrumented knee implant performing four gait patterns, changes in external knee loading and electromyography were generally weak indicators of changes in measured total, medial, and lateral knee contact force.[^11-09] One participant cannot settle every gait model. The study demonstrates why an external moment, muscle-activity signal, or visible strategy cannot automatically stand in for an internal contact-force change.

Local anatomical relations beyond a direct muscle–tendon series can be investigated with the same restraint. In a lower-limb experiment, changing hip and knee positions while holding the ankle apparatus constant changed ankle resting position and passive torque; the authors interpreted the findings as consistent with myofascial force transmission.[^11-10] The result supports a bounded positional interaction and an indirect mechanical effect. It does not establish a clinically dominant force line from foot to skull.

Endpoint equivalence therefore creates a question, not a verdict. A changed allocation might reduce one local exposure, increase another, alter energetic cost, preserve reserve, accommodate anatomy, or make no consequential difference under the observed conditions. Each outcome requires its own measure and time window. Task success cannot prove unchanged routing, and routing change cannot prove hidden harm.

## A route account must be inspectable and vulnerable

A route narrative earns explanatory value by allowing another reader to reconstruct it and by risking a result that narrows or rejects it. Four records help keep its principal objects separate. For candidate relation (e) at phase or time (t):

```text
A_e(t): availability — can this relation perform the stated job now?
U_e(t): use — to what extent does it participate in the selected transfer?
E_e(t): exposure — what defined mechanical history reaches it or a named receiver?
C_e(t): capacity — what present, outcome-specific capacity meets that exposure?
```

These symbols are labels for records. They are not components of a validated score, not a vector whose entries can be added, and not a law for a force packet moving along an edge. Availability can be partial or uncertain. Use can be distributed. Exposure and capacity must refer to the same receiver, quantity, direction, outcome, and time window before they can be compared. The records prevent several shortcuts: available is not used; used is not dominant; dominant is not preferred; loaded is not overloaded; and deformed is not harmed.

Applied to the object-placement comparison, a candidate forearm-support edge might be recorded as available because the surface is reachable, used because contact force is measured during a declared interval, exposed through a time-varying contact-force or pressure history, and capable relative to a selected comfort, skin, task, or recovery endpoint. A candidate lower-limb relation might remain anatomically available while its modeled moment contribution falls. Neither record says that one route is better. The entries make clear which conclusions rest on observation, which on calculation, and which remain hypotheses about local exposure or capacity.

The records can also reveal that the proposed graph is the wrong representation. If participation is diffuse, highly state dependent, or inseparable from a field of contact and pressure, a single edge may conceal more than it clarifies. The account should then use a network, field, or lower-resolution relation. Formalism is serving the chapter only when it makes the hypothesis easier to challenge.

The account then needs enough context to make the records meaningful: the task and endpoint, phase, system boundary, incoming demand, candidate carriers and contacts, proposed route, observed allocation change, proposed substitute, local exposure, alternatives, and disfavoring observations. It must also preserve how each entry is known. Direct observation, instrument measurement, calculation, model output, inference, hypothesis, and embodied report are different kinds of support.

Several observations could count against a proposed routing account. The candidate carrier may not be anatomically or mechanically available in the stated phase. The external balance may not close under the proposed arrows. Changing the visible configuration may leave the relevant internal exposure materially unchanged. A proposed substitute may not change when the presumed interruption is removed. The receiver may deform and recover normally without the predicted loss of excursion or function. Another model may predict the measurements with fewer assumptions. Repeated measurement may fail to distinguish the proposed states. The hard/soft description itself may add no prediction beyond ordinary geometry, contact, pressure, activation, and capacity.

These are constructive outcomes. A failed local leakage account does not decide every larger question about preferred organization. It does require that this account be narrowed, rebuilt, or left unresolved. A route model is strongest when it can lose.

## From transfer to concentration

Return once more to the two object placements. Both succeed. The added forearm contact plainly changes the system boundary and creates another available path. It does not, by itself, tell us whether an internal route was interrupted, whether a bypass was chosen, whether a local exposure rose or fell, or whether either version was preferable. Those claims require the allocation record.

The chapter's positive conclusion is stronger than mere uncertainty. Living movement depends on workable relations through which demand can be received, transformed, transmitted, redirected, stored, returned, opposed, and resolved. Those relations can change carriers and roles without losing continuity. The hard/soft formulation asks whether gross structural demand is being resolved through capable shape-preserving relations while form-receiving structures retain the freedoms their functions require.

Its critical conclusion is equally specific. Continuity is not one hidden cable. Deformation is not leakage. Interruption, bypass, overload, substitution, and rerouting are not synonyms. Similar endpoints can conceal different allocations, and different allocations can be neutral or beneficial. A serious route account names the system, phase, quantity, carriers, receiver, exposure, capacity, alternatives, and a result that could count against it.

The next question is what happens after redistribution has been established or credibly proposed. A modest whole-task demand can become a larger local stress, strain, pressure, or contact exposure near a smaller area, changing geometry, or material transition. Chapter 12 takes up that problem of concentration. Only after concentration is distinguished can Chapter 13 ask what relation might maintain a deformation or loss of excursion through time.
