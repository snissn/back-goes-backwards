# Chapter 14 — Instability, discoordination, bracing, and repeated demand

Consider a hypothetical laboratory task, not a clinical case. A person stands on a movable support while investigators record motion, contact forces, and muscle activity. The task is to remain within a declared support condition when the surface moves sideways and, if that relation is lost, to recover it. On one trial, the person responds through the ankle and hip while the arms remain quiet. On another, the trunk turns, an arm rises, and the foot briefly changes contact. Both trials end with the person upright and ready for the next perturbation.

A final photograph would show two successes. It would not show how far the selected relation moved, which action arrived first, how much correction occurred, or whether the response changed with repetition. The more visibly eventful trial is not necessarily the worse one. An arm movement or a step can be the capable solution. The quieter trial may contain substantial co-contraction. A strategy that looks variable may adapt well when perturbation direction changes, while a visually consistent strategy may fail outside the one condition in which it was observed.

The preceding chapter examined a state that remains deformed or loses excursion because some relation continues to maintain it. The present problem is different. Nothing has to remain stuck. A living assembly can repeatedly leave and recover a task relation, or its available participants can arrive in a sequence that changes the response. The resulting correction is a real mechanical event through time, even when the materials are intact and the task is completed.

The question is therefore not whether the movement looks stable. It is whether a named relation was maintained or recovered, whether the relevant participants were coordinated in time, and what the system did when the demand changed.

## Stability belongs to a task and a relation

*Task-specific instability* is failure to maintain or recover a named mechanical relation within a declared tolerance. Each part of that definition matters. The task might concern foot contact, joint translation, segment rotation, the relation of center of mass to support, or another bounded performance variable. The tolerance can change with phase, speed, environment, assistance, and the consequences of crossing a boundary. Recovery requires a stated endpoint and interval. There is no context-free amount of movement that converts directly into instability.

In the opening task, investigators might choose the mediolateral relation between center of mass and the available base of support. They would declare what counts as remaining within the task condition, what perturbation occurred, and how return is assessed. A departure could be large and rapidly recovered. A smaller departure could matter near a contact boundary. Either observation is more informative than labeling the person's stance from appearance.

The acceptable relation is a set, not one ideal line through space. It can be asymmetric and phase-dependent. During quiet preparation, one range may be sufficient; during the platform motion, momentum and changing contact can make a different range relevant; after the event, readiness for the next trial may supply the recovery criterion. A handrail or device can enlarge the available support system. A task that values precise object control may require a tighter relation than one that permits a step. Declaring the set makes the objective visible without turning the investigator's chosen boundary into a universal norm.

Perturbation also need not mean an imposed laboratory jolt. A changing external contact, an added object, altered speed, fatigue, or a transition between phases can change the demand. What matters is that the event is stated clearly enough to compare response with requirement. If the demand is unknown, a departure cannot be distinguished from an appropriate response to conditions that were never recorded.

This use of instability is deliberately narrower than everyday and clinical uses of the word. Anatomical laxity, a report of giving way, pain, visible asymmetry, or an imaging finding may motivate different questions, but none fills in the task record by itself. A mobile joint can be controlled. A relatively still joint can be stabilized by active force. A disabled person using a device can form a capable person–device support system whose relevant relations differ from those of an unsupported task. The comparison has to match the actual system.

Instability also does not mean material failure. Bone, ligament, tendon, muscle, joint surfaces, and surrounding tissues can remain structurally available while control of a selected relation becomes inadequate under one demand. Conversely, a material change does not tell us whether the relation will fail in the task being observed. Material state and control state can interact, but they are not the same variable.

A useful record therefore begins with five entries:

1. the task and system boundary;
2. the relation to be maintained or recovered;
3. the perturbation or changing demand;
4. the allowed range and recovery criterion; and
5. the contacts, motions, forces, moments, and activations observed during the response.

Without those entries, *unstable* remains an impression. With them, instability becomes a claim that can succeed, fail, or remain unresolved.

## Variability is a capability before it is a failure

Living control does not usually solve a task with one invariant trajectory. It exploits options. Some variation changes the task result; other variation preserves the result while joint combinations, timing, contact, or effort differ. The second kind can be evidence of abundance rather than loss of control.

In a virtual throwing task, skill improvement could be decomposed into reduced execution noise, better use of the task's tolerant regions, and covariation among execution variables.[^14-01] In a reaching study involving shoulder, elbow, and wrist, participants could reach the same endpoint through different joint configurations. Reinforcement changed the joint-space solution while the endpoint goal remained the same.[^14-02] These experiments do not tell us whether every alternative has the same internal exposure. They establish the prior point: endpoint variation and joint variation are different objects, and variation within available solutions is not automatically error.

Balance studies make the distinction concrete. Twenty-four healthy young adults recovered from lateral platform perturbations during both single-leg and bipedal stance. The conditions showed similar damping effectiveness but different directional recovery actions.[^14-03] In perturbed overground walking, fifteen healthy adults combined foot placement, ankle inversion or eversion, push-off, and trunk adjustment differently depending on perturbation direction.[^14-04] The useful response was distributed across several regions and changed with the event.

Capable variability has three important features. It preserves the declared relation or recovers it within the task criterion. It changes when demand changes rather than repeating one rigid answer. And it does not produce an important rise in the particular exposure or cost being investigated. The third feature cannot be assumed from success alone, but neither can cost be assumed from visible complexity.

Variation can also reflect exploration, learning, anatomy, training, assistance, measurement noise, or preference. Early attempts at a skill often contain corrections that later become smaller or better directed. An athlete may deliberately accept more activation for precision. An older adult may use a step or hand support to preserve participation. A prosthesis, cane, rail, altered workspace, or another person can change the boundary conditions and make a different solution the capable one. A group average cannot decide whether those strategies work for the declared task.

Endpoint equivalence is therefore informative but incomplete. It tells us that several organizations can satisfy the selected task variable. It does not tell us that joint contact, muscle activation, effort, work, or tissue exposure is equal among them. Those quantities require their own measurements or models. This distinction keeps the argument balanced in both directions: success cannot prove equal cost, but a different route cannot be declared costly merely because its internal distribution is unknown.

Variability also has a scale. Trial-to-trial variation in foot placement can coexist with highly repeatable control of the center of mass. Variation in one joint can be coordinated with variation in another so that the endpoint changes little. A noisy instrument can create apparent variability without a changing strategy. The useful question is not whether variation exists, but where it appears, what task variable it preserves or changes, and whether it remains responsive when conditions change.

This gives the chapter an affirmative null. If the relation remains controlled across relevant perturbations, the strategy adapts, and the selected exposure does not meaningfully rise, visible variation is not demonstrated instability. It is a successful control result.

## Discoordination is a timing relation

An assembly can have available routes and intact materials yet still respond differently because its participants arrive in a different sequence. *Discoordination* names a task-specific mismatch in timing, sequence, or phase among participants that are otherwise available. It is not a synonym for instability, weakness, clumsiness, or unfamiliar style.

The analysis needs an event marker: perturbation onset, foot contact, release of an object, a change of support, or another observable boundary. It then compares the onset, peak, offset, or transition of the relevant participants. Those participants might be muscle activity, joint motion, contact, pressure compartments, or segment motion. Their quantities must remain separate. Surface electromyography records an electrical signal under a stated method; it is not direct measurement of muscle force. A calculated net joint moment is not the contribution of one muscle. A visible segment motion does not reveal the control instruction that produced it.

In twelve healthy young adults exposed to unexpected waist-pull perturbations, lower-limb muscle activity, joint moments, and motions showed direction- and magnitude-dependent timing. Larger perturbations produced faster muscle responses but did not change every kinetic and kinematic measure in one uniform direction.[^14-05] The result argues against one generic recovery sequence. It also shows what a coordination claim requires: time-resolved observations tied to a defined event.

This timing problem is adjacent to, but narrower than, the handoff problem introduced earlier. A route can be available while its contributors enter at a phase that changes contact or demands correction. A route can also be unavailable, forcing a perfectly well-timed substitute. Calling both situations a broken chain would lose the difference. The first is a candidate coordination relation; the second is a route-state problem. In a real task they can coexist, but one cannot be inferred from the other.

Instability and discoordination likewise form two questions rather than two mutually exclusive boxes. A reliable timing mismatch may lead to departure of the selected relation. Repeated relation loss may provoke timing changes as the system catches itself. Or the relation may remain controlled because a correction successfully compensates for altered timing. Time order and task variation help distinguish those accounts. A label attached after the endpoint does not.

A timing difference becomes consequential only when it predicts something else in the account. It might predict departure of the selected relation, a repeated correction, a contact change, a local exposure, or a performance outcome. If timing varies but the task relation remains controlled and no relevant exposure changes, the observed sequence may simply be another capable solution. If the relation repeatedly leaves its accepted set but the proposed timing difference does not track that event, discoordination is not established as the explanation.

Two response terms complete the distinction. A *correction* is an observed action that attempts to maintain or restore the declared relation after an actual or anticipated disturbance. *Bracing* is an active strategy that changes task-specific stiffness or available motion, often through co-contraction but not defined by one muscle pattern. Correction and bracing are responses, not failure classes. Either can occur without instability, and either can be useful.

## Bracing is a solution before it is a cost

Co-contraction can increase mechanical stiffness. In seven healthy participants, strong co-contraction of antagonist muscles at the ankle produced greater measured joint stiffness than plantar-flexion activation at similar strength. Reflex behavior and the weak co-contraction condition varied among participants.[^14-06] The experiment supports a task- and condition-specific stiffness effect, not the rule that every increase in activity produces the same response.

The same strategy can change more than one quantity. In lifting tasks performed by ten healthy men, measured motion, reaction loads, and trunk electromyography were combined with a spine model. Greater antagonistic co-contraction was associated in the model with both greater spinal compression and a larger stability margin.[^14-07] The result is not a simple verdict. The modeled benefit and cost occurred together, and both depended on the task and model.

Bracing should therefore enter the account through what it preserves. Anticipatory stabilization can prepare for a known perturbation. Reactive protection can reduce motion after surprise or pain. A learned strategy can improve precision. A person may choose sustained stiffness because the alternative is loss of balance, loss of contact, or inability to continue a valued task. In each case, more activation can be the efficient response to the actual objective even if it would be unnecessary under different boundary conditions.

Stiffness is not a single body-wide property. It relates an applied change to a response around a stated state, direction, timescale, and set of boundary conditions. An ankle can resist one perturbation direction more than another. A trunk model can predict a greater margin under one loading task without establishing the same benefit during walking or reaching. Even within one task, anticipatory and reactive activity can have different timing and mechanical jobs. The word *bracing* becomes useful only when those conditions are retained.

Possible cost comes second and needs a denominator. Does the strategy increase a modeled contact force, local compression, activation time, effort, fatigue, or recovery time? Does it reduce adaptability when direction changes? Does it preserve one relation while repeatedly altering another? Does it persist after the demand has changed? No single answer follows from observing muscle activity. More activation does not establish fear, inefficiency, sensory deficit, or pathology. Less activation does not establish improvement.

This order matters for guarding as well. Pain, uncertainty, expectation, or threat can provoke protective behavior while mechanical relations remain controlled. That behavior can be short-lived and appropriate. If it persists, restricts options, or changes a measured exposure, those are additional observations. They cannot be read backward from the word *guarding*.

Bracing can also occur before any demonstrated relation loss. A participant who expects the support to move may co-contract in advance. If the perturbation is then handled successfully, the activity is evidence of a strategy, not evidence that hidden instability preceded it. Only a comparison—across perturbations, instructions, support conditions, or time—can show what the strategy contributed.

## Repeated correction creates a history, not a verdict

One correction is an event. Repeated corrections form a history. That history matters because a final pose and a single peak value can omit the time spent catching, decelerating, reestablishing contact, or recruiting additional structures. The history can be counted and measured without being called damage.

Repeated-perturbation experiments show both accumulation and change. In twenty-six healthy young adults exposed to ten stance-slip perturbations, some muscle-synergy activation features changed from early to later trials, while other synergy structures changed little.[^14-08] In a separate study, twenty-three people with chronic ankle instability and twenty-three controls received repeated mediolateral perturbations during single-leg stance. Practice improved the margin of stability and its coordination with hip torque in the instability group, while co-contraction changes differed by muscle pair and group.[^14-09] The clinical group label does not generalize to every person who makes corrections. The study's useful result here is that repeated response can reorganize, and different measured features need not change together.

For a selected corrective event, a time-series record can include perturbation onset and direction, departure of the declared relation, recovery time, contact changes, force or moment, motion, activation, and state before the next event. Across repetitions, the investigator might summarize count, cumulative activation, impulse, work, excursion, or time outside the accepted relation. Those quantities are not interchangeable. Each answers a different question and needs its own method and uncertainty.

The state before the next event prevents repetition from being treated as a row of identical copies. The preceding response may change expectation, activation, contact, fatigue, confidence, or the strategy selected on the next trial. A smaller later correction might reflect learning, a stiffer anticipatory state, a different foot placement, or a weaker perturbation. A larger response might reflect fatigue, exploration, changed timing, or measurement variation. Repetition becomes a history when these state transitions are retained, not when events are simply counted.

Summation also needs a reason. Counts can describe frequency but ignore magnitude. Peak force can omit duration. Impulse integrates force through time but does not become tissue work. Electromyographic amplitude and cumulative activation do not directly equal muscle force or metabolic cost. Excursion is kinematic rather than material strain. Choosing a descriptor is therefore part of the hypothesis: it states what aspect of correction is proposed to matter and makes an inappropriate or null result possible.

There is therefore no generic correction dose. A small event becomes mechanically relevant through repetition only when a named cumulative quantity changes. Even then, the result is an exposure history, not a tissue outcome. To connect repeated correction to fatigue, pain, injury, or degeneration would require a receiver, a local exposure, a capacity comparison, a biological response, a chronology, and alternatives. Those bridges belong later in the book.

The affirmative branches remain equally important. Practice may reduce correction, redistribute it, or make it more precisely matched to the perturbation. A learner's early corrections can be the mechanism of acquiring skill. An assistive strategy can make repetition possible. A trained person can accept greater activation for a task that values accuracy or safety. A repeated-correction record measures control work; it does not score the person or movement morally.

## The sensory–bracing loop has more than one direction

The project proposes a feedback possibility. Changed position, motion, tissue state, or pain may alter selected sensory information. Uncertainty may change activation or provoke bracing. Bracing may alter stiffness, contact, available motion, and the sensory information produced by the next action. Under some conditions, that sequence could reinforce protection. It could also resolve, remain useful, fail to appear, or begin somewhere else.

Experimental pain studies demonstrate why every arrow must remain open. In four thumb-pain conditions completed by ten to twelve participants per condition, movement-direction detection worsened at some anatomically relevant muscle or skin sites but not at nearby control sites; selected other perceptual judgments were unchanged. Twelve healthy adults participated overall, and nine completed all four conditions.[^14-10] An ankle study found a change in movement-detection threshold only when relatively high pain was induced in two antagonist muscles, while position recognition remained unchanged.[^14-11] In twenty-eight healthy participants, nerve growth factor injections produced sustained forearm pain and hyperalgesia without changing the measured wrist joint-position error over the study period.[^14-12]

These findings support a conditional proposition: pain can change some sensory measures under some site, intensity, and task conditions. They do not establish a generic proprioceptive deficit, and they do not show that a sensory change caused instability or bracing. The null results are not empty. They identify measures and conditions under which the proposed link did not appear.

Proprioception is not one scalar signal. Movement-direction detection, threshold to detect movement, and joint-position matching ask different questions under different protocols. A change in one does not license a claim about all sensing of position and action. The same caution applies to a null: unchanged position error does not prove that every sensory process, motor decision, or perceived state is unchanged. The proposed loop must name the sensory object it actually needs rather than borrowing the broad word *proprioception* after the fact.

The reverse direction is just as important. Pain can come first and alter both sensation and behavior. Instruction or expectation can provoke bracing before any sensory difference is measured. Fatigue can change timing and increase corrective activity. Inflammation or another tissue state can influence pain, movement, and sensation together. Habit, training, environment, or a support device can change motion and contact without any deficit. A later sensory finding cannot by itself tell us which sequence occurred.

Nor does altered sensory information guarantee a mechanically important consequence. Activation may change while the task remains well controlled. Stiffness may increase and improve the selected relation. Available motion may narrow without impairing the task. Protection may resolve when uncertainty falls, or another strategy may replace it. A loop becomes explanatory only when its time order is observed, component changes covary as predicted, changing one term changes the next, and competing sequences do less explanatory work.

This is a demanding standard because feedback stories are easy to complete after the fact. If sensory state, activation, mechanics, and outcome were not measured through time, the loop remains a hypothesis rather than the explanation for a person's behavior. If bracing precedes the sensory finding, the proposed order needs revision. If the sensory measure changes but activation does not, one arrow fails. If pain or instruction explains both, a common initiator may be sufficient. The model gains precision by allowing those outcomes.

## Dynamic comparison distinguishes similar appearances

The same outward pattern can represent capable variation, intentional stabilization, pain-related guarding, fatigue drift, inadequate relation control, or a timing mismatch. Static posture cannot distinguish them. One successful trial cannot distinguish them. Even a time-resolved signal remains incomplete if the task relation and comparison are missing.

The measurement set should match the claim. An instability question needs the declared relation, perturbation, departure, recovery, and repeatability. A discoordination question needs an event marker, reliable participant timing, and a linked mechanical or performance result. A bracing question needs activation together with a mechanical response, task outcome, and the proposed cost or benefit. A sensory-loop question needs time-resolved sensory, activation, and mechanical measures, plus an intervention or comparison capable of separating directions.

Measurements must retain their identities. Kinematics describe motion. Contacts and force plates measure external interactions. Electromyography samples electrical activity under method-dependent conditions. Joint moments are calculations. Stiffness and local exposure may be model-dependent. A report of uncertainty, pain, or effort is another observation, not a substitute for these mechanical quantities. Combining them can produce a richer account only if the transitions remain visible.

Varying the demand can be more informative than repeating one condition. Change perturbation direction or magnitude and ask whether the strategy adapts. Compare early and later repetitions. Alter support or assistance when that is the system of interest. Separate rested and fatigued states if fatigue is part of the claim. Treat a stable null, an improved response, and an alternate successful strategy as informative results rather than failed attempts to reveal dysfunction.

Return now to the opening trials. If the selected center-of-mass/support relation remains within its accepted set while ankle, hip, arm, and step strategies vary with perturbation direction, the record supports capable variability. If the relation repeatedly leaves the set or recovery exceeds the criterion under matched demand, it supports a task-specific instability candidate. If an event-locked timing gap reliably precedes extra correction while the endpoint remains successful, it supports a discoordination candidate. If anticipatory co-contraction preserves the relation, it supports useful bracing. If relation, timing, or force was not measured reliably, the honest result is unresolved.

These readings do not compete for one mandatory winner. A trial can contain more than one state, and the important relation can change by phase. Their value is operational: each reading points to a different missing observation and can be disfavored by a different result. The framework replaces a global judgment about movement quality with questions that a dynamic record can answer.

The candidate interpretations should also have explicit ways to lose support. Instability is disfavored when the relation remains controlled across the relevant perturbations. Discoordination is disfavored when the timing difference is unreliable or does not predict the selected relation, exposure, or outcome. A costly-bracing interpretation is disfavored when the strategy resolves, remains well tolerated, or improves the declared task without the proposed cost. The sensory loop is narrowed when its arrows lack time order, fail to covary, or are better explained by another initiator.

## Control has mechanics and history

A living system's ability to vary, brace, catch, and recover is part of its capacity. Those actions become evidence of a failure family only when the relevant relation, timing, and consequence are named. Instability concerns maintenance or recovery of a task relation. Discoordination concerns timing or sequence among available participants. Correction and bracing are responses that can address either problem, anticipated demand, ordinary error, pain, or uncertainty. None is a diagnosis.

This framework gives repeated control activity a legitimate place in mechanical reasoning. A task can end successfully while its path contains departure, correction, changed contact, or greater activation. Those events can be measured across time. Their cumulative importance depends on which quantity changes and what receives it. Repetition can support learning, stable adaptation, or harmless variation just as readily as it can produce a candidate exposure.

The strongest conclusion is modest but consequential: control has a mechanical history. Endpoint success does not erase that history, and visible complexity does not condemn it. Naming the relation, timing, response, and repeated quantity allows useful protection and capable variability to remain visible while genuine loss of control becomes testable.

The sensory–bracing proposal remains worth testing because it turns a compelling intuition into separable arrows. It also remains conditional. Positive, null, reverse-order, and common-cause results determine whether the proposed loop survives in a particular system.

The next chapter begins with the successful solution. A brace, altered timing, alternate contact, or rerouted action may preserve something valuable now. If that solution persists, it becomes part of the starting state for the next task. Chapter 15 follows that compensation through time and asks whether it remains useful, is replaced, or changes later demand.
