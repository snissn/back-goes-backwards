# Chapter 7 — The posterior chain as an anatomical system

Look at the back of the body and draw what belongs to the *posterior chain*. One defensible drawing would show a familiar muscular line: calf, hamstrings, gluteal muscles, spinal extensors, latissimus dorsi, trapezius, and the posterior neck. A second would add the Achilles tendon, plantar and pelvic interfaces, aponeuroses, thoracolumbar fascia, ligaments, and retinacula. A third would show bones, joints, ground contact, abdominal and lateral partners, pressure-bearing regions, sensory control, and the arms that connect a carried object to the trunk.

The three drawings would not be competing answers to one labeling quiz. They would answer different questions. The first identifies selected posterior muscles and their direct tendons. The second asks how those structures attach, blend, separate, slide, and meet across regions. The third closes a mechanical system around a task. Confusion begins when a statement established on one drawing is silently promoted to another—when an attachment becomes a task result, or a smooth line on an atlas becomes a measured force route.

The posterior body nevertheless warrants treatment as a system. It contains extensive muscle groups, long tendons, long and short axial structures, broad aponeuroses, layered fasciae, major skeletal junctions, and several documented regional continuities. Some posterior muscles occupy large volumes; some have large physiological cross-sectional areas or broad attachment spans; the Achilles tendon is conventionally described as the body's largest and strongest tendon. Those are different measures, and none proves that posterior structures dominate every task. Together, however, they establish a physical system too consequential to be dismissed as a fitness slogan.

This chapter maps that system from ground contact toward the cranium. It uses *posterior chain* as a family of three nested scopes rather than as one official anatomical object. At each transition, it asks what kind of relation is actually present: a direct attachment, a connective interface, a shared skeletal junction, coordinated participation, a measured regional effect, or a proposed long-range route. The result is not one cable. It is a layered and branching anatomical architecture in which carriers can change at the foot, pelvis, thoracolumbar region, shoulder girdle, and neck.

## Three scopes for one useful term

The **narrow posterior chain** contains selected muscles situated primarily on the posterior aspect of a region, together with their direct tendons or aponeuroses. Its recognizable members include the gastrocnemius–soleus–Achilles complex; the hamstrings and their tendons; gluteus maximus; the erector-spinae and multifidus groups; latissimus dorsi; trapezius; and selected posterior scapular and neck muscles. The narrow scope is a muscular and myotendinous map. It does not claim that every member attaches serially to the next or acts at the same time.

The **broad posterior chain** adds a named anatomical reason for crossing from one structure to another. That reason may be a direct tissue continuity, an aponeurotic blend, a fascial layer, a ligament, a retinaculum, a periosteal relation, a joint capsule, or a sliding interface. The plantar relation at the calcaneus, proximal hamstring and sacrotuberous-ligament relations, erector-spinae aponeurosis, thoracolumbar fascial layers, vertebral aponeurosis, and nuchal-ligament relations belong here when the particular connection is stated. The broad scope is not simply the narrow map with every nearby connective tissue colored in. Inclusion requires a specified relation.

The **whole-posterior support system** begins with that broad map and adds everything required to close a selected mechanical question. It may include the skeleton and articular contacts, the ground, abdominal and lateral muscles, pressure-bearing boundaries, sensory information, active control, an assistive device, or an object being handled. These additions are not reclassified as posterior. They are included because a posterior subset cannot support, move, or stabilize a body by itself. The whole-support scope is therefore task-dependent even though its underlying anatomy can be mapped before a task is analyzed.

Current international anatomical terminology supplies standard names and orientation for the components.[^07-01] *Posterior chain* functions here as an analytic grouping, not as a replacement nomenclature entry. Its value depends on whether the inclusion rules remain stable and whether the grouping helps later mechanics more than an unstructured list of regions would.

The scopes are nested for reasoning, not because nature contains three sharply bounded chains. A structure can wrap from posterior to lateral, change orientation along its course, or participate through different relations at different scales. *Posterior* here is a regional anatomical descriptor. It is not a synonym for exterior, shape-preserving, healthy, or mechanically preferred. The hard/soft vocabulary introduced earlier is motivated by traditional Chinese medicine but assigns relative mechanical roles in a declared relation. A posterior tendon may receive form while carrying tension; an anterior or lateral structure may provide shape-preserving support. Posterior, hard, and Yang therefore cannot be substituted for one another.

The physical scale of the posterior system can also be stated without turning size into rank. A whole-body magnetic-resonance dataset segmented 70 muscles and 13 bones in 102 active healthy adults aged nineteen to fifty. By muscle volume, posterior groups including the gluteals, erector spinae, soleus, latissimus dorsi, and trapezius occupied substantial measurable volume.[^07-02] Volume estimates the amount of contractile material; it is not physiological cross-sectional area, moment arm, activation, force, work, endurance, or task priority.

Architecture changes the comparison. A dissection study of 27 lower-limb muscles from 21 limbs found large physiological cross-sectional areas for soleus and gluteus medius, a proxy relevant to force-generating capacity under architectural assumptions. The nonposterior vastus lateralis also ranked highly by that measure.[^07-03] Fiber length would answer another question; tendon dimensions and attachment span would answer still others. The strongest permitted conclusion is also the useful one: the posterior system includes several of the body's largest or most architecturally substantial muscle–tendon units under named measures, alongside substantial nonposterior structures. That is enough to justify a focused map. It is not enough to assign functional primacy.

Calling the posterior anatomy a *system* depends on more than adding the sizes of its parts. The components recur across major junctions: muscle changes into tendon; tendon meets bone; bone and joint geometry redirect relations; ligament and fascia constrain, blend, or separate; another muscle begins from the resulting skeletal or connective region. Long structures span several segments while shorter structures organize local relations. Broad sheets meet narrow tendons. Superficial layers overlap deep ones. The repeated architecture is what makes a system-level map informative even when no single material runs through it from end to end.

The map also creates testable alternatives. If two regions share only a bone, a soft-tissue-cable model should not be assumed. If they share a dissectible interface, a perturbation can ask whether that interface carries a measurable effect. If several regions move together without a reproducible anatomical transition, linked-segment dynamics or neural coordination may provide the better account. The nested scopes do not guarantee that posterior-chain language will outperform regional anatomy. They make that comparison possible without losing the larger hypothesis in advance.

## Ground, foot, calf, and the first change of carrier

The atlas begins at the ground, but the ground is not an anatomical continuation of the body. It enters only in the whole-support scope, through contact with the foot. That distinction already prevents a misleading picture: a route can begin at an external boundary without becoming a continuous tissue line from the floor upward.

In the narrow posterior chain, the distal system has an unusually clear myotendinous relation. Gastrocnemius and soleus contribute to the Achilles tendon; plantaris may also contribute. The tendon inserts into the posterior calcaneus. An anatomical review conventionally describes the Achilles as the body's largest and strongest tendon and emphasizes its conjoined gastrocnemius–soleus origin.[^07-04] The convention identifies exceptional gross size and a comparative strength description. It does not supply an ordinary in-vivo force, strain, safety factor, energy return, or continuation beyond the insertion.

At the calcaneus the simple line changes character. The Achilles tendon approaches from the calf; the plantar aponeurosis extends toward the forefoot; both relate to the calcaneal region. It is tempting to erase the bone and draw one tendon-like cable around the heel. A study of 19 cadaveric feet instead found a more instructive arrangement. Imaging, sectioning, plastination, morphometry, and selected histology supported morphometric correlations and a superficial relation among Achilles paratenon, calcaneal periosteum, and plantar fascia, but did not confirm complete fiber continuity.[^07-05]

The calcaneus is therefore not an embarrassing gap in an otherwise perfect chain. It is a **shared skeletal junction** accompanied by connective and periosteal relations. Bone geometry, attachment, local interfaces, trabecular structure, and contact can change how demand is expressed across the region. The carrier need not remain one population of collagen fibers for the region to be mechanically connected. Equally, a connection through shared bone and interface cannot be renamed direct soft-tissue continuity.

The wider map makes the distal system still more three-dimensional. The ankle and subtalar articulations, other foot bones, arches, intrinsic muscles, deeper posterior-compartment structures, anterior and lateral muscles, retinacula, sensory information, and the geometry of ground contact all participate in the whole-support boundary. The narrow calf–Achilles relation remains real inside that assembly. The broad scope adds the plantar, periosteal, and retinacular relations. The whole-support scope adds the foot and environment needed to ask what happens during a task.

This first junction establishes the method for the rest of the chapter. A regional pathway can be anatomically serious while changing carrier. The relevant statement may move from direct myotendinous continuity to tendon–bone attachment, then to a shared skeletal junction, connective interface, and external contact. Each relation is positive. None needs to impersonate the others.

## The pelvis is a junction, not a passive bridge

From the distal limb, the narrow posterior chain reaches the thigh through the hamstrings and the hip through the gluteal system. Even this familiar map is not one uniform cord. The hamstrings differ in architecture, proximal attachment, distal insertion, and whether they cross one or two major joints. Gluteus maximus spans broad pelvic and femoral relations. Other gluteal and deep hip structures enter or leave the map according to the declared region and question.

The proximal hamstring region shows why the broad scope must preserve layers. In an anatomical, histological, and ultrasound study covering 97 hemipelves from 66 cryopreserved specimens, investigators described retinacular tissue around the proximal hamstring tendons, a layered sacrotuberous ligament, continuity between its superficial layer and the long-head biceps-femoris tendon, and relations involving semimembranosus and adductor magnus.[^07-06] This is more than adjacency. It documents direct continuity at one relation and connective or retinacular relations at others. It does not establish that every pelvis has the same arrangement or that tension crosses every layer in the same magnitude and direction.

The skeletal map prevents those soft-tissue relations from bearing the whole explanation. Hamstring tendons attach around the ischial region; gluteal structures relate to the pelvis, femur, iliotibial tract, and neighboring fasciae; the hip joints connect the femora to the pelvis; the sacrum and sacroiliac regions relate the pelvic ring to the axial skeleton. Ligaments, articular contacts, and bone geometry can constrain or redirect regional interactions. A shared bone or joint can couple structures without providing a direct fascial continuation between them.

The whole-posterior support system widens the junction further. Adductors, deep and lateral hip muscles, abdominal structures, pelvic pressure-bearing relations, neural interfaces, contralateral limb support, and external contacts may be required to close a mechanical question. Their inclusion does not dilute the posterior thesis. It locates the posterior structures inside the system in which their anatomy can matter.

Size again supports attention without deciding the result. Gluteus maximus is substantial by volume in whole-body imaging, and the posterior thigh contains long, region-spanning muscle–tendon units. Yet physiological cross-sectional area, fiber length, moment arm, activation, and available joint geometry can order structures differently. No anatomical measure produces one net action across all hip positions. The pelvis is important precisely because several routes converge, branch, slide, and change direction there.

This junction also makes the difference between **direct continuity** and **coordinated participation** visible. A superficial sacrotuberous-ligament relation with the long-head biceps-femoris tendon is an anatomical observation. Motion or activation in the contralateral trunk during a task would be a coordination observation. A measured mechanical response across a named pelvic connection would be a transfer observation. A proposed foot-to-trunk route would be a model. Those four objects can coexist, but they do not confer evidence on one another.

## The axial system has long spans, short spans, and sliding layers

Above the pelvis, the phrase *spinal extensors* can obscure the anatomy it is meant to summarize. Erector-spinae subdivisions have long regional spans and broad aponeurotic attachments. Multifidus and other transversospinal muscles use shorter, overlapping fascicles. Segmental muscles, posterior vertebral elements, supraspinous and interspinous relations, posterior ligaments, ribs, facets, discs, and regional fasciae create different pathways and constraints at different levels.

The narrow posterior chain includes selected erector-spinae, multifidus, transversospinal, and posterior cervical muscle groups. The broad posterior chain adds their aponeuroses, ligamentous relations, regional fasciae, compartments, and sliding interfaces. The whole-posterior support system includes vertebrae, discs, facets, ribs, the abdominal wall, pressure-bearing relations, psoas major, quadratus lumborum, sensory control, and external support whenever the question requires them.

Cadaveric morphology demonstrates how much is lost by drawing one back muscle. A chemical-dissection study of eight cadavers described four lumbar-multifidus layers, multipennate architecture, and attachments among fascicles rather than a set of completely separable parallel cords.[^07-07] A study of ten fresh cadavers described erector-spinae aponeurosis and thoracolumbar fascia around subcompartments and identified sliding areas between fascia and paraspinal muscles, between erector spinae and multifidus, and between longissimus and iliocostalis.[^07-08] The axial system is connected partly because layers attach and partly because they can move relative to one another.

That distinction matters mechanically. Connection is not the only form of organization. A compartment boundary can separate structures while transmitting traction. An aponeurosis can distribute attachment over a broad region. A sliding plane can preserve relative excursion while neighboring tissues carry demand. A ligament can become recruited in one configuration and less recruited in another. Bone and articular contact can redirect resultants between levels. The broad posterior chain is therefore a layered architecture, not a single material extended upward.

The word *extensor* also needs its ordinary limit. It names a conventional action or moment tendency under stated geometry. It does not mean that every listed muscle is shortening, active, or producing extension in every upright state. A muscle can act while shortening, holding, or lengthening; a net external moment does not identify one muscle's force. Those functional questions belong to the task analysis of the next chapter. Here the anatomical conclusion is that the posterior axial system contains structures operating across different spans, depths, directions, and scales.

The axial region also makes reference frames unavoidable. *Posterior* describes location relative to standard anatomical orientation; a fascicle's local line, an aponeurotic fiber direction, and a joint's instantaneous mechanical relation need not remain parallel to that global direction. Fibers can turn, fan, or cross levels. Ribs and vertebral processes provide oblique attachments. A ground-to-cranium map can therefore preserve a longitudinal sweep without treating every local element as a cranio-caudal cable. Direction belongs to the particular structure and relation being described.

## The thoracolumbar and scapular region is a crossroads

The thoracolumbar region provides the strongest anatomical reason to think in regional systems. Latissimus dorsi spans the trunk and upper limb through broad attachments. Erector-spinae aponeurosis and thoracolumbar fascia organize paraspinal regions. Gluteal, sacral, rib, vertebral, abdominal, and shoulder-girdle relations meet in overlapping layers. The posterior view is compelling, but the regional architecture is not posterior-only.

An integrative anatomical review describes the thoracolumbar fascia as a girdling arrangement rather than a single sheet laid over the back. Latissimus-dorsi and serratus-posterior-inferior aponeuroses contribute to a superficial posterior layer; a deeper paraspinal retinacular sheath surrounds axial structures; abdominal aponeuroses meet the region laterally.[^07-09] The narrow posterior chain identifies latissimus and posterior axial muscles. The broad posterior chain includes the named fascial and aponeurotic layers. The whole-support scope requires abdominal, lateral, skeletal, pressure, and control relations to make the trunk mechanically complete.

This region also offers evidence above simple anatomical continuity. In eight unembalmed cadavers, investigators applied 10 newtons of traction to named muscular attachments and measured tension and marker displacement in posterior and middle lumbar fasciae. Under that setup, latissimus dorsi and transversus abdominis affected the largest regions of the posterior layer, while gluteus maximus and oblique attachments produced more local patterns.[^07-10] The experiment demonstrated **measured regional transfer** in dissected tissues under imposed traction. It did not determine ordinary living magnitude, active sharing, a unique carrier, whole-body propagation, or a clinical consequence.

Living experiments add another type of observation. In 37 participants, passive or active tensioning of latissimus dorsi changed a contralateral hip resting-position endpoint; active tensioning also changed passive hip stiffness.[^07-11] The study measured joint endpoints, not force inside a fascial layer. The result is compatible with regional connective transfer, but it can also include joint mechanics, active control, skeletal coupling, or other carriers. In a later 48-participant comparison involving people with chronic low-back pain and controls, the distal hip-stiffness difference was small and below the reported standard error of measurement, underscoring how population, endpoint, distance, and measurement error affect the inference.[^07-12] These are bounded regional findings, not evidence for a disease mechanism.

The scapular region extends the crossroads into the upper limb. Trapezius is broad and region-spanning, but the scapula does not rest on the thorax as a free posterior plate. Serratus anterior is an indispensable synergistic partner; clavicular and shoulder relations, ribs, humerus, thoracic geometry, contact, and several muscles participate in scapular position and motion.[^07-13] Rhomboids and other posterior muscles belong in a posterior atlas. They do not close the scapular system by themselves.

This is not a concession that weakens the posterior account. It is the account. A major posterior system can be defined by the scale and arrangement of its members while remaining integrated with nonposterior partners. Latissimus, trapezius, thoracolumbar fascia, abdominal aponeuroses, scapula, ribs, humerus, clavicle, and serratus anterior form a junction where several carriers and directions can be studied. The existence of the junction is anatomically firm. The magnitude and distribution of its contribution remain task-specific.

## The posterior system reaches the cranium, but not identically in everyone

A map that stops at the low back would preserve only part of the original anatomical question. Posterior muscular, fascial, aponeurotic, ligamentous, and skeletal relations continue through the thoracic and cervical regions toward the cranium. Selected semispinalis, splenius, suboccipital, and other posterior cervical muscles enter the narrow chain according to the detail required. Nuchal ligament, deep cervical fascia, vertebral aponeurosis, and entheses enter the broad chain. Craniovertebral joints, skull, sternocleidomastoid and other anterior or lateral neck structures, sensory systems, and head–environment relations complete the whole-support boundary.

A study of 40 cadavers described a vertebral aponeurosis deep to latissimus dorsi with attachments at thoracic spinous processes and rib angles, inferior relations with thoracolumbar structures, and superior blending with deep cervical fascia.[^07-14] This supplies a documented regional connective continuation. It does not demonstrate one physiological force from lumbar fascia to cranium.

The upper endpoint also varies. In a dissection study of 20 cadavers from New Zealand and Thailand, direct cranial attachment of upper trapezius was present in six. Most specimens in that sample attached proximally only to the nuchal ligament.[^07-15] A canonical illustration in which trapezius reaches the skull directly would therefore misrepresent the variation. The posterior neck–cranial system remains real: it includes muscle, ligament, fascia, entheses, bone, joint, and control relations. What changes is the exact route and tissue relation.

Variation does not make the atlas unusable. It tells us what kind of atlas is needed. A credible ground-to-cranium map must allow a relation to branch, end, blend, or change carrier, and it must represent variant attachment patterns rather than choosing one body as the universal body. The full posterior ambition survives in a stronger form: every proposed transition is visible enough to be studied.

## Connection, coordination, and transfer occupy different levels

The regional atlas establishes many real connections. It does not make every use of *connection* mechanically equivalent. Seven relation types keep the argument inspectable.

A **named component and attachment** identifies a structure and where it attaches. A **direct tissue continuity** shows continuous fibers or a direct attachment by dissection, histology, or imaging. A **connective continuity or shared interface** identifies a blend, aponeurosis, retinaculum, periosteal relation, fascial layer, or sliding plane. A **shared skeletal junction** identifies structures acting through the same bone or joint complex without asserting direct soft-tissue continuity. These four relations are principally anatomical.

**Coordinated task participation** occurs when several regions change activation, motion, stiffness, timing, or control during one declared task. It need not result from a direct tensile connection. **Measured regional transfer** requires a perturbation at one named structure and a quantified mechanical effect at another under controlled conditions. It is stronger than a drawing, yet remains bounded by its carrier, preparation, endpoint, and scale. **Inferred long-range transfer** is a model linking several regions through declared quantities, carriers, boundary conditions, and measurements. It becomes evidence only as its distinct predictions are tested.

The research literature supports different levels. A 2016 systematic review examined 62 human dissection studies and reported strong morphological support for the transitions it assessed in several proposed myofascial chains, while leaving functional relevance unresolved.[^07-16] A companion review of nine human dissection and in-vivo studies found evidence for tension transfer across some adjacent connections, with substantial heterogeneity, anatomical variation, and limited support for functional significance.[^07-16] Morphology and bounded transfer are not empty findings. They simply answer narrower questions than a whole-system route.

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis examined 15 studies of remote stiffness changes in healthy adults during local lengthening or muscle contraction. The authors rated certainty very low for lengthening and moderate for contraction; protocols varied, reporting bias was suspected, and only eight studies monitored unwanted muscle activity with electromyography.[^07-17] Remote stiffness is a measured endpoint. It does not, by itself, identify whether connective tissue, active control, skeletal coupling, pressure, or another route produced the change. Nor does it state whether the magnitude matters during an ordinary task.

The resulting ladder is cumulative only when evidence crosses each step:

1. named structures and attachments exist;
2. adjacent structures share a direct attachment, interface, aponeurosis, joint, fascial relation, or skeletal junction;
3. several regions participate during a declared task or perturbation;
4. a mechanical effect is measured across a named regional relation; and
5. consequential foot-to-cranium transfer is demonstrated for a named quantity, carrier, direction, task, and state.

Evidence can stop at any rung. A dissection can demonstrate continuity without measuring living force. Concurrent motion can demonstrate coordination without identifying a fascial carrier. Cadaver traction can demonstrate regional transmission without estimating its ordinary magnitude. A remote stiffness change can be real while its pathway remains uncertain.

Embodied continuity belongs beside this ladder rather than being erased from it. A felt internal force line can motivate where to inspect anatomy or what multiregional variables to measure. It is an observation with its own provenance, not a direct measurement of force through a cable. Likewise, *force line* can mean an external line of action, an inferred internal multicarrier relation, or an embodied continuity. The meaning has to be stated before the term does technical work.

The ladder preserves a strong research program. A whole-posterior route becomes more credible when predicted transitions are anatomically reproducible, measured effects remain after active and external alternatives are controlled, and magnitudes matter in the selected task. It narrows when a proposed transition is absent, when the effect becomes negligible under better controls, or when a simpler linked-segment, regional-fascia, pressure, or control model predicts the observation equally well. The underlying anatomical system can remain useful even if a particular transfer hypothesis fails.

This last possibility is a genuine null rather than a rhetorical escape. Posterior-chain language may prove most useful as an anatomical index: it may help organize recurring structures and junctions while adding little prediction beyond regional models. Alternatively, a multiregional state may predict effects that isolated regional descriptions miss. The distinction cannot be settled by drawing a longer line. It requires the proposed route to name its quantity, carrier, boundary, task, and expected magnitude before the result is known, then to compare that result with active-control, skeletal, pressure, contact, and linked-segment accounts.

That standard preserves both outcomes. A negative whole-route result would not make the Achilles tendon, sacrotuberous relation, thoracolumbar layers, or cervical continuities disappear. A positive regional result would not automatically promote the entire atlas. Evidence can accumulate junction by junction while the system-level model becomes more specific about where it branches, where one carrier ends, and where another begins.

## One task, three anatomical boundaries

The object-transfer task used throughout Part II makes the scopes concrete without requiring a force calculation. Consider only the carry phase. The object is in the hands, the feet exchange contact with the ground, and the shoulder girdle connects the upper limbs to the trunk. The task is deliberately neutral: no technique, posture, symmetry, pace, or strategy is selected as correct.

On the narrow posterior-chain overlay, the calf–Achilles complexes, hamstrings, gluteus maximus, selected axial muscles, latissimus, trapezius, and posterior scapular muscles are available to participate. *Available* is the controlling word. Anatomy alone does not show which structures are active, how much force they produce, or whether another viable strategy uses them differently.

On the broad posterior-chain overlay, the map adds calcaneal and plantar relations, proximal hamstring and pelvic interfaces, axial aponeuroses and fasciae, thoracolumbar layers, scapular attachments, and cervical relations. These structures reveal possible handoffs and junctions. The overlay still contains no force magnitude, stress field, strain map, or energy account.

On the whole-posterior-support overlay, the ground, skeleton, joints, abdominal and lateral structures, pressure-bearing boundaries, serratus anterior, sensory control, hands, and object appear. The broader boundary is not a claim that everything shown belongs to the posterior chain. It is the system required to ask a closed mechanical question about posterior contribution.

## A major system without a universal-route conclusion

The posterior chain is best understood as a controlled family of anatomical scopes. In its narrow scope, it contains extensive muscle groups and direct myotendinous relations. In its broad scope, it contains aponeuroses, fasciae, ligaments, retinacula, attachments, skeletal junctions, and sliding interfaces that connect and partition regions in several real ways. In its whole-support scope, it joins the complete skeletal, articular, active, pressure, sensory, environmental, and nonposterior system required for mechanical analysis.

The ground-to-cranium map is not an arbitrary collection. It begins with the direct calf–Achilles unit, changes carrier through the foot, branches through the pelvis, divides into long and segmental axial layers, crosses a thoracolumbar and scapular crossroads, and reaches the cranium through variable muscular, fascial, ligamentous, articular, and skeletal relations. Bounded regional transfer has been measured under some experimental conditions. Several posterior structures rank highly by measured muscle volume or physiological cross-sectional area, by gross tendon size or conventional strength description, or by anatomical span. These facts make posterior anatomy a major support and transfer system worthy of focused study.

Anatomy does not determine contribution in a task, comparative efficiency, a preferred global organization, relation to Yang, protection from injury, or need for intervention. Those are different propositions with different observations. A later volume can ask whether posterior participation is necessary within a preferred organization; nothing in this atlas provides that derivation.

The immediate next question is more concrete. The map has shown what structures and relations are available. Chapter 8 asks what they actually do during standing, walking, balance recovery, lifting, carrying, and other ordinary tasks—by phase, through which carrier, in what magnitude, and alongside which other systems.
