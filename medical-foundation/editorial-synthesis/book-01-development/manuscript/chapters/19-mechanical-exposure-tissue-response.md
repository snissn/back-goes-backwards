# Chapter 19 — From mechanical exposure to tissue response

The phrase *load through the lower limb* sounds as if it names one physical event. Under measurement, it separates into different questions.

In one experiment, implanted gauges recorded axial strain at two sites on the tibia while four men walked, ran, and jumped. The measured strain differed by site, activity, and participant.[^19-01] In another, three-dimensional ultrasound followed the Achilles tendon during a controlled isometric contraction. The tendon lengthened, changed shape across its transverse dimensions, and rotated differently along its length.[^19-02] In a third, magnetic resonance imaging recorded regional displacement and strain within knee cartilage during cyclic loading at the foot.[^19-03]

These were different studies, tasks, participants, tissues, and measurements. Their juxtaposition is deliberate. A task name, body weight, ground reaction, or joint moment can describe demand at one scale without specifying what any one tissue receives. Bone requires a local account of stress or strain at a named site. Tendon requires a regional account of tensile and transverse deformation through time. Cartilage requires contact, shear, compression, fluid movement, and recovery to be distinguished. The units need not match, and the values cannot be pooled into a single bodily dose.

The difference matters because living tissues are not passive endpoints. Once a local mechanical exposure has been defined, a tissue can detect and respond to it. The response depends on the receiver, the magnitude and mode of the exposure, its direction and rate, its repetition and sequence, the tissue's existing state, and the opportunity for recovery. The result can be ordinary maintenance, useful adaptation under a stated function, little detectable change, a transient response, remodeling or repair, reduced tolerance, injury, or a mixture of these.

That is the biological bridge this chapter builds. It is an affirmative claim: mechanics can become biologically consequential through specific, measurable relations. It is also a bounded claim. Exposure is not damage. A biological response is not necessarily a structural finding. A finding is not a symptom, a diagnosis, or an outcome. Each transition has to earn its own arrow.

## A demand becomes an exposure only at a receiver

Chapter 18 ended with a history that could contain a declared task, an environment, available paths, an internal distribution, and a named local receiver. Even a well-supported history stops short of biology until the quantity entering that receiver is specified.

Start with a person and environment during a bounded phase of a task. External contacts and inertial effects help determine the force and moment balance of the selected system. Muscles, joints, supports, and other contacts contribute to the internal distribution. At a smaller boundary, a calculated joint moment or estimated muscle force may inform a model of contact or transfer. Smaller again, a tissue can undergo a local pattern of stress, strain, deformation, pressure, fluid movement, or another physical condition.

Each transition changes the object under discussion. A measured ground reaction is not a measured muscle force. A calculated joint moment is not a local stress field. A model of contact pressure is not an observation of matrix strain. A tissue-scale measurement at one surface or imaging plane does not reveal the complete three-dimensional field. A coherent upper-level mechanical account can therefore leave the biological exposure underdetermined.

Even the phrase *local strain* can conceal several decisions. Strain has direction, sign, and a reference configuration. It can vary through depth and across a surface, and it can contain shear as well as extension or compression. A value averaged across a region can answer one question while hiding a concentration that matters to another. Rate requires a time base. A peak requires a sampling window. A model requires constitutive behavior and boundary conditions. None of this makes a local exposure unknowable; it identifies the information required to say what has actually been estimated or measured.

The relevant quantity is chosen by the receiver and proposed response. If the question concerns a mineralized surface under bending, a directional local strain history may be useful. If it concerns a tendon whose regions move differently, a mean longitudinal strain may omit transverse deformation and rotation. If it concerns hydrated cartilage under contact, solid deformation, fluid movement, depth, and recovery can be coupled. The name of the tissue does not by itself choose the variable, but it prevents an upper-level quantity from being treated as sufficient by default.

The tibial measurements make that underdetermination concrete. The gauges sampled one axial orientation at two medial surface locations in four participants. Their directness is unusual, but it does not turn two sites into a complete field or establish a population norm.[^19-01] The Achilles measurements show a different limit: even during a controlled contraction, longitudinal loading did not produce one uniform longitudinal deformation. Transverse strain and rotation varied by region.[^19-02] The cartilage measurements show still another: a force applied at the foot led to rigid-body motion and complex regional intratissue deformation rather than one compressive number.[^19-03]

The scale stack also clarifies the role of the wider routing hypothesis. A proposed whole-body route could eventually predict how a task is distributed and which local exposures differ between configurations. The bone, tendon, and cartilage studies do not test such a route. They establish the narrower premise that a local exposure must be defined before a tissue response can be attributed to it. A route earns biological relevance only by improving that exposure account and then surviving the tissue-specific tests that follow.

This supplies a practical stopping point for a reconstruction. If a history ends with “repeated loading reached the knee,” the receiver and physical quantity remain unspecified. If it ends with a modeled contact field, the model assumptions and validation remain part of the claim. If a study measures intratissue deformation, the biological response remains open. The chain can be scientifically useful at any of these endpoints, but it cannot borrow the next endpoint's meaning.

## Dose is a history, not a count

Once a local exposure is named, its temporal organization becomes part of the input. *Mechanical dose* is useful here only as shorthand for a multidimensional history. Depending on the receiver and question, that history can include magnitude, mode, direction, spatial distribution, rate, waveform, repetition, duration, sequence, variability, intervening rest, prior exposure, and the interval over which recovery is measured.

No single number automatically combines those dimensions. Ten rapid cycles and ten cycles separated by long rests have the same repetition count but not the same sequence. A brief high-rate deformation and a prolonged lower-rate demand can have different peaks, material responses, and recovery histories. Two exposures with the same estimated mean strain can differ in regional distribution or strain duration. Even the word *recovery* is incomplete until it names what is recovering and how that change is measured.

A defensible dose statement answers a set of linked questions. What was the receiving structure and region? Which mechanical mode and local frame were used? How were magnitude and spatial distribution represented? What were the rate, waveform, duration, sequence, and variability? What rest separated the events? What prior history and current capacity entered the interval? Which response was sampled, and when? Not every study can answer every question. The unresolved entries should remain unresolved rather than being supplied by the general word *load*.

The observation interval can change the apparent branch. A short experiment may capture deformation and an early signal but miss retained material change. A late measurement may capture remodeling while missing the transient response that preceded it. A single sample can show a difference without showing whether it was rising, recovering, or stable. Temporal resolution is therefore part of the evidence, not merely a detail of study design.

Bone offers a clean sequence example. In avian ulna and mouse tibia experiments, inserting ten seconds of rest between low-magnitude loading cycles markedly changed periosteal bone-formation measures compared with the corresponding continuous regimen. In the mouse experiment, the rest-inserted regimen also used fewer cycles, and selected endocortical measures did not change.[^19-05] Magnitude alone did not describe the experiment. Neither did repetition count. Sequence, rest, site, and response measure were part of the result.

That result does not supply a human exercise interval. It does something more foundational: it shows that the receiver's biological history is not determined by the largest force or the number of repetitions alone. The same logic applies when an experiment yields a null. A response absent at one site, assay, or interval is not evidence that nothing occurred anywhere; it is a bounded result about that measurement.

Tendon displays several clocks that should not be merged. In healthy young men, collagen fractional synthesis in the patellar tendon rose after a one-hour exercise task, peaked at about twenty-four hours, and remained elevated at the last sampled time. The study measured an acute synthesis response, not local tendon strain, net matrix accumulation, improved function, or tendon injury.[^19-06] In a separate fourteen-week intervention, legs trained at different estimated Achilles-tendon strain magnitudes showed different material outcomes: stiffness and elastic modulus increased in the higher-strain condition, while cross-sectional area did not significantly change in either condition.[^19-07] In another small experiment, three-dimensional Achilles deformation after a conditioning protocol recovered within roughly two hours under the tested conditions.[^19-08]

Those findings concern acute synthesis, retained material behavior, morphology, and mechanical deformation recovery. They do not define one tendon recovery time. A tissue may regain a measured dimension while cellular or matrix processes continue. A synthesis signal may rise without establishing net adaptation. Elapsed rest is therefore not a universal proxy for restored capacity.

Cartilage makes the temporal problem visible through fluid-dependent behavior. After repeated knee bends in a small in-vivo study, patellar-cartilage volume deformation reached a group-level plateau within the tested repetitions, and recovery after one hundred bends took longer than ninety minutes.[^19-04] The observation describes ordinary deformation and fluid-associated recovery under that protocol, not damage or a readiness rule. Ex-vivo work adds different temporal dimensions. Dynamic-compression amplitude, waveform, and frequency affected cartilage biosynthesis,[^19-18] and spatial response patterns were compared with predicted fields of fluid flow, pressure, streaming potential, and cell deformation.[^19-19] The comparison did not independently establish which candidate physical field caused the response.

This is why *cumulative load* must remain an opened rather than a magical phrase. It can name a history after the relevant dimensions have been declared. It cannot imply that forces, moments, strains, pressures, and rest periods add linearly or share one unit. The proper dose description is the smallest history that retains the variables needed for the receiver and response under study.

Mechanical homeostasis is useful at this point as an organizing idea, not a universal set point. A living tissue functions within dynamic mechanical and biological ranges. An exposure can remain compatible with current capacity, contribute to a change in capacity, exceed a local capacity, or be overshadowed by another process. Which of those occurred cannot be inferred from the word *load*. It must be observed through a receiver-specific response and time course.

Capacity also changes while exposure accumulates. Prior loading can be followed by adaptation, fatigue, repair, or incomplete recovery. Disuse, illness, medication, development, sleep, and nutrition can change the state encountered by the next task. A history that adds mechanical events while holding the receiver constant is therefore a useful simplification only when that assumption is declared and reasonable for the interval.

## The receiver changes the question

A dose history still does not determine its own result. The receiver at the beginning of the interval is part of the exposure–response relation.

For bone, relevant state can include site geometry, material quality, existing damage, age, endocrine and metabolic conditions, medication, prior loading, and disuse. For tendon, matrix organization, training history, prior injury, recovery state, age, and metabolic or vascular context can alter the relation among force, deformation, turnover, and material behavior. For cartilage, matrix composition, hydration, thickness, depth, inflammatory state, prior injury, and existing osteoarthritic change can alter deformation and biological response. These are not generic reminders to consider the whole person. Each belongs at a named node when it changes the receiver, the exposure, or the response being claimed.

Human bone evidence shows why mechanical history cannot stand alone as a sufficient explanation. In a population-based cross-sectional sample of 700 adults, age-related patterns in habitual-loading proxies did not correspond closely or consistently to estimated bone-strength indices.[^19-14] The design did not observe individual chronology, but it challenges the proposition that reduced habitual loading by itself accounts for age-related bone loss. A randomized twenty-five-week trial in older women with low bone mass found small, site-specific cortical-density changes in selected training groups alongside many measures with no significant between-group difference.[^19-15] The useful result is the positive-and-null pattern, not a slogan that exercise either does or does not affect bone.

Cartilage supplies a different state comparison. Loaded magnetic resonance imaging in healthy women and women with radiographic knee osteoarthritis found greater deformation tendencies in the osteoarthritis group under the tested condition.[^19-24] Because the comparison was cross-sectional, it does not establish that the tissue state caused the deformation difference, that mechanics initiated the osteoarthritis, or that the measured deformation drove progression. It shows that a named tissue state and a mechanical response can be associated. Chronology remains open.

The matrix is not a ranking of tissue strength or adaptability. It is a guard against transfer. An animal fatigue threshold in bone cannot become a tendon rule. An Achilles strain estimate cannot become a cartilage dose. An imaging proxy in one selected human group cannot become a whole-tissue clinical outcome. The common principle is receiver specificity; the content of the response remains local.

Receiver specificity has a second consequence: nominally matched exposures may not be biologically matched. Two people can perform the same task while local distributions differ. Two samples can reach the same final strain at different rates. Two sites in one tissue can receive different fields or express different responses. A comparison becomes informative only after it states which part of the exposure was held similar and which parts were measured, modeled, or left uncontrolled.

This prevents *individual variation* from becoming an explanation of last resort. Variation can be located. It may enter through geometry, material state, prior history, execution of the task, exposure estimation, assay sensitivity, or observation time. Once located, it can be measured, stratified, modeled, or admitted as an uncertainty. Left unnamed, it cannot rescue a proposed universal relationship.

State dependence also preserves a biology-first branch. A cytokine condition, metabolic change, medication, illness, or pre-existing tissue alteration can precede the mechanics under observation and change how the receiver responds. Mechanics remains consequential when it modifies that state, but consequential is not the same as initiating. This distinction becomes essential once mechanotransduction enters the argument.

## Mechanotransduction opens a fork

*Mechanotransduction* names processes by which cells and their surrounding matrices detect mechanical forces or deformations and translate them into biological activity. Mechanical inputs can alter signaling, synthesis and degradation, cellular organization, and extracellular structure; biological changes can in turn alter the mechanical environment.[^19-09] The term gives us a bridge from a specified physical event to a specified biological measure. It does not tell us in advance which branch the response will take.

The minimum chain begins with a named physical condition at a named tissue. It then identifies the sensing or material event actually studied, the measured cellular or matrix response, and any later structural or functional change. If an experiment ends at biosynthesis, the inference ends there. If it measures deformation but not cellular behavior, it establishes an exposure, not a mechanism. If it records a material property after training, the study can support a change in that property without proving which molecular process produced it.

Mechanotransduction therefore opens possibilities rather than declaring harm. Ordinary maintenance, a useful change under a stated function, no detected response, a transient response with recovery, remodeling, repair, fatigue, reduced tolerance, protective change, sensitization, injury, and failure are different branches. They are not stages in one universal descent from repetition to degeneration.

The language of response must follow the measured object. *Adaptation* means a state change through time; it is useful only relative to a declared task or function. *Remodeling* means organized turnover or structural change; it is not a synonym for degeneration. *Inflammatory response* should identify measured mediators or cells rather than stand in for pain. *No detected response* belongs to an assay, location, and interval, not to the entire tissue.

Four checks make the branch labels usable. First, the object check asks what changed: a molecule, cell population, matrix component, material property, geometry, imaging signal, behavior, symptom, or outcome. Second, the direction check asks whether the change was useful, adverse, neutral, mixed, or not classifiable under the measured function. Third, the time check asks whether it was transient, recovering, retained, progressive, or sampled only once. Fourth, the alternative check asks whether prior state, measurement error, a common cause, behavior, or another biological process can explain the observation.

These checks matter most when a result sounds intuitively positive or negative. Increased synthesis is not necessarily net accumulation. Increased stiffness is not necessarily useful for every function. Signaling near damage is not itself degeneration. A null imaging measure does not cancel a material response captured by another method. Branch language becomes informative only after the object and criterion are declared.

These distinctions do not weaken the mechanical argument. They make it discriminating. If almost any molecular change can be called damage, mechanobiology predicts nothing. If the proposed exposure and receiver state help distinguish formation from fatigue, recovery from retained change, or a material response from a null, mechanics has done explanatory work.

## Three tissues populate the branches differently

The bone, tendon, and cartilage evidence does not produce one exposure–response curve. It populates different portions of the fork.

### Bone: formation, localized fatigue, and active repair

In one small human intervention, twenty postmenopausal women performed daily one-legged jumps for three months. An impact-microindentation measure of tibial bone material changed in the exercised leg, while selected density and microarchitectural measures did not show corresponding changes.[^19-10] This is a positive response in one material proxy beside selected nulls. It is not proof that the whole bone became stronger, that fracture risk changed, or that impact has one general effect.

Animal fatigue models show an adverse branch with unusually clear localization. In rat ulnae, apoptotic osteocytes appeared near microdamage while neighboring non-apoptotic osteocytes expressed pro-osteoclastogenic signals before osteoclasts appeared.[^19-11] A later experiment inhibited apoptosis and also blocked selected fatigue-associated signaling changes, supporting one causal segment within that animal model.[^19-12] The sequence is mechanically and biologically substantive: a localized fatigue exposure can recruit a spatially organized remodeling response. It is not evidence of generalized inflammation, human pain, or an inevitable disease trajectory.

Recovery in the same broad receiver is not merely the absence of loading. After fatigue damage was induced in rat tibiae, weight-bearing and intermittent-weight-bearing conditions showed intracortical resorption and declining microdamage over time, whereas hindlimb suspension did not show the same repair pattern.[^19-13] Ordinary mechanical input participated in the tested repair process. The finding does not become a human treatment rule; it establishes that recovery can include mechanically conditioned activity rather than simple unloading.

Bone therefore contains formation, material change, regional null, fatigue, signaling, remodeling, and repair branches. Which branch is relevant depends on site, protocol, state, and timescale.

### Tendon: acute response, retained material change, and adverse change

The tendon studies separate at least four objects. An ordinary contraction produces heterogeneous three-dimensional deformation. A strenuous exercise task can be followed by an acute rise in collagen synthesis. A fourteen-week strain-targeted intervention can be followed by altered stiffness and modulus without a detectable change in cross-sectional area. A conditioning protocol can be followed by recovery of measured deformation. Those are exposure, synthesis, material adaptation, morphological null, and mechanical recovery—not one continuous experiment.

An adverse branch requires direct adverse evidence. In a rat overuse model, repeated downhill running was followed by increased supraspinatus-tendon cellularity and cross-sectional area, reduced collagen organization, and lower modulus and maximum stress at failure.[^19-16] That regimen altered those tendons in those animals. It does not define a human occupational dose, a diagnosis, or a universal meaning of repetition.

Even structural information does not complete the clinical chain. In prospective studies synthesized in a 2025 review, sonographic abnormalities in initially asymptomatic Achilles and patellar tendons were associated with later pain, but their positive predictive value was approximately twenty-seven percent in the included, mainly athletic cohorts.[^19-17] Structure can carry information without becoming a latent symptom or an individual diagnosis.

### Cartilage: fluid-dependent deformation, interaction, and rate-sensitive injury

Cartilage shows why the receiver cannot be represented as a dry solid under one compressive scalar. Ordinary joint loading produces regional deformation and a fluid-associated recovery history. Dynamic compression in explants changes biosynthetic activity in ways that depend on amplitude, frequency, waveform, and spatial pattern. These responses can be measured without being labeled benefit or damage.

A selected human intervention supplies a bounded longer-term branch. People who had undergone partial medial meniscus resection were randomized to four months of supervised exercise or nonintervention. Among the thirty participants with paired imaging, an imaging-derived measure changed between groups in a direction interpreted as compatible with greater glycosaminoglycan content.[^19-21] The local cartilage exposure was not measured, the outcome was an indirect compositional proxy, and the selected postsurgical group does not define a universal protective dose.

A cell-culture experiment makes the biology-first branch explicit. Primary bovine articular chondrocytes were stimulated with interleukin-1 beta and then exposed to dynamic compression in agarose constructs. Under those conditions, compression altered nitric oxide, prostaglandin E2, proteoglycan synthesis, and proliferation through partly distinct pathways.[^19-22] The inflammatory condition was an experimental input, not an outcome of the compression. The result demonstrates interaction at cell-culture scale; it does not establish an anti-inflammatory effect of exercise, disease modification, or treatment.

Direct adverse experiments populate another branch. Cartilage explants compressed to the same final strain at different rates showed markedly different outcomes: faster protocols reduced viability and biosynthesis, changed compressive and shear stiffness, and reduced the remaining cells' anabolic response to later low-amplitude stimulation.[^19-20] In bovine osteochondral cores exposed to controlled impact, thinner cores fractured at lower energies; among intact cores, cell death increased with impact energy and from the one-day to the four-day assessment.[^19-23] These experiments establish rate-, energy-, structure-, and time-dependent injury within their models. They do not supply a threshold for an ordinary human joint or a direct trajectory to osteoarthritis.

Across all three tissues, the adverse evidence is real where the experiment measured damage, viability, organization, material behavior, or failure. The null and adaptive evidence is equally real where the selected measures remained stable, recovered, or changed in a retained and functionally interpretable direction. A useful mechanobiology framework must be able to contain both without relabeling every result as confirmation.

## Biology changes the mechanics of the next task

The exposure–response relation does not end at biology. A response can change geometry, material behavior, contact, active control, task selection, or capacity, which can alter the mechanics of a later task. That creates feedback.

The tendon intervention gives a simple material example. Increased stiffness and elastic modulus change the relation between force and deformation during a later loading event. The rat overuse model gives the opposite direction: lower modulus and failure stress change what the same nominal task would mean for the altered tendon. Bone formation, damage, resorption, and repair can change a later local field. Cartilage composition, hydration, injury, and existing state can change deformation under a later contact.

Feedback need not amplify. A response can support recovery, redistribute exposure, or leave later mechanics detectably unchanged. A person may also change task, assistance, or activity in response to symptoms or context, making behavior an entry into the next exposure. A common factor—age, treatment, inflammatory state, development, environment, or another change in capacity—can alter both biology and mechanics without either being the sole origin.

A feedback claim needs at least two states and a stable question across them. The first record identifies the exposure and receiver. The next identifies the response or material change. A later mechanical measurement then asks whether deformation, force sharing, contact, or capacity changed under declared conditions. If the later task also changed, the task change belongs in the account. If no later mechanical quantity was measured, the return arrow remains a hypothesis even when the biological change is well established.

This reciprocal structure is stronger than a competition between “mechanical” and “biological” explanations. A mechanical exposure is already acting on a biological receiver. A biological change can become a mechanical condition. The analytical separation exists so that timing, scale, and causal priority remain inspectable.

The separation also prevents a common error. Evidence that an exposure changed a tissue does not prove that the tissue change later altered body-wide distribution. Evidence that an altered tissue deforms differently does not prove which process initiated the alteration. A complete feedback diagram can remain a set of hypotheses until both directions are observed in sequence.

## A tissue response is not yet a clinical conclusion

The final boundary is between the response studied here and the medical objects that follow it. A local exposure, cellular or matrix response, structural finding, function or symptom, diagnosis, and later outcome are not different names for the same event.

Evidence for their non-equivalence is common. A systematic review of thirty-three studies found that several spinal imaging features commonly labeled degenerative were prevalent in asymptomatic people and increased with age.[^19-25] A review of knee magnetic resonance imaging in asymptomatic, uninjured adults likewise found substantial prevalence of cartilage defects, meniscal tears, bone-marrow lesions, and osteophytes, with age patterns differing by feature.[^19-26] The tendon review adds the complementary observation that a structural abnormality can be associated with later pain while retaining low predictive value for an individual.[^19-17]

These findings do not mean that structure is irrelevant. They mean that correspondence has to be measured. A finding can matter in one condition, population, or time frame and be incidental in another. Symptoms can occur without the selected finding. A symptom can change movement and exposure even when its origin remains unresolved. Diagnosis requires criteria and differential reasoning that are not contained in a local strain measurement. Outcome depends on more than the presence of a diagnosis.

The stopping rule is simple: end the claim where the measurement ends. An explant study that measures biosynthesis does not reach pain. A human imaging study that measures deformation does not establish cell signaling. A training study that measures stiffness does not establish symptom change. An asymptomatic finding does not prove future disease, and a symptom does not reconstruct the exposure that preceded it.

Stopping does not mean discarding the preceding result. A measured strain remains a measured strain when no response was recorded. A cellular response remains biologically relevant when no imaging or symptom outcome was studied. A structural finding can be clinically useful in a suitable context even when it occurs without symptoms in other people. The purpose of the ladder is to prevent one valid object from acquiring the authority of another, not to require every investigation to measure the entire chain.

This discipline allows mechanics to occupy different causal positions without forcing it into one rank. A mechanical exposure can initiate a bounded tissue response, contribute to an existing process, modify its expression, follow from a biological or behavioral change, or prove incidental to the clinical question. Chapter 21 will formalize those possibilities. Here, the important result is that none can be selected merely because a mechanical chain is plausible.

## Three receivers, three questions, one biological bridge

Return to the opening measurements. The tibia, Achilles tendon, and articular cartilage did not receive one substance called load. Each study began with a task-level demand and resolved a different local physical event. That event acquired biological meaning only when its magnitude, direction, rate, spatial distribution, temporal history, receiver state, response measure, and recovery interval were made explicit.

The central proposition is therefore both strong and limited. Living tissues can detect and respond to mechanical conditions. Bone, tendon, and cartilage show that maintenance, adaptation, null response, recovery, fatigue, remodeling, repair, injury, and retained material change are genuine branches under different conditions. Biological change can then alter the mechanics of a later exposure. Mechanics is part of living tissue behavior, not an optional metaphor added after biology.

What has not been established is equally exact. A task does not specify a local dose. Repetition does not imply degeneration. Mechanotransduction does not imply harm. A response does not imply a symptom or diagnosis. The tissue studies do not validate a preferred body-wide route, and they do not yield a personal threshold or practice rule.

Chapter 20 inherits the positive rule rather than only its cautions: every receiver needs its own mechanical variables, biological function, and evidence. It applies that rule to conduits, nerves, sliding interfaces, airways, and pressure-bearing cavities, where the consequential output may be patency, flow, excursion, glide, conduction, containment, or pressure rather than the solid-tissue responses compared here.
