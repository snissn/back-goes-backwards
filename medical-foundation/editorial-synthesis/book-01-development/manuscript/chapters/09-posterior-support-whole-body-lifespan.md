# Chapter 9 — Posterior support across the whole body and lifespan

The object is already being carried. The full transfer ended in Chapter 8, so there is no need to lift it again. Keep only one frame: a person moving with the object in the hands while the feet exchange contact with the ground. The posterior overlay remains important—calf and Achilles, hamstrings and gluteal structures, axial muscles, latissimus, trapezius, and their broader connective relations are all available to participate.

Now widen the frame. Add the abdominal wall and thorax, lateral hip and ankle relations, deep segmental structures, skeletal contacts, pressure-bearing boundaries, sensory information, the object's shape and grip, the floor, and any device, furniture, or person supplying support. None of these additions makes the posterior structures less substantial. They show the system in which posterior contribution becomes possible and measurable.

Widen the frame once more, this time through time. The person did not arrive as a completed adult mechanism. Anatomy, proportions, control, experience, confidence, tasks, and environment have histories. Pregnancy can change mass distribution and the assumptions used in a model. Injury, pain, and illness can change the state in which a task begins. Disability and assistance can change available contacts and goals. Training may alter one capacity without altering another. Aging can change margins, strategies, and recovery without placing everyone on one decline curve.

Posterior support remains a major theme across those histories. Its anatomy persists, its available contribution changes, and its meaning depends on the body, task, phase, environment, and support actually present. The purpose of this chapter is to keep all three statements true at once: posterior anatomy is mechanically significant; it operates within a larger whole; and the larger series asks a stronger question about preferred organization that Book I does not need to answer.

## A major participant inside a whole-body system

Whole-body cooperation does not mean that all structures contribute equally. It means that the contribution under discussion belongs to a closed question. Posterior muscles may generate or resist moments, propel, brake, suspend, or redirect. Anterior structures may actively brake, guide, contain, or change pressure relations. Lateral structures can help manage side-to-side demands and foot placement. Deep structures can alter segmental relation. Bones, joints, passive restraints, pressure boundaries, sensory control, and external contacts change the available solution. The partition can be unequal and still be cooperative.

This distinction separates three claims that are easy to compress into one. An anatomical system can be substantial because of its scale, arrangement, and connections. It can have considerable capacity under selected conditions. It can nevertheless contribute little to a particular output during a particular phase. Conversely, a comparatively small structure, a contact with the environment, or a modest pressure change can be decisive when the geometry gives it the relevant leverage. Anatomical significance, available capacity, and participation in the selected task are related questions, not interchangeable answers.

The output matters just as much as the contributor. A structure can oppose a joint moment while doing little positive work. It can receive load while another relation controls motion. It can help stabilize a segment while an external contact carries much of the resultant demand. A pressure-bearing boundary can support shape without replacing the skeleton, and the skeleton can transmit a substantial resultant without explaining how the person maintained balance. “What carried the load?” is therefore unfinished until *load*, carrier, boundary, direction, phase, and outcome have been named.

Walking makes this specificity visible. In simulations based on eight participants, estimated contributions of gluteal, hamstring, plantar-flexor, vasti, and skeletal-alignment relations changed with walking speed and phase.[^09-01] The result is model-dependent, but its central lesson is secure: “posterior participation in walking” is not one constant number. Support and progression are changing outputs, and muscles that contribute strongly in one phase need not do the same work in another.

Balance recovery changes the partition again. In an overground perturbation study of fifteen healthy adults, recovery strategies varied with the direction of the disturbance and included different combinations of stepping and segmental response.[^09-02] A posterior hip or ankle contribution can be substantial without becoming the universal recovery strategy. Arms, lateral foot placement, visual and vestibular information, a rail, or assistance may change what happens next. Information influences the selection of forces and contacts; it is not itself an anatomical carrier of load.

External support makes the boundary change especially clear. In an eight-participant lifting study, varying the lumbar-to-hip support ratio of an active exoskeleton changed modeled spinal loading and lumbar motion.[^09-03] The device did not become part of the person's anatomy. It added contact, force, geometry, mass, and control relations to the task. Whether such a change is useful depends on the endpoint—modeled loading, freedom of motion, comfort, effort, performance, access, or another outcome. The chapter uses the experiment only to establish that an external carrier can reorganize the mechanical solution.

The same logic applies to ordinary assistance. A hand on a counter, an object resting against a shelf, a cane, a cart, an orthosis, a second person, or permission to sit changes the boundary. Removing that support from a model does not reveal the body's pure mechanics. It analyzes a different task.

Posterior importance is therefore compatible with several kinds of variation. A task may rely heavily on posterior work in one phase and mostly on contact or skeletal geometry in another. A person may use an external support to reduce one requirement while increasing upper-limb or control demands. Another may use a distinct but stable asymmetric strategy. The mechanical question is not whether posterior participation is maximized. It is what contributes, to which output, under which boundary, and with what alternatives.

This is a stronger posterior account than a simple inventory. It allows posterior contribution to be large without requiring it to be visually obvious, and it allows a visible posterior motion to occur without assuming posterior tissues carry every important demand. It also makes comparison possible. A study can ask whether posterior participation changes when object position, pace, support, fatigue, or task phase changes, while preserving the other contributors needed to interpret the result. The posterior system becomes a tractable object inside whole-body mechanics rather than either a universal explanation or an isolated chain.

## Region, domain, role, and sign are different questions

Several project terms often appear together because the larger theory proposes a systematic relationship among them. They cannot be made synonymous at the definition stage.

**Region** asks where a structure lies relative to a declared anatomical frame: posterior thigh, anterior abdominal wall, lateral hip, or deep spinal region. **Relative domain** asks what lies outside or inside a selected boundary: skin relative to viscera, a cavity wall relative to its contents, or a superficial layer relative to a deep compartment. The answer changes with the boundary. Exterior is not automatically posterior, and interior is not automatically anterior.

**Mechanical role** asks what a selected structure or assembly does in a direction, task, state, and timescale. The hard/soft language developed earlier is motivated by traditional Chinese medicine, but in Book I it names relative shape-preserving and form-receiving roles. A posterior muscle can be active while lengthening and receiving form. A tendon can transmit substantial tension while deforming. An anterior or lateral pressure-supported boundary can preserve a larger shape. A bone can cease to carry a dominant path when contact changes. No region or tissue owns one role.

**Proposed sign or traditional relation** asks a later question about Yin, Yang, and the coordinate families developed in Books II and III. Those categories may prove to correspond systematically with region, domain, or role. They are not yet validated mechanical variables, and the correspondence cannot be installed by labeling posterior structures Yang in advance.

Keeping the columns separate protects rather than weakens the broader hypothesis. If a future model predicts that certain relations recur together, the prediction can be tested against counterexamples. A correspondence that is true by definition cannot fail and therefore cannot explain why one body, task, or phase differs from another.

Consider one structure across a single movement. It may begin in a comparatively shape-preserving role, accept deformation while controlling motion, transmit tension as another joint changes relation, and then become mechanically quiet after an external support receives the object. Its anatomical region has not changed. Its role has. A neighboring structure may make the opposite transition, while a pressure boundary helps preserve the organization of the trunk across both phases. The whole-body account is therefore not a contest in which hard defeats soft. It is an account of where shape is preserved, where form is received, and how those roles exchange without losing the task.

The same discipline applies to the word *exterior*. Skin is exterior relative to the tissues beneath it, yet a deep cavity wall can be exterior relative to its contents. A posterior layer can lie inside another posterior layer. A later Yin/Yang correspondence may assign importance to such nested relations, but posterior location cannot settle the assignment. By preserving these distinctions now, Book I can supply examples and counterexamples to later formal work instead of quietly deciding its result.

## Development and training create specific options

Support is acquired and reorganized. Early movement does not consist of a weak version of one completed adult pattern. Body proportions, contact possibilities, muscle and tendon properties, sensory calibration, confidence, environmental opportunity, and learned coordination change together. The history is not contained in anatomy alone.

A longitudinal study followed eighteen infants at two or three time points between birth and walking onset. Trial-to-trial organization of leg-muscle activity changed across development: early variability could be represented through relatively few modeled locomotor primitives, while later activity became more regular as the identified primitive structure changed.[^09-04] The small electromyography and modeling study does not supply a universal developmental law. It does show why development should not be described as one fixed coordination merely becoming stronger.

Naturalistic observation provides another scale. Recordings of twelve- to nineteen-month-old infants showed large numbers of short, variable locomotor bouts, with steps and falls distributed through ordinary exploration rather than confined to periodic straight walking.[^09-05] The sample averages are not milestones or instructions. They show that variability, repetition, contact, failure to complete a step, and renewed attempts can all be part of acquiring a task.

Childhood and adolescence continue the history without following a single mechanical script. Growth changes segment lengths, mass distribution, leverage, contact geometry, and the relation between a familiar task and present capacity. New environments and activities add skills and exposures. A strategy can be viable during one period and reorganized as proportions, goals, or opportunities change. The relevant continuity is not visual preservation of one pattern. It is the repeated construction of workable relations among the current body, task, support, and surroundings.

Adult mechanics remain historical as well. Occupation, sport, caregiving, travel, inactivity, injury, chosen practice, and ordinary repetition can make some demands familiar and others novel. Two people with similar gross anatomy may reach the same external result through different timing, contacts, confidence, or effort because they arrive with different histories. Those differences need not be errors. They become mechanically important when the question concerns exposure, transfer, fatigue, recovery, or available alternatives.

Posterior structures participate throughout this history, but “posterior engagement” is too coarse to describe what changes. Growth alters geometry and mass distribution. Experience alters prediction and contact choices. A task that is difficult under one set of proportions may become ordinary under another. Different developmental routes can arrive at viable ways of standing, moving, reaching, or using assistance. The useful question at each stage is what anatomy, task, environment, support, and strategy are available now—not how closely the person resembles one adult template.

Training is also specific. It can alter force, power, endurance, tissue dimensions, stiffness, timing, skill, confidence, perception, or task strategy, and those changes do not transfer evenly. In a six-week randomized comparison of thirty older adults, conventional high-intensity resistance training produced larger changes in the trained strength tests, while loaded functional practice produced larger changes in gait speed and timed-up-and-go; some isometric and muscle-size measures changed in both groups.[^09-06] The study was small and short. Its value here is that it separates “improvement” into trained exposure, measured outcome, and transfer to another task.

A posterior muscle group can become stronger without becoming more important in every activity. A task can improve because timing, confidence, contact, or another system changed. A measured gain can persist, fade, or transfer selectively. Training can therefore expand one option without restoring an assumed natural route. This chapter makes no program recommendation. It establishes that current participation and capacity are historical, acquired, and measure-specific.

## Pregnancy changes the model, not the person's status

Pregnancy changes the person–environment system through time. Mass and its distribution change. Anthropometric assumptions used to estimate segment centers can become less accurate. Tissue and pressure relations, fatigue, recovery, comfort, available support, and chosen strategies may change. None of those changes supplies one pregnancy posture or one posterior deficit.

In a three-dimensional gait comparison involving fifteen women late in pregnancy and again one year postpartum, overall kinematic changes were smaller than a stereotyped “waddling” account would suggest even though body mass and trunk mass distribution changed. Hip and ankle kinetic measures increased, and pelvic-tilt changes varied among individuals.[^09-07] The external task could remain recognizable while internal demands and individual trajectories differed.

A separate repeated-measures treadmill study of fifteen pregnant participants found gestation-related changes in center-of-mass-based walking-balance measures. The estimated result also changed materially with the anthropometric method used to locate the trunk center of mass.[^09-08] The model has to change when the body being modeled changes. A familiar silhouette or a calculation based on nonpregnant segment assumptions cannot establish the actual internal distribution.

Posterior structures may contribute differently as geometry, pressure relations, support, and fatigue change, but anterior, lateral, deep, sensory, and environmental partners remain part of the account. A handrail, changed pace, footwear, rest, or another chosen support can be an effective part of the system rather than evidence that an unassisted pattern has failed. The cited studies do not establish symptoms, fall mechanism, treatment, or a correction. They establish variable mechanics within a changing and still viable task.

Pregnancy also makes timescale explicit. A comparison between late pregnancy and a later postpartum measurement does not describe every transition between them, and a group mean does not describe every participant. The same person may use different solutions as the task, day, comfort, or available support changes. A useful mechanical model follows those changing conditions rather than treating pregnancy as a fixed category appended to an otherwise complete adult model.

## Pain, injury, illness, and connective-tissue variation require separate accounts

Pain can change movement without identifying its own origin. In a crossover experiment with thirty healthy adults, electrical stimulation was coupled to lumbar flexion or extension during repeated box lifting. Participants reduced movement in the pain-provoking direction, and some changes persisted after stimulation ended.[^09-09] Because the pain was experimentally imposed and direction-linked, the study supports adaptation to that protocol. It does not show that a similar movement in chronic pain reveals the initiating tissue, earlier load path, or needed correction.

Injury changes the starting state. Material properties, sensation, confidence, active control, external support, and the task itself may be different after injury. The mechanical question is not automatically “Which posterior route failed?” It is: given the present tissue state, goal, contacts, and alternatives, what does the current organization preserve and what demand does it redistribute? Any specific injury would require its own evidence before the direction or magnitude of that reorganization could be asserted.

Illness is not one mechanical category. A particular condition or its treatment may alter energy availability, recovery, circulation, sensation, control, range, attention, or the value of assistance. Another condition may affect none of those variables in the task being studied. A useful account must name the condition, relevant variable, task, time, treatment context, and comparison. The only general claim needed here is that illness can change the system being modeled and cannot be represented as a generic departure from a canonical healthy body.

Connective-tissue variation shows why a label does not dictate a strategy. In a cross-sectional comparison of twenty-nine people classified with joint hypermobility syndrome and thirty controls, investigators reported group differences in walking speed, stance and double-support duration, joint moments, and power. The observed gait was described as relatively stiff rather than visibly hypermobile.[^09-10] Selection, symptoms, and cross-sectional design prevent a causal conclusion. The result demonstrates that passive-range classification does not uniquely determine how a person organizes an active task.

These contexts also clarify *compensation*. A compensation is first a change that preserves something valuable: task completion, comfort, protection of a current tissue state, balance, participation, or energy. It may later prove efficient or costly, temporary or stable, optional or necessary. Those are separate questions. Calling it compensation does not diagnose hidden damage, predict future failure, or tell the person to remove it.

Preservation and consequence must be evaluated on their own timescales. A strategy can solve the immediate problem and increase demand somewhere else. It can also look unusual while remaining comfortable, durable, and well suited to the person's priorities. Either possibility needs evidence. The existence of redistributed demand does not establish that the redistribution exceeds capacity, and the absence of an immediate symptom does not establish that every exposure is inconsequential. Part III will supply the language for dose, concentration, continuity, and recovery that those questions require.

The chronology matters. A changed strategy can follow pain, injury, illness, or altered support; it can also precede a symptom or be unrelated to it. A later observation does not reconstruct the unobserved transition by itself. Part III will ask when a useful substitution persists, concentrates demand, or narrows available options. Chapter 9 supplies the positive baseline: reorganization can be coherent and successful under the conditions in which it occurs.

## Disability and assistance belong inside the system boundary

Disability cannot be represented mechanically by subtracting abilities from an unmarked reference body. The World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health treats functioning and disability as interactions among health conditions and contextual factors, including environment, activity, and participation.[^09-11] The classification is not a constitutive model of tissue. Its relevant discipline is that the body, task, environment, and desired participation belong in the same description.

An assistive device adds contacts, constraints, geometry, mass, sensory information, and control requirements. It can create a route that does not exist in the unassisted model. In a within-subject study, twenty healthy participants walked while unloading one ankle and foot with forearm crutches, a knee crutch, or an unloading orthosis. Kinematic, force, metabolic, comfort, and stability measures differed among devices.[^09-12] The study does not represent disabled users, long-term use, clinical effectiveness, or every device. It demonstrates that the chosen support changes the mechanical solution and its tradeoffs.

A complete assisted system is therefore:

```text
person + device or helper + fit and contact + ground + task + environment + control history
```

Removing a wheelchair, cane, prosthesis, orthosis, rail, chair, helper, or environmental support from that boundary may remove what makes the task viable. It does not reveal the true person beneath an artificial compensation. Assistance can be ordinary architecture.

The interface matters as much as the device name. Contact location, fit, friction, stiffness, timing, user control, and the ground or furniture against which the device acts can change the resulting forces and possibilities. A device can reduce demand at one region while adding work, pressure, attention, or coordination elsewhere. Those changes are neither automatically beneficial nor automatically harmful; they identify the actual system that should be measured.

Assistance also changes what counts as capacity. If a person can reliably complete a valued task with a chosen device or another person's help, that capability belongs to the assisted system. An unassisted laboratory result may answer a narrower research question, but it cannot replace the participation outcome by definition. The boundary should be selected to match the question, not selected in advance to privilege independence from support.

This view also prevents symmetry from becoming an undeclared goal. An asymmetric strategy can be stable, economical, comfortable, skilled, or required by the available contacts. Its value depends on the person's goals and alternatives, not on visual resemblance to a bilaterally symmetric diagram. Laboratory mechanics can compare contacts, forces, moments, energy, and performance. It cannot determine a person's priorities or preferred participation without lived-experience and accessibility input.

Posterior structures remain significant within assisted and disabled systems. Their contribution may be increased, reduced, redistributed in time, or joined through a different external carrier. No single direction follows from the category. The posterior question remains empirical and task-specific rather than disappearing or becoming a deficit score.

## Aging changes reserve along more than one trajectory

Aging is not one posterior-chain decline. Muscle mass, force, power, tendon behavior, bone, cartilage, balance, sensation, recovery, confidence, medication, health conditions, task familiarity, and environment can change at different rates and in different directions. Population averages describe populations; they do not assign an individual trajectory.

An eight-year study followed 2,364 adults who were seventy to seventy-nine and initially well functioning. The analysis identified three gait-speed trajectories with meaningfully different rates of change.[^09-13] The categories were statistical descriptions, not destinies. They show that even one familiar mobility measure does not follow a single aging curve.

Different measurements also answer different questions. In longitudinal data from 934 adults aged at least sixty-five, baseline lower-extremity strength and power related to later mobility outcomes, but baseline level and change over time were not interchangeable predictors.[^09-14] A later analysis from the same cohort found that leg power discriminated later loss of activities of daily living better than knee strength in men, while neither measure showed the same advantage in women.[^09-15] Strength, power, walking speed, and daily activity are related, not substitutable.

This chapter uses *reserve* provisionally as a conditional margin. For a defined task, time window, and current state, reserve is the margin between the demand being met and the capability available through the whole person–environment system. It is not one scalar already measured by age, gait speed, posterior strength, or a laboratory maximum.

Nor is reserve necessarily one hidden quantity waiting to be found. A person may have ample force for a task but little endurance for its repetition, or sufficient steady-state capability but a narrow margin under an unexpected perturbation. Recovery may become limiting even when the first attempt is easy. A supportive environment may enlarge practical margin without changing tissue capacity. For now, reserve is best treated as a family of task-specific margins whose dimensions must be declared rather than collapsed into one score.

The margin can change from either side. Training may expand one capability. Illness, sleep loss, or recent exertion may narrow it temporarily. A rail can reduce effective demand during a transition. Familiarity, pacing, or task redesign can make the same goal occupy less of the available margin. A device may reduce demand at one region while adding another requirement. Recovery time can matter even when peak capability is unchanged.

Experience and assistance are therefore part of reserve, not contaminants in its measurement. A slower pace may preserve a useful margin. A familiar route may require less attention. A chair at the right height can make a transition available. None of these proves superior health or optimal mechanics. Each shows why reserve belongs to a declared task and boundary rather than to a person as a permanent score.

Posterior capacity can be an important component of reserve. It may matter greatly for a particular gait phase, transition, carry, or perturbation. But posterior strength alone cannot represent sensory confidence, balance, endurance, recovery, environment, and alternative support. The positive claim survives intact: posterior capability is consequential. Its consequence belongs to the whole system.

## Candidate observations are questions, not tests

The larger project needs observable consequences if it is to become testable. The correct response is not to suppress recognizable signs; it is to state what question each sign might answer.

**Participation** asks whether a named structure or system contributed during a task. Candidate observations include electromyography, tendon strain, pressure, contact force, device load, joint-moment sensitivity, or modeled muscle force. Each needs a task, phase, signal model, and alternative account.

**Capacity** asks what a selected system can meet under stated conditions. Candidate observations include maximal or repeated task output, force, power, endurance, range under demand, and recovery of performance. Repeatability, safety, task specificity, strategy, and population matter. A capacity result is not a tissue-health score.

**Compensation** asks what changed to preserve a valued outcome. Timing, contact, asymmetry, bracing, pace, support, assistance, or route may change. Chronology, the preserved goal, available alternatives, and any cost must be established before the change is called adverse.

**Reserve** asks what margin remained as demand was graded, support was removed or added, a perturbation occurred, fatigue developed, or recovery proceeded. The question needs a stopping rule, timescale, whole-system boundary, and a definition of the capability being compared.

A sit-to-stand transition illustrates the separation. Observing trunk inclination or foot placement describes movement. Measuring seat and ground reaction forces adds external load information. Estimating joint moments adds a calculation whose assumptions must be stated. Surface electromyography may indicate selected muscle activation without determining total force. Repeating the task under a graded demand may inform one capacity or fatigue question. Adding arm support may reveal how an external contact reorganizes the solution. None of these observations alone establishes compensation, reserve, tissue condition, or the reason one strategy was chosen.

The same marker can therefore serve different questions only through different study designs. Slower movement might reflect deliberate pacing, limited power, pain avoidance, unfamiliarity, a precision goal, or the requirements of a device. Greater asymmetry might preserve contact, reduce discomfort, accommodate anatomy, or simply fall within ordinary variation. A useful marker does not carry its interpretation in its appearance. Comparison, chronology, task definition, and alternatives give it meaning.

Observation states must remain explicit. An observed movement is not a force measurement. A calculated joint moment is not an individual-muscle force. A modeled contribution is not direct observation. An inference about compensation requires chronology. An embodied continuity can motivate measurement without becoming an instrument reading. A change after assistance shows that the boundary mattered; it does not diagnose why.

This measurement discipline leaves room for null results. An asymmetry may be reliable but add no useful prediction. A posterior marker may not distinguish the selected outcome. A visible brace may be purposeful. Reported fatigue may occur without a decline in measured output, and output may decline without identifying a tissue. The project advances when an observation narrows, revises, or fails to support a proposed relation—not only when it confirms one.

## Three independently gated posterior propositions

The posterior argument now has three levels. They are related by the research program, but each has its own evidence and publication job.

**Level 1 — anatomical and mechanical existence.** Posterior structures form a major, layered anatomical and mechanical system. Chapter 7 established the anatomy: large-by-measure muscles, substantial tendons, long and short axial layers, broad aponeuroses and fasciae, branching skeletal junctions, and bounded regional continuities. Chapter 8 established task-specific mechanics: posterior structures can make substantial contributions to moments, work, braking, propulsion, elastic behavior, balance, and ordinary function. This level is a Book I conclusion. *Major* does not mean exclusive, sufficient, or dominant in every task.

**Level 2 — a conditional whole-body lens.** Anterior–posterior distribution is a useful way to investigate whole-body load sharing when that comparison changes a prediction, measurement, or interpretation. It can ask whether a task is resolved differently as posterior and anterior contributions, contacts, or exposures change. It remains one lens alongside lateral, deep, pressure, temporal, sensory, and environmental relations. This level is also Book I. The lens may add little for a particular task or outcome, and that null result is allowed.

**Level 3 — the preferred-organization hypothesis.** We expect posterior participation to be necessary but insufficient within the preferred organization investigated in Book II. The later model proposes an all-Yang organization involving downward axial resolution, external or opening rotation, and outward expansion. Posterior participation alone is not sufficient: ordinary extension, visible backward movement, or large posterior work can occur without the proposed organization. The expectation is direct, but it is not derived by Chapters 1–9 and does no load-bearing work in Book I.

*Necessary* at Level 3 still needs a scale and definition. The proposal may concern a whole-person organization while a selected posterior structure is quiet, lengthening, internally loaded, or mechanically secondary during one phase. Book II must define participation, the preferred state, the relevant efficiency or comparison criterion, and observations that would disfavor necessity. Tasks or scales with absent or optional posterior contribution are countercases to explain, not exceptions to absorb after the fact.

>

A reader can therefore accept Level 1, use or test Level 2, and remain unconvinced by Level 3 without contradiction. The project can also revise Level 2's usefulness in a particular domain without erasing posterior anatomy. Keeping the gates independent gives the stronger hypothesis somewhere honest to stand while its formal work remains ahead.

## From viable support to load management

Posterior support is substantial, variable, and inseparable from the whole person–environment system. Its anatomy and ordinary function justify focused attention. Its available contribution changes with development, training, pregnancy, pain, injury, illness, connective-tissue variation, disability, assistance, aging, and reserve. Those changes do not arrange bodies along one scale from normal to defective.

Hard and soft remain changing roles rather than posterior and anterior territories. Compensation begins as preservation of something valuable. Assistance can be architecture. Reserve is a conditional margin rather than a youth or health score. Candidate observations become useful only when their question, provenance, validation, and alternatives are specified.

This closes the positive architecture of Part II. Level 1 establishes the posterior system; Level 2 authorizes a conditional anterior–posterior lens; Level 3 states the preferred-organization hypothesis directly without making it carry Book I.

Part III begins from this viable baseline. It asks what happens as demand approaches available capacity, continuity or handoff changes, exposure concentrates, a form-receiving structure is maintained under deformation, control becomes unstable, or a compensation alters what happens next. Those are questions about changing load management, not a catalogue of defective bodies.
