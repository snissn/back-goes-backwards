# Chapter 21 — Causal scope, alternatives, and the whole person

A person reports pain and reduced tolerance for a familiar lifting task. During observation, the lift is slower than it once was, the trunk moves through a smaller range, and support shifts earlier toward the arms. Imaging also shows a common structural change near the painful region.

This is a hypothetical observation set, not a disguised diagnosis. Nothing in it is unusual enough to choose a cause. The movement strategy may have altered local exposure and contributed to a tissue response. A tissue or inflammatory state may have produced pain first, with the movement change following. Fear of the expected pain may have changed the lift. Work demands, sleep, available help, or prior advice may have changed both exposure and recovery. Several of these processes may now reinforce one another. The imaging finding may matter to anatomy while adding little to the explanation of the current pain.

All of those accounts can be told coherently. Coherence is not what separates them.

The preceding chapters have made a mechanical account increasingly specific. We can name a task, define a system boundary, describe configuration and available paths, distinguish force from stress and strain, identify a receiver, and ask how repeated exposure meets tissue state and recovery. We can compare a vessel, nerve, sliding interface, or pressure-bearing boundary without pretending that their outputs are interchangeable. That work makes a causal question possible. It does not settle one.

This chapter supplies the causal contract for the mechanical program. Its question is not whether mechanics exists in a physical body. It is: **For a declared question, how much explanatory or decision-relevant work does the proposed mechanical relation perform beyond the alternatives?**

The answer can be substantial. Mechanical history may organize an exposure, explain a local transition, distinguish two otherwise similar histories, or reveal a research object that ordinary endpoint descriptions miss. It can also explain only one part of a mixed history, follow from another process, or add no useful information to the question at hand. A serious mechanical medicine must be able to produce all of those results. Otherwise it is not a framework for inquiry; it is a way to redescribe every outcome in its own language.

## Scope comes before causal rank

“Mechanics matters” is too broad to test. It combines different questions whose answers need not agree. Did a mechanical exposure participate in the origin of a tissue state? Does configuration alter local exposure now? Does support change present symptoms? What limits the task? What predicts recurrence? What affects recovery? Would adding a routing account change a decision? A mechanical relation can be central to one of these questions and peripheral to another in the same person.

A reviewable causal question needs a contrast. It identifies the population and setting, the system boundary, the candidate relation, a starting time and interval, a comparator, the receiver, and the outcome. Causal-inference methods formalize this discipline by asking what change is being compared and what would otherwise have occurred.[^21-01] A causal diagram can then disclose the paths being assumed, including common causes, mediation, and competing routes.[^21-02] The diagram does not prove its arrows. It is a disciplined statement of the model that evidence must test.

Classic causal considerations such as temporality, consistency, gradient, experiment, plausibility, and coherence are useful viewpoints, not a checklist that turns an association into a cause.[^21-03] In this chapter, their practical lesson is simple: define the proposition before ranking its importance. A visible configuration is not a measured local exposure. A measured exposure is not a tissue response. A tissue response is not necessarily a symptom source. A symptom response to support is not a reconstruction of origin.

Consider the opening observations again. If the question is whether the person currently moves differently while pain is present, direct task observation may answer it well. If the question is whether the movement strategy preceded the pain, the same observation is temporally inadequate. If the question is whether the imaging finding identifies the painful structure, prevalence in people without symptoms and competing sources become important. If the question is whether adding a detailed routing history improves an existing decision, accuracy at an earlier mechanical link is not enough; the added account must change the declared result.

Scope also prevents a common retreat. If a broad origin claim fails, the framework cannot preserve itself merely by saying mechanics was “a modifier” without defining what was modified. But neither must a strongly measured local relation be discarded because it does not explain the entire presentation. The exact proposition can win or lose while the larger research program continues to learn.

The comparator deserves equal care. “No mechanical account” is often impossible as a literal contrast because every task has mechanical conditions. A useful comparison might instead be one routing model against another, a routing history added to ordinary clinical information, a measured receiver-level variable added to a structural finding, or a mechanical perturbation compared with a credible control. The question is not whether the comparator escapes physics. It is whether the proposed account contributes information not already contained in the alternative.

This distinction makes causal scope more than cautious wording. It determines what must be measured. An origin question needs temporal evidence before the selected tissue state. A current-exposure question needs a receiver-level quantity or a validated model under declared boundary conditions. A modulation question needs a defined change and comparator. An incremental-value question needs a baseline account and a prespecified result that could fail to improve. Without those elements, disagreement about “how much mechanics matters” is often disagreement among different questions concealed by one phrase.

## Mechanics can enter a history without occupying one fixed box

This project has often distinguished mechanically led, mixed, downstream, and other-primary histories. That distinction is worth preserving as a set of questions, not as a form to be completed for every person.

Did a defined mechanical relation precede the receiver state? Did it alter a measured intermediate? Did repeated exposure change the dose presented to the next event? Does it change current function without identifying origin? Is the movement pattern chiefly a response to injury, inflammation, pain, or fatigue? Did a common environment change both task demand and recovery? Does the added account discriminate anything beyond what is already known?

Words such as *primary*, *contributory*, *modifying*, *consequential*, and *incidental* can summarize an answer when the evidence supports one. They are not natural kinds. The same relation can occupy different places at different times. An inflammatory state can change tissue and movement first. The changed movement can then redistribute later exposure. Pain and protection can alter the next task again. A single label applied to the whole history would hide the very sequence the routing model is meant to reveal.

This flexibility does not make the framework unfalsifiable. A proposed relation becomes vulnerable to evidence when it names the transition it claims to explain. It weakens if the required exposure cannot be measured or reconstructed reliably, if temporal order runs the other way, if the variable fails to distinguish candidate histories, if manipulating it does not change the predicted intermediate, or if ordinary clinical, ergonomic, behavioral, or social information already captures everything useful it supplies.

That last possibility is important to the ambition of this book. The project does not propose that biomechanics remain forever a modest adjunct to an otherwise fixed medical account. Mechanical history and distribution may eventually change how selected problems are researched, taught, prevented, or understood before rehabilitation begins. But the route to a larger role is evidence of added explanatory or decision value, accumulated across specific questions. Declaring mechanics foundational to all disease would make that ambition less testable, not more consequential.

## Joint disease tests relevance against priority

Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis offer a difficult boundary exercise because both make mechanics visible while resisting a simple mechanical-versus-biological split.

Osteoarthritis is a heterogeneous whole-joint disease in which local and systemic factors interact.[^21-04] A deliberately strong mechanical account argues that mechanically induced joint injury and pathomechanics occupy a central position in much osteoarthritis.[^21-05] That argument deserves to be stated at full strength. It also has to be compared with age, adiposity, prior injury, joint shape, inflammation, metabolism, muscle, behavior, pain, and differing phenotypes rather than allowed to replace them.

Rheumatoid arthritis is an inflammatory disease in which immune dysregulation, genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures, synovial inflammation, and downstream cartilage and bone damage carry major causal weight.[^21-06] That makes it a clear test of whether a mechanical framework can allow another process to lead. It does not make mechanics disappear. Inflamed or damaged joints still meet task demand. Pain, swelling, weakness, treatment, and structural change can alter movement and distribution. Those later mechanical relations may matter greatly to function or local exposure without becoming the upstream explanation of rheumatoid arthritis.

The evidence becomes more useful when each result is assigned to its own arrow. In adults who already had knee osteoarthritis, baseline malalignment was prospectively associated with compartment-specific structural progression over eighteen months.[^21-07] That temporal relation supports a role for geometry in progression under the measured conditions; it does not prove disease initiation or eliminate residual common causes. In knees without radiographic osteoarthritis, baseline magnetic-resonance evidence of synovitis was prospectively associated with incident disease after adjustment for measured structural and mechanical covariates.[^21-08] That observational result keeps inflammatory tissue state inside the prospective account without proving an independent causal effect.

An experimental inflammatory-arthritis program provides a third kind of evidence. Loading and unloading manipulations changed the location and severity of disease in mouse joints while the systemic immune process remained. The accompanying human study mapped erosion location cross-sectionally in people with rheumatoid arthritis.[^21-09] The causal manipulation was performed in mice, not in people. The bounded inference is nonetheless important: mechanics can plausibly localize or amplify an immune-driven process without being established as the initiator of human rheumatoid arthritis.

The contrast therefore produces several answers, not two disease boxes. For disease initiation, immune-inflammatory processes have a central position in rheumatoid arthritis, while osteoarthritis contains interacting mechanical and biological histories. For current local exposure, geometry, task, pain, strength, tissue state, and support can matter in either. For present symptoms and participation, structural state, inflammation, pain processing, behavior, environment, and mechanics can diverge. A response to a support or movement change would establish present modulation under its conditions in either disease; it would not retroactively identify origin.

This exercise is not a lesson in diagnosing or managing either condition. Its job is narrower. Mechanical relevance and causal primacy are different questions. A framework that cannot preserve that distinction will mistake the physical reality of a joint for a complete account of its disease.

The rheumatoid-arthritis example supplies a clear version of another process carrying the greater origin burden. The same reasoning applies whenever an independently established process already organizes the origin and immediate medical question more strongly than a newly noticed asymmetry or routing story. The person still has forces, moments, pressure, support, and movement. Those facts do not make the added mechanical account discriminating.

Suppose an independently established biological process explains the tissue state, the timing, and the urgent decision. A detailed account of how the person now protects the region might still describe current function accurately. It may identify a later exposure or a question about participation and recovery. But if it changes neither the explanation under review nor the selected outcome, its priority for that question is low. The correct conclusion is not that mechanics has ceased to exist. It is that the proposed mechanical history has done no additional work here.

This stopping point is especially important when a mechanical observation is visually compelling. A marked postural change can follow pain, weakness, swelling, neural loss, treatment, or the practical need to protect a region. In such a history, the observation may be a consequential adaptation rather than the origin. Reversing it in imagination does not reverse the disease process. The account earns a larger role only if it identifies a separate transition—perhaps current exposure, task tolerance, or recovery—and supports that transition on its own terms.

Other-primary does not mean permanently nonmechanical. Priorities can change with time and endpoint. An acute biological process may dominate origin and immediate care while mechanical questions later become important to function. A mechanical exposure may then join a reciprocal loop. The point is not to keep domains in separate territories. It is to prevent the later relevance of mechanics from being used as retrospective proof that mechanics initiated the history.

## The arrows run backward and form loops

Forward mechanical stories are cognitively easy. A demand changes exposure; exposure changes tissue; tissue change affects function or symptoms. The reverse direction is equally physical: pain, inflammation, fatigue, fear, injury, or altered sensation can change task selection, active control, stiffness, movement, and the distribution of the next demand.

Controlled experiments make that direction visible. In thirty healthy adults performing repeated lifts, experimentally induced movement-evoked lumbar pain led participants to reduce movement in the pain-provoking direction but not the non-provoking direction.[^21-10] The result shows pain preceding a direction-specific strategy under the protocol. It does not reproduce chronic low-back-pain biology or establish whether that short-term response is helpful, costly, or persistent.

In another experiment, fifty-one initially pain-free participants performed reaching tasks before and after delayed-onset trunk-extensor soreness was induced. Pain-related fear did not predict baseline lumbar flexion, but it did predict reduced flexion during selected reaching tasks after soreness developed.[^21-11] Fear did not replace the physical state; it changed the motor response conditional on that state.

The redistribution can be hidden behind a stable task output. Fourteen healthy volunteers maintained a matched low external force while experimental deep-tissue pain altered which motor units were recruited.[^21-12] The observed force therefore did not reveal an unchanged internal strategy. The experiment was acute and small, so it cannot supply the origin or consequence of a chronic recruitment pattern. It does show why present distribution cannot always be inferred from successful task completion.

Biological state can also precede gross movement change. In a double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover experiment, investigators obtained nineteen usable movement datasets after inducing a transient inflammatory sickness state or administering saline. Walking and mobility measures changed during the induced state.[^21-13] The experiment cannot isolate inflammation from the accompanying fatigue, nausea, pain, dizziness, mood, or awareness of condition. That inseparability is part of the result needed here: biology and lived sickness can lead into changed movement instead of appearing only after a mechanical exposure.

Once the reverse arrow exists, a reciprocal history becomes possible:

**candidate exposure → tissue or pain state → changed strategy → changed later exposure**

Each arrow needs separate support. Evidence that pain changed movement does not establish the exposure that produced the pain. Evidence that the later strategy changes exposure does not establish whether the change is protective or adverse. A loop can become important even when its first entry point remains unresolved, but uncertainty about the entry point must remain visible.

Expectation and meaning can enter a different node. In a controlled healthy-participant experiment, cues associated with higher or lower pain altered pain ratings and brain responses to a controlled stimulus.[^21-14] This supports expectation-related modulation of experience under the protocol. It does not imply that pain is unreal, that physical exposure is absent, or that expectation explains a disease.

A within-person study of sixty-six people with episodic migraine similarly varied whether rizatriptan or placebo was labeled positively, neutrally, or negatively and included an untreated condition. The information attached to the pill altered reported pain response while the active drug retained an overall advantage.[^21-15] On the primary pain-score outcome, open-label placebo improved relative to no treatment; it did not significantly improve the secondary pain-free outcome. The distinction matters. Meaning changed a selected symptom measure without becoming migraine origin or replacing pharmacology.

These examples put psychological and experiential variables inside causal structure rather than outside a “real” mechanical account. Expectation can modify the symptom bridge. Fear can alter task selection after pain appears. Prior learning can change protection and variability. Those changes can then alter later exposure. None of this requires psychology to explain every symptom or mechanics to disappear.

## The whole person enters at named nodes

Whole-person language becomes weak when it is appended as a list after the preferred mechanism. A stronger account asks where a factor enters, which transition it could change, over what interval, and for which outcome.

The biopsychosocial proposal challenged models of illness that excluded social, psychological, and behavioral dimensions.[^21-16] The World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning connects health conditions with body structures and functions, activity, participation, and environmental factors.[^21-17] Its social-determinants framework places living and working conditions, aging, and access to resources within the production of health and inequity.[^21-18] These frameworks enlarge the model's boundary. They do not assign causal weight in one person's history.

Work offers a concrete example of node entry. The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health's Total Worker Health framework includes work organization, scheduling, compensation, physical environment, technology, and policy among conditions relevant to safety and health.[^21-19] Those variables can change the external task, available assistance, exposure duration, rest, control, and access to recovery. In a cross-sectional analysis of 17,464 employed adults in the United States, physical exertion, several psychosocial factors, and measures of work organization were associated with low-back-pain prevalence after adjustment.[^21-20] Self-report, cross-sectional timing, coarse exposure measures, and residual or common-method confounding prevent causal assignment; formal interactions with physical exertion were not statistically significant. The study keeps several upstream conditions in the graph. It does not tell us which one caused an individual's pain.

Several distinctions keep this matrix from becoming a new checklist. A biological marker can change the expected tissue response without explaining a symptom. Job control can alter task options and recovery without being reducible to a mechanical quantity. Care history can change pain, belief, assistance, tissue state, and movement at once, creating a new state rather than merely revealing the old one. Culture and meaning can change which activities matter and which outcomes count as improvement. Goals and values determine decision relevance even when they do not explain biological origin.

Factors can also enter more than once. Sleep loss might change present capacity, attention, task choice, and recovery opportunity without being one homogeneous cause. A workplace policy might set repetition and pace, restrict when recovery can occur, and influence whether assistance is available. Medication can alter symptoms and activity at the same time, so a later movement measure is already post-treatment. These are not reasons to abandon a graph because reality is complicated. They are reasons to draw only the portion needed for the selected question and to mark where one factor opens several paths.

Interaction should not be assumed merely because two domains appear together. The workplace study above found physical, psychosocial, and organizational associations, but its formal interactions with physical exertion were not statistically significant. “Both belong in the graph” and “one changes the effect of the other” are different propositions. The same discipline applies to biology and mechanics: coexistence can motivate a mixed account, while a claim of synergy requires its own scale, comparator, and evidence.

The matrix is therefore not a demand to measure everything. A row belongs in an account only when it changes a defined proposition. Blank cells are preferable to generic balance. Mechanical precision and whole-person reasoning become allies when each prevents the other from carrying claims it has not established.

## Modulation does not recover origin

An intervention can supply stronger causal evidence than a cross-sectional observation, but only for the contrast it actually assigns. If changing a support, shoe, brace, movement strategy, or multicomponent program changes an outcome relative to a comparator, the result establishes modulation under those conditions. It does not automatically identify the active mechanical variable, the mediator, or the history that produced the condition.

A randomized trial assigned 164 people with medial knee osteoarthritis to unloading or conventional walking shoes. At six months, the groups did not differ in pain or function change, although both improved on several measures.[^21-21] The conclusion is not that knee mechanics are irrelevant. This unloading design did not add symptomatic benefit over the tested comparator.

An exploratory analysis of that trial sharpened the boundary. None of the proposed biomechanical mediators showed a significant mediating effect in the full cohort, while subgroup signals were underpowered and exploratory.[^21-22] Calling the intervention mechanical did not demonstrate that a measured mechanical change carried its effect. Assignment, change in the proposed mediator, symptom change, and mediation remained distinct.

A positive trial requires the same discipline. In 126 adults with painful patellofemoral osteoarthritis, six weeks of bracing reduced pain and the volume of targeted patellofemoral bone-marrow lesions; the non-targeted tibiofemoral measure did not change detectably.[^21-23] That regional result supports modifiability under the trial conditions. Pain change was not significantly associated with change in the magnetic-resonance measure, so the study did not establish that the imaging change mediated pain, recover disease origin, or supply a complete symptom mechanism.

The RESTORE trial provides a whole-person example rather than a purely mechanical one. It compared cognitive functional therapy with or without movement-sensor biofeedback against usual care in 492 adults with chronic disabling low-back pain. Both cognitive-functional-therapy groups improved activity limitation relative to usual care, while the addition of sensor feedback showed no apparent added benefit.[^21-24] The individualized, multicomponent, unblinded intervention does not isolate cognition, emotion, behavior, movement, lifestyle, or another active component, and it does not recover etiology. Nor is “no apparent added benefit” a formal equivalence result. It is a bounded finding that a technically richer measurement layer did not improve the declared outcome in that comparison.

Mediation analysis can estimate direct and indirect effects only under explicit assumptions about confounding, measurement, timing, and the relations among treatment, mediator, and outcome.[^21-25] The chapter does not require readers to perform such an analysis. It uses the logic to prevent three arrows from collapsing:

**assigned change → measured mechanical change**

**assigned change → symptom or function change**

**measured mechanical change → mediated portion of the outcome**

Even if all three were supported, none would by itself establish the historical origin of the condition. An effective way to change a present system need not be the reverse of the path by which the system arrived there.

Structural findings require the same endpoint discipline. A systematic review of thirty-three studies included imaging from 3,110 asymptomatic people and found several degenerative spine features increasingly common with age.[^21-26] This does not make the findings unreal or clinically meaningless. It prevents their presence alone from identifying a symptom source or a prior route. A finding can have high priority for describing anatomy or planning under relevant clinical conditions and low priority for explaining a selected symptom. Priority belongs to the question, not permanently to the object.

## Evidence belongs to arrows, and stopping is a result

No universal ladder can rank every form of evidence for every question. Direct measurement can be strong for the variable measured and silent about another scale. A prospective association improves temporal order but can retain residual common causes. An experiment can identify the effect of an assigned manipulation in its study system while leaving disease origin and population transfer open. Mechanistic evidence can support an intermediate relation without showing how often it matters in people. A clinical outcome can be clear while the active component remains uncertain.

Evidence becomes stronger when unlike methods make compatible predictions and their important biases are genuinely different. This is the purpose of triangulation, not the accumulation of several analyses that share the same error.[^21-27] Instrumented mechanics, prospective histories, tissue-specific measurements, perturbation studies, clinical outcomes, and qualitative experience should contribute different information. They should not be treated as interchangeable glimpses of one hidden route.

The stopping rule is the weakest consequential arrow. Suppose configuration and demand are measured well enough to estimate a local exposure, but the exposure-to-tissue-response relation is unsupported. The local mechanical conclusion can stand; the biological conclusion stops. Suppose a symptom changes after an intervention but the proposed mediator does not. Modulation can stand; the preferred mechanism stops. Suppose a model predicts an outcome accurately but acting on it adds no benefit relative to existing information. Prediction can stand; decision value stops. Decision-curve methods formalize the broader distinction between predictive performance and consequences of using a prediction.[^21-28] This chapter needs the distinction, not a required calculation.

A candidate mechanical account gains support when the proposed relation precedes the selected change, reaches the right receiver and scale, predicts an intermediate that can be measured, survives comparisons with alternative histories, and adds explanation or decision value beyond a named existing account. It narrows when it explains current modulation but not origin, one task but not another, an intermediate but not an outcome, or a reciprocal loop whose entry point remains unknown.

It should stop when the route cannot be measured or reconstructed reliably, when temporal order contradicts the proposed origin, when the variable fails to discriminate histories, when its own predicted intermediate does not change, when the endpoint changes without the proposed mediator, or when existing reasoning already captures all the useful information. “No added value for this question” is not a defeat for a mechanical science. It is how the research program avoids becoming self-sealing.

The low-priority branch must be allowed to carry real weight. Imagine that the opening movement pattern can be described in exquisite mechanical detail, but repeated measurements show that it does not distinguish people with and without the selected outcome, does not precede change, and does not improve a prediction made from information already available. The description may remain true. It may be valuable for another task or for basic biomechanics. For the declared causal or decision question, however, further detail does not rescue its priority.

Conversely, an incomplete chain need not be called a failure. If a perturbation reliably changes a receiver-level exposure but no study has yet connected that exposure to symptoms, the result can justify better tissue-response research. If a prospective history distinguishes later structural change but not present pain, the structural claim can stand while the symptom claim stops. This is the practical advantage of arrow-specific evidence: a missing later bridge does not erase an earlier result, and a strong earlier result cannot be spent twice.

The framework should therefore leave the reader with three broad movements rather than a score. Evidence can **support** a more specific mechanical relation by ordering it in time, measuring the right intermediate, and discriminating alternatives. It can **narrow** the relation to one task, phase, receiver, outcome, or reciprocal interval. Or it can **disfavor** the proposed use through failed prediction, failed mediation, contrary chronology, duplication, or irrelevance to the endpoint. Unresolved is also a result when the necessary observation does not yet exist. These outcomes make the program cumulative because each changes what should be asked next.

## A causal contract strong enough for a larger project

Return to the person who lifts more slowly, moves through a smaller range, shifts support toward the arms, reports pain, and has a common structural finding. We still do not have to choose one total story.

We can ask whether the present strategy changes a receiver-level exposure and measure that relation. We can investigate whether pain, inflammatory state, fear, fatigue, work, or care preceded the strategy. We can follow both directions through time. We can test whether the structural finding corresponds to the selected symptom or merely describes anatomy. We can decide whether a routing history adds anything beyond the account already available. Each answer can be precise without becoming the answer to every other question.

This discipline does not push mechanics to the margin. It makes stronger mechanical claims possible. A relation that survives a declared comparator, a measured intermediate, competing graphs, temporal tests, and a meaningful endpoint has earned more authority than one protected by the fact that bodies are physical. A relation that explains only one transition can remain useful. A null can redirect measurement. A low-priority result can preserve attention for an immune, infectious, genetic, metabolic, traumatic, psychological, social, or environmental process that better explains the question.

The larger ambition remains open. If longitudinal routing repeatedly adds discrimination, predicts receiver states, improves research coordination, or changes consequential decisions beyond existing approaches, it may deserve a larger place in medical education, prevention, research, and eventually practice. Book I does not assume that outcome. It builds a foundation capable of discovering it—or discovering where the proposed addition is redundant, too coarse, or wrong.

The next chapter inherits that contract. Its task is to place load history and routing inside ordinary clinical work without creating a parallel diagnosis or a mechanical-first protocol. The rule is now clear: add the account only for a named question, preserve established medical and safety processes, record what is observed and inferred, compare alternatives, and permit the inquiry to end when no further mechanical explanation is needed.
