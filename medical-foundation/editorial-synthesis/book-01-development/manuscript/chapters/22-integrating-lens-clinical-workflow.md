# Chapter 22 — Integrating the lens into clinical workflow

## The extra question comes second

Carry the previous chapter’s deliberately incomplete hypothetical observation set into an ordinary encounter. A person reports pain and reduced tolerance for a familiar lifting task. The lift is slower than it once was, the trunk moves through a smaller range, support shifts earlier toward the arms, and imaging shows a common structural change near the painful region. Relevant evaluation and care are underway, and the person has a practical goal involving the task. The observations do not establish why they coexist. This is not a disguised patient history, and no diagnosis or management decision will be supplied to complete it.

The ordinary questions come first. What problem is the person trying to solve? What is known, what remains under evaluation, and what care is already in place? How is function affected? What matters to the person? Which professional is asking which question, within what scope? A mechanical account that enters before those questions can become a parallel story about the body rather than a useful part of care.

That order is not foreign to medicine. The National Academies describes diagnosis as an iterative and collaborative process that unfolds through information gathering, interpretation, communication, and outcomes within a health-care work system.[^22-01] NICE places care decisions within collaboration between a person and a health professional, including the options and their possible benefits, risks, and consequences.[^22-02] The World Health Organization describes rehabilitation in relation to function, disability, environment, personal goals, and multidisciplinary care.[^22-03] These frameworks differ in purpose, but together they establish an important boundary: the person’s problem and the existing care context do not wait outside while a new model is applied.

Only then does this book add one question:

> Would a selective account of task, mechanical demand, distribution, and time clarify this particular problem—or would it merely restate what is already known?

The question is narrower than “What is wrong with the person’s mechanics?” It has no preferred answer. The added account might identify a present task relation worth measuring. It might expose a missing link that can be turned into a research question. It might duplicate a good rehabilitation or ergonomic history and add nothing. The last result is not failure; it is evidence about the proposed framework.

This chapter develops the reasoning architecture for that comparison. It is conceptual and unvalidated. It is not an intake form, a diagnostic method, a referral pathway, or a sequence of actions for a reader to perform. Its present role is additive because the proposed representation has not established reliability, validity, incremental value, or clinical utility. That publication posture does not place a permanent ceiling on the project. If later work establishes that the representation improves important questions or outcomes, a larger change in education or care could become justified. The first obligation is to make the proposed addition inspectable without borrowing authority from the ambition behind it.

## One declared question sets the boundary

A mechanical history can expand without limit. Almost any life contains changes in work, sleep, sport, caregiving, travel, illness, injury, equipment, environment, and activity. Recording everything would not make the account more causal. It would make it easier to select a persuasive story after the fact.

The declared question determines what belongs. If the question concerns variation during one recurring task, the record might need the task phase, external supports, duration, repetition, relevant changes through time, and the function or symptom being compared. It does not automatically need a whole-body map. If the question concerns a possible receiver exposure, the receiver and physical quantity must be named before distant history is added. If no consequential node changes when more history is supplied, the inquiry should remain small.

Established ergonomics provides a useful precedent for this selectivity. NIOSH guidance organizes occupational risk-factor inquiry around force, repetition, posture, intensity, frequency, duration, work organization, psychosocial conditions, worker reports, records, and observation of the task.[^22-04] That material is designed for hazard identification and program development, not for reconstructing one person’s internal load path. Its transferable lesson is more modest: exposure belongs to a particular task, dose, environment, and history, and several physical and contextual factors can coexist.

The routing account proposed in Chapter 18 adds a sequence of candidate objects:

- **Task and environment:** the purpose, contacts, supports, tools, assistance, schedule, and relevant history.
- **Configuration and available paths:** geometry, contact, active state, material state, and movement possibilities at a named phase.
- **External demand and internal distribution:** measured forces or moments where available, followed by calculated, modeled, or inferred sharing.
- **Local receiver exposure:** stress, strain, pressure, contact, excursion, or another tissue- or system-specific quantity.
- **Response through time:** immediate change, repetition, recovery, retained state, remodeling, or another defined response.
- **Function, finding, and symptom:** separate endpoints that may correspond, diverge, or remain unmeasured.
- **Whole-person context:** biological state, expectations, behavior, work, resources, environment, care, meaning, and goals, attached to the nodes they actually affect.

The list is not a form to complete. It is a map of possible distinctions. For the hypothetical encounter, the bounded question may require only the documented task change, a comparison of current function under two ordinary task conditions already present in the record, and the uncertainty about internal distribution. The chapter does not invent a tissue, hidden route, or initiating exposure to make the example satisfying.

One optional query is inherited from Chapter 3. Is gross demand being resolved through relatively shape-preserving support, or is a form-receiving structure sustaining deformation or substituting for support in a way that matters to the declared question? This hard/soft polarity is motivated by traditional Chinese medicine’s hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts, but the mechanical and traditional categories are not definitionally identical. The query is relational: a bone can deform, an active muscle can perform a shape-preserving role, and a vessel can bear pressure while remaining form-receiving. It is not a diagnosis or required field. Any consequential use must name the receiver, task, direction, physical quantity, sustaining relation, and evidence available. Traditional motivation neither validates nor disqualifies that mechanical question.

A history also needs dates rather than a crowded list of factors. At minimum, it distinguishes a candidate initiating or recurring exposure, an observed change, and the current state. Those moments may contain different mechanical relations. A task may initially have been performed with one strategy, later preserved through substitution, and eventually limited under a new biological or environmental condition. The present configuration cannot be projected backward across those intervals without evidence.

This is where compensation can enter without becoming a hidden diagnosis. Compensation means that the task or outcome was preserved while some combination of strategy, effort, distribution, variability, reserve, or recovery changed. It does not mean that damage silently accumulated. A changed strategy can be successful, temporary, neutral, costly, protective, or unrelated to the later concern. To make the history consequential, the record must identify what changed, when it changed, which outcome remained stable, and what later observation would distinguish an important rerouting from ordinary variation.

Recovery is equally specific. A symptom returning toward baseline, a task becoming easier, a deformation reversing, and a tissue restoring capacity are different events. The record should state which one occurred and over what time. Otherwise the word *recovery* can close a causal gap as easily as the word *load*. A rest interval may change the next exposure; medication may alter symptoms or capacity; a work change may remove a task without changing tissue state; a procedure may create an entirely new anatomical boundary. The history should preserve those possibilities rather than narrate every improvement as restoration of a preferred route.

Whole-person context becomes useful when it changes a named part of this chronology. Sleep may alter symptom sensitivity or recovery. Work organization may determine repetition and available breaks. Caregiving may constrain the choice of strategy. Expectations may alter a reported endpoint. Disease, medication, prior surgery, or disability may change material state, active control, or the task itself. These are not miscellaneous influences placed outside the mechanical account. They are possible causes of the boundary conditions, measurements, responses, and meanings that the account is trying to organize.

The entry rule can therefore be stated compactly:

> Begin from the declared problem, existing clinical and safety context, current care, function, goals, and professional scope. Add mechanical history only for a named question. Preserve alternatives and missing observations. Stop when the account cannot discriminate, changes nothing, or another concern has priority.

The order is logical, not a universal chronology for an encounter. Real conversations move back and forth among symptoms, task, diagnosis, medication, function, and goals. The controlling point is that a routing hypothesis cannot displace established obligations or gain diagnostic authority merely by appearing first in a diagram.

## A record must keep its seams

The proposed chain combines evidence from different sources. A person can report pain, effort, fatigue, ease, or a felt line of support. A clinician or researcher can observe a task. An instrument can measure motion, pressure, force, or another specified quantity. A calculation can derive a moment from declared inputs. A model can estimate an internal distribution under stated assumptions. A later interpretation can connect those elements into a possible history. They do not become one kind of evidence because they occupy adjacent rows.

The FDA–NIH BEST terminology makes an analogous distinction among biomarkers and patient-, clinician-, observer-, and performance-based outcome assessments.[^22-05] Book I does not import that regulatory taxonomy as its own measurement system. It takes the underlying discipline: an image, a person’s report, an observed movement, a performance result, and a pressure measurement answer different questions.

In the hypothetical encounter, suppose the person reports that the recurring task sometimes feels easier. That report establishes the experience and its relevance. Suppose the task is observed under a documented condition and a difference in completion time is measured. The observation and timing add new information. A calculation based on object mass and geometry might estimate an external moment. A musculoskeletal model might estimate internal sharing. An inference might propose that one receiver experienced a different exposure. Each step has its own assumptions and alternatives. The measured time does not validate the modeled distribution, and the person’s experience does not become a tissue-force measurement.

The same discipline applies to embodied language. “I feel a line of support” can be preserved as a first-person description tied to a task, side, phase, comparison, and time. It may motivate a candidate correlate. It does not by itself establish an anatomical cable, an internal force resultant, or a universal route. Treating the report at its actual resolution is neither dismissive nor credulous. It protects the observation from being forced into a claim it was never able to bear.

A reconstructed history needs particular care. *Reconstructed* describes how several dated elements have been assembled across an unobserved interval; it is not a higher evidence rank. A reconstruction can contain direct measurements, reports, and images. The sequence connecting them remains an inference unless the relevant transition was observed at the necessary times and scales. Present anatomy may retain information about prior events without uniquely revealing their order or origin.

Evidence can become stronger without changing kind. Repeating an observation can establish repeatability under the stated conditions. Independent observers can support agreement. A better instrument can reduce uncertainty in the quantity it measures. A validated model can support estimates within its tested domain. None of those improvements silently promotes a measurement of motion into stress, a model estimate into an observed route, or a present association into historical cause. The ledger should make genuine gains visible without letting them migrate across constructs.

Missing cells are therefore positive information. If external demand was measured but local exposure was not, the record should show the gap. If a present finding and a prior event are documented but the interval was not observed, the reconstruction should name compatible alternatives. If the person’s experience is clear while its tissue correlate is unknown, both facts can remain. A blank cell is not an invitation to fill the narrative with the mechanically most elegant possibility.

This is why a light provenance record matters. It should identify the source, task, time, evidence type, uncertainty, and missing interval for every consequential entry. The record need not burden ordinary prose, but the distinctions must remain recoverable. If two reviewers cannot tell which link was measured and which was inferred, the narrative is not ready to support a stronger claim.

Reliability and validity do not follow from clear documentation. The GRRAS reporting guidance emphasizes the need to define subjects, raters, measurement conditions, design, and error when agreement or reliability is studied.[^22-06] COSMIN work separates properties such as content validity, reliability, measurement error, construct validity, and responsiveness.[^22-07] Neither source validates the ledger proposed here. They show why turning the ledger into a score, classification, or clinical tool would create a separate development burden.

## Change now does not recover the past

Current change can be informative without being historical proof. A task, support, position, rest interval, device, medication, procedure, explanation, shift of attention, or therapeutic interaction can alter a symptom, a performance measure, or an observed movement. That change may identify a present relation worth retaining. It may also reflect expectation, learning, fatigue, natural fluctuation, measurement error, concurrent care, or several mechanisms at once.

Controlled experiments make the contextual branch impossible to dismiss. In healthy participants, experimentally manipulated expectations changed pain ratings and neural responses under the study conditions.[^22-08] The result does not make pain unreal and does not show that physical exposure is absent. It shows that a symptom endpoint can be modulated through a relation that is not reducible to the local mechanics under discussion.

Mechanically motivated interventions also resist simple interpretation. In a randomized trial of 164 people with knee osteoarthritis, an unloading shoe did not produce additional pain or function benefit over conventional walking shoes at six months.[^22-09] The result does not establish that knee mechanics are irrelevant. It shows that a mechanical rationale, assignment to a device, and a meaningful clinical difference are separate propositions.

An exploratory analysis of that trial separated the propositions further. The investigators found no significant mediation by the proposed biomechanical variables in the full cohort; subgroup signals were exploratory and underpowered.[^22-10] An intervention can change an intended mechanical variable without changing the selected outcome, improve an outcome without the proposed mediator explaining it, or produce mixed changes across people and time. The arrow from assignment to mechanics and the arrow from mechanics to experience require different evidence.

For the hypothetical encounter, a documented improvement under one task condition would support only the relation actually observed: under those conditions, at that time, the selected symptom or function differed. It would not establish that the condition repaired a route, revealed the initiating cause, identified a tissue, or should become a general correction. Even repeatability would strengthen the present relation without recovering the unobserved past.

A useful modulation record therefore keeps seven objects separate:

1. what changed and whether the change was assigned, naturally occurring, or already part of care;
2. the task state and comparison;
3. the symptom, function, movement, or mechanical quantity actually observed;
4. the timing and persistence of the difference;
5. concurrent care and contextual alternatives;
6. the narrow present relation supported; and
7. the origin claims that remain unresolved.

This is an interpretive grammar, not a home test or an examination maneuver. Its purpose is to prevent a compelling response from doing three jobs at once: documenting current modulation, explaining past cause, and selecting future care. One observed change can matter while the latter two questions remain open.

## Findings have local jobs

Imaging is neither a mechanical verdict nor meaningless noise. Its job depends on the condition, question, modality, protocol, and finding. The American College of Radiology’s low-back-pain criteria are organized by clinical scenario and address the appropriateness of particular imaging choices within those scenarios.[^22-11] They do not provide a general theory of load routing, but they show why “the image” cannot be separated from the question for which it was obtained.

Noncorrespondence adds another boundary. A systematic review of thirty-three studies included 3,110 asymptomatic people and found that several spinal degenerative imaging features became more common with age.[^22-12] That result does not make every finding incidental. It establishes that the presence of a structural feature does not, by itself, identify the source of a symptom or the history that produced it.

A finding can perform several different jobs:

- define anatomy or a tissue state relevant to an established diagnosis;
- answer a condition-specific procedural or clinical question;
- constrain a mechanical model without explaining a symptom;
- correspond with a selected function or experience under appropriate evidence;
- represent a downstream state whose history remains open;
- be common, incidental, or nondiscriminating for the declared endpoint; or
- remain of unresolved importance.

The job has to be stated rather than inferred from visual vividness. A scan may measure geometry but not dynamic contact, pressure, stress, strain, excursion, or cumulative dose. A structural observation can be entirely real and still fail to distinguish among several histories. Conversely, the absence of a selected routine finding does not establish that no mechanical exposure occurred. Many task-dependent or tissue-scale quantities are outside the selected image and protocol. That logical limitation cannot be reversed into proof of a hidden route.

Laboratory, examination, and functional findings follow the same rule. Each has a construct, method, time, uncertainty, and condition-specific interpretive role. A finding can alter the plausible set of histories without choosing one. A diagnosis can constrain a mechanical account without supplying its full exposure history. A symptom can be the endpoint that matters most to the person without identifying the tissue that generated it.

For the hypothetical encounter, an existing image or examination finding would be placed at the node it actually measures. The routing account could ask whether the finding changes the candidate receiver, geometry, or tissue state. It could not use the finding to complete the prior task history, and it could not reinterpret the finding outside the expertise and criteria that give it clinical meaning.

## The surrounding fields are not empty

The proposed lens enters mature and overlapping domains. World Physiotherapy describes physiotherapy in relation to movement and function across promotion, prevention, treatment, and rehabilitation, with physical, psychological, emotional, and social dimensions.[^22-13] American Physical Therapy Association materials describe movement as complex and contextual and place history, systems review, movement, function, cognition, affect, communication, collaboration, and referral within physical-therapy examination and evaluation.[^22-14][^22-15] WHO rehabilitation similarly places function, environment, goals, and multidisciplinary care within an established field.[^22-03]

Occupational therapy and ergonomics occupy more of the same landscape. AOTA’s professional materials include daily activities, participation, work, environmental and personal context, performance, goals, and adaptation.[^22-16] The International Ergonomics Association defines ergonomics through interactions among people and other elements of systems and through the design of tasks, jobs, tools, products, environments, and organizations.[^22-17] The proposed framework therefore cannot claim novelty from asking about movement, activity, work, environment, or task demand.

Medicine, imaging, pharmacology, surgery, rehabilitation, physical therapy, occupational therapy, ergonomics, psychology, pain care, and the person and family each contribute different observations and decisions. The mechanical record is not a territory placed between them. It is a candidate representation through which selected information might be connected across time without erasing the domain in which it was produced.

The interfaces matter because care changes the system being described. A diagnosis can identify a tissue state or disease process that constrains which mechanical histories remain plausible. Medication can change inflammation, pain, alertness, muscle activity, or tolerance without being reducible to a mechanical node. Surgery or another procedure can alter anatomy, contact, stability, pressure, or available paths. A device can redistribute external demand while also changing behavior and expectation. Rehabilitation can change capacity, task strategy, confidence, participation, and environment. Work redesign can remove or transform the exposure itself.

Each change needs a date and an evidence state. A postoperative image does not recover preoperative distribution. Improvement after rehabilitation does not prove which proposed mechanism produced the improvement. A medication-associated change does not make the prior mechanical observation irrelevant. The routing record’s legitimate contribution is to preserve how the boundary conditions changed and which questions remain, not to redescribe every intervention as mechanics.

Handoffs also create translation risk. A radiology report, patient-reported outcome, gait measure, workplace observation, surgical description, and embodied account may all refer to the same period while representing different objects. A shared graph can place them in relation only if their original meanings survive. “Load,” “restriction,” “instability,” “tension,” and “response” cannot be assumed to mean the same thing across professions. Integration begins by retaining the source language and specifying the proposed connection, not by replacing every term with the book’s vocabulary.

The World Health Organization’s interprofessional framework treats collaborative practice as a health-system strategy rather than the adoption of one profession’s vocabulary by all others.[^22-18] A routing record should therefore be judged partly by whether it improves communication without making its own terms prerequisites for competent care.

“This is just physical therapy” is not answered by asserting that it is or is not. At least four results are possible. Existing physical therapy or rehabilitation may already perform the entire useful job. The substantive work may already exist while this project contributes a clearer cross-disciplinary vocabulary. The longitudinal record may preserve a chronology, scale transition, or receiver-specific relation that is not reliably represented in the comparator. Or the project may propose a distinctive hypothesis that remains unobservable or invalid.

Those possibilities can be compared. Can independent reviewers reconstruct the same relation from the same information? Does the representation distinguish candidate histories better than ordinary documentation? Does it add a measurable quantity or only new language? Does it improve communication, research design, prediction, or a defined decision? What burden does it add? Does a simpler existing framework produce the same result?

The answers could range from full duplication to a substantial contribution. Book I does not decide the range by rhetoric. Its broader ambition—to make biomechanical history more consequential within medicine—remains active, but the ambition defines a research program rather than its result. Current complementarity protects both existing care and the integrity of that larger test.

## A stop is an informative result

A coherent representation is not ready for use merely because its fields can be named. If the proposed record ever becomes a professional instrument, it will need staged development. The updated Medical Research Council framework for complex interventions emphasizes context, program theory, stakeholder perspectives, key uncertainties, feasibility, evaluation, implementation, comparative resources and outcomes, refinement, and the legitimate possibility of stopping.[^22-19] Reporting and measurement frameworks add different obligations: GRRAS for reliability and agreement, COSMIN for measurement properties, and STARD for diagnostic-accuracy studies if a later artifact ever makes such claims.[^22-06][^22-07][^22-20]

The stages should not be collapsed. First comes the construct: what exactly is a routing history, and for which question? Then content: do the affected people and relevant disciplines agree that the representation contains what matters? Feasibility asks whether it can be completed accurately without displacing more important work. Reliability asks whether observations or reconstructions agree where they should. Validity asks whether fields correspond to the quantities they claim to represent. Discrimination asks whether competing histories generate different observations. Incremental value compares the record with an explicit alternative. Utility asks whether its use improves a meaningful process or outcome without unacceptable burden, inequity, privacy loss, or harm. Governance asks who may use it, for what purpose, and with what accountability.

The word *incremental* describes the comparison, not the size of the project’s aspiration. A representation adds value only relative to something else: existing documentation, a rehabilitation history, an ergonomic analysis, a local biomechanical model, or no additional record. The comparator has to be named before the result is known. Otherwise every extra detail can be celebrated as a contribution even when it changes no interpretation, prediction, communication, or outcome.

The outcome also has to be named in advance. Agreement may matter for research even if no care decision changes. Better communication may matter even if prediction does not improve. A more discriminating history may justify a measurement study without justifying clinical deployment. Conversely, a representation could be reliable yet systematically wrong, valid for one task yet too burdensome for routine use, or informative in a research setting while inequitable in a setting where time and instrumentation are scarce. “Useful” cannot remain one undifferentiated endpoint.

This comparison should include the cost of attention. A long mechanical record can crowd out the person’s priorities, overfit a fluctuating history, create false confidence, or redirect resources from better-established work. It can also preserve chronology that would otherwise be lost. Both possibilities belong in the same evaluation. The framework does not earn practical relevance by containing more information; it earns it by improving a declared question under acceptable tradeoffs.

Book I is at the construct and narrative-record stage. It cannot borrow the authority of later stages. A transparent table is not a validated form. Agreement is not validity. Validity is not incremental value. Predictive performance is not automatically beneficial decision consequence. Decision-curve analysis was developed precisely to distinguish prediction from the consequences of acting across specified thresholds, although this chapter does not propose that method for the routing record.[^22-21]

Three outputs are therefore legitimate for the hypothetical encounter.

**A bounded present relation.** A documented task feature and an observed or measured output correspond under stated conditions. The relation is worth preserving. Origin, tissue state, and care implications remain open.

**A useful but incomplete history.** Chronology and task changes support a candidate exposure sequence, but a receiver quantity or biological bridge is missing. The result is a researchable hypothesis, not a clinical conclusion.

**No added value.** The reconstruction is nonunique, duplicates the existing account, cannot be measured, adds unacceptable burden, or changes no meaningful question. The mechanical extension stops while the ordinary care context and the person’s goals remain intact.

Stopping here is a validity rule for the representation, not advice to a patient and not a referral criterion. The added inquiry should also narrow or stop when one image, posture, response, or report is carrying the entire causal story; when the proposed internal distribution cannot be distinguished; when missing intervals invite retrospective invention; when another concern has priority; or when the question belongs to research rather than the present context.

Negative results refine the project. If reviewers cannot agree on consequential entries, if simpler documentation produces the same conclusion, or if the record changes attention without improving a meaningful outcome, current use is disfavored. Such findings do not automatically refute every later preferred-configuration, traditional-coordinate, breath, or practice hypothesis. They do show that those later programs cannot inherit clinical utility from this conceptual record.

## The record the worked narratives inherit

Part V needs a common structure because a persuasive story can hide different missing links in each example. The shared record is an explanatory and research scaffold. Readers are not asked to complete it for themselves, and clinicians are not given a new checklist. Its job is to expose what each narrative knows, reconstructs, compares, and leaves blank.

The record is deliberately more exacting than the prose that surrounds it. A chapter can read smoothly while moving from an observed posture to an inferred distribution, from that distribution to an unmeasured strain, and from strain to a symptom. The table forces each transition into its own row. It also prevents the examples from changing standards: a missing biological bridge in one narrative cannot be excused merely because a different chapter supplied a compelling clinical endpoint.

The null row matters as much as the proposed chain. Each narrative must preserve ordinary variation, recovery, reverse direction, another primary account, and the possibility that the routing lens adds little. The discriminator row then asks what observation would separate the leading histories. When no feasible discriminator exists, that limitation is part of the result rather than a reason to strengthen the prose.

The chapter began with a task-limited concern whose variation did not establish an explanation. The load-routing lens earns a place only if it improves that defined question while preserving the existing clinical frame. It can add chronology without pretending the past was observed, place measurements beside reports without making them interchangeable, and connect professions without claiming their territory. It can also conclude that the present account is sufficient without it.

That is the chapter’s answer. Mechanical history belongs in clinical reasoning as structured context and as a candidate research object. It does not arrive as a second diagnosis. The current framework can organize observations, propose links, identify missing measurements, and stop. Later evidence determines whether it remains a useful vocabulary, becomes a reliable professional representation, supports a larger reform, or proves unnecessary.

Part V now subjects the method to four different mechanical narratives. Chapter 23 asks whether a change in geometry can be followed through moment arms, contact, and local strain without converting strain into injury or a finding into a symptom. Chapter 24 changes the receiver to a compliant corridor. Chapters 25 and 26 add repeated instability and mixed chronic compensation. Each must use the same record, including its blank cells and no-added-value branch. If the method survives only by changing the rules between examples, Chapter 22 has not supplied a framework at all.
