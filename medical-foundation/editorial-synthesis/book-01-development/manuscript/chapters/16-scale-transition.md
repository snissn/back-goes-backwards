# Chapter 16 — Scale transition and possible regulatory consequences

One way to follow a compensation is to ask where its mechanical effects appear next. Consider a laboratory manipulation of walking. Investigators restricted extension of one knee in seventeen healthy adult men, recorded motion and ground reaction forces, and entered those measurements into a musculoskeletal model of the lumbar region. Under the restricted conditions, the participants walked more slowly and changed their trunk alignment. In the condition with the greater nominal restriction, the model estimated a larger lumbar extension moment during defined phases of gait; selected modeled paraspinal forces and components of the L4–L5 joint reaction force also increased across comparisons.[^16-01]

The experiment provides a clear transition, but not a simple chain. A condition imposed at the knee was followed by changes in the gait task and by modeled changes at the lumbar region. The brace did not transmit one identifiable force directly from knee to spine. Speed, segment motion, ground contact, active control, and the assumptions of the lumbar model all participated. Nor did the estimated increase establish overload, damage, or a naturally occurring history. The result belongs to the changed walking solution.

A second restriction experiment prevents that result from becoming a rule. Eight adults without reported gait impairment walked with unilateral and bilateral restrictions at the ankle, knee, or both. Some conditions changed measured ground reaction force peaks and loading rates. Yet participant-scaled models estimated relatively similar lower-limb joint reaction forces across many comparisons because modeled muscle forces changed during loading response.[^16-02] A visible redistribution and a changed external measure did not dictate the proposed internal exposure.

These experiments are not contradictory. They used different restrictions, models, regions, and outputs. Together they establish the chapter's central proposition: a mechanical change can cross from one region or level of description to another, but its destination, magnitude, and meaning cannot be read from the initiating change alone. Sometimes another region participates differently. Sometimes active redistribution keeps a selected internal result similar. Sometimes the important change occurs in a quantity that was not measured.

The previous chapter followed compensatory solutions through time. A successful strategy could persist, resolve, transfer, or become the starting condition for another task. This chapter follows such organization across scale. It asks how a local relation can recruit a region, how several regional strategies can form a repeatable whole-task organization, and how that organization can focus demand back onto a local receiver. It then stops the mechanical argument before sensation, flow, tissue response, or energetic demand are allowed to merge into one systemic conclusion.

## Scale is not a zoom control

The word *scale* can suggest that the same object merely becomes larger or smaller. That is not what happens here. A local field, a regional distribution, and a task-level result require different information.

At a local scale, the object might be strain within a declared tissue volume, pressure at an interface, excursion of a nerve relative to its bed, or contact stress over a surface. At a regional scale, the record might contain segment motion, ground contact, net joint moments, muscle activation, or modeled force sharing. At the task scale, it might contain walking speed, object control, support geometry, external work, or whole-body angular momentum. A whole-person record adds goals, fatigue, pain, attention, assistance, environment, and other circumstances that can alter the task without being reducible to one mechanical variable.

Moving between these levels requires a bridge. A task-level result does not contain a local stress field waiting to be magnified. A local measurement does not contain the organization of the whole task waiting to be enlarged. Geometry, contacts, forces, moments, activation, pressure, material behavior, and time connect the levels, and the necessary set depends on the question.

This point changes the familiar phrase *kinetic chain*. The phrase can be a useful prompt to look beyond one joint. It is not an explanation until the intermediate relations are named. If a knee condition is proposed to change a lumbar exposure, the account must identify the altered task, regional motion and forces, modeled or measured lumbar quantity, relevant phase, and plausible alternatives. If the selected lumbar quantity remains unchanged, the chain has not merely found a different hidden route; that null is part of the result.

Direction also remains open. A local pain state, restriction, or contact can precede a regional response. A common task change can alter both regions. A global support condition can change what is demanded locally. A later symptom can change movement and make the observed mechanical pattern a consequence rather than a cause. The scale map is therefore bidirectional from the start.

A useful transition record contains four entries:

1. the object and quantity at the starting level;
2. the intermediate contacts, motions, forces, moments, activations, or pressures;
3. the object and quantity at the receiving level; and
4. the task, chronology, alternatives, and null result that bound the relation.

Without those entries, connection is anatomical or visual. With them, a proposed transition can be measured, modeled, compared, or disfavored.

## Regional participation can become whole-task organization

Walking makes multiregion organization unusually visible. Arms, trunk, legs, ground contacts, speed, and external support can be manipulated separately, while task-level results can be calculated from their combined motion.

In one experiment, sixteen healthy young adults walked on a treadmill while following unilateral and bilateral arm-swing instructions. The manipulations changed the amplitude of the opposite arm and altered a phase-coordination measure of the lower limbs.[^16-03] This is evidence of interlimb coupling under the tested instructions. It does not reveal a unique carrier. Spinal mechanics, active control, balance, attention, and the novelty of the instruction remain possible participants.

A split-belt experiment makes the task level more explicit. Twenty-four healthy young adults walked with the treadmill belts moving at different speeds, either while holding a handrail or with their arms free. The initial asymmetry changed whole-body angular momentum through both the momenta of individual segments and the degree to which those segmental contributions canceled. Handrail contact reduced the early perturbation, changed the relation between whole-body angular momentum and step-length asymmetry, and produced a smaller early aftereffect.[^16-04]

The handrail did not simply add an abstract quantity called stability. It changed the boundary of the working system. The person, rail, belts, and contacts together produced a different task. Segmental behavior, aggregate momentum, and adaptation all changed, but none can stand in for the others.

Whole-body angular momentum is especially instructive because it is an aggregate. It can show that segmental contributions combine into a task-level quantity. It cannot uniquely recover the organization that produced that quantity. Different combinations can yield similar totals, and a similar-looking movement can conceal different internal contributions.

Arm-swing experiments also separate mechanical aggregation from energetic outcome. Twelve healthy adults walked under in-phase, restricted, ordinary, and several exaggerated arm-swing conditions. Increasing arm-swing amplitude generally reduced vertical angular momentum and ground reaction moment until the most exaggerated condition. Metabolic cost did not follow those mechanical measures indefinitely; the greatest swing raised rather than further reduced cost of transport.[^16-05] The task-level mechanical result and the metabolic result were related, but they were not one variable.

For this book, *whole-task organization* means a repeatable relation among declared regions, contacts, forces, timing, and task outcomes over a stated interval. It does not mean that every tissue shares one state. It does not require bilateral symmetry, visual straightness, low movement, unsupported performance, or one origin. A repeatable organization can be learned, assisted, protective, trained, disability-specific, culturally practiced, comfortable, temporary, or imposed by the environment.

The word *repeatable* also needs a boundary. A relation observed for ten laboratory trials is not a permanent bodily baseline. A pattern that transfers across speeds may not transfer across terrain. A strategy used during pain may resolve when pain changes. A durable account requires repeated measurement across the times, tasks, and environments for which durability is claimed.

## The arrow also runs back toward the local

Once several regions participate in a whole task, their organization can alter a local exposure in return. That statement is mechanical, but it is not automatic. The task-level description must pass through the same bridge developed earlier in this Part:

```text
task and environment
    -> body configuration, contacts, and active state
    -> regional forces, moments, pressure, and support relations
    -> interface sharing and local boundary conditions
    -> local stress, strain, pressure, activation, contact, or excursion
```

Suppose a repeated task changes support from two contacts to one, adds a handrail, redistributes mass, or alters the timing of a trunk segment. Any of those changes can modify regional resultants and interface conditions. A selected local receiver may then experience a different moment, pressure, strain, or excursion. But the visible configuration is not itself that exposure. Active force, a device, pressure-mediated support, or another contact can redistribute demand before it reaches the proposed receiver.

The knee-restriction experiment contains both directions in one sequence. A local experimental condition altered the gait organization; the resulting gait organization altered modeled lumbar quantities. The split-belt experiment likewise began with an environmental asymmetry, recruited several segments into a task-level response, and then expressed adaptation through individual steps. Local-to-global and global-to-local are not rival doctrines. They can be consecutive portions of one feedback history.

The null remains equally important. A visible whole-body change may leave the selected local exposure materially similar. It may change a different receiver. Or one common instruction, speed, pain state, or environment may produce both the global pattern and the local measurement without one causing the other. These possibilities belong beside the proposed arrow, not in a disclaimer at the end.

## A whole-body transition remains a research question

The multiscale map preserves a stronger possibility. Some bodily histories may involve more than an isolated change superimposed on an otherwise unchanged task. Repeated relations among contacts, timing, active support, and regional distribution may reorganize together, so that the next task begins from a meaningfully different multiregion state.

That possibility should be stated directly. It should not be settled by appearance. A lateral displacement, rotation, asymmetry, straight spine, or coherent silhouette does not reveal a force, moment, pressure, stress, or strain field. Nor does a smooth anatomical continuum prove that one mechanical relation propagated through the whole body.

A research program on *configuration transition* would first have to define the representation. Is the proposed object a set of regional resultants, a distribution of local strains, a network of contacts and active tensions, or a combination? It would then need criteria that distinguish ordinary movement within a capable repertoire from reorganization of that repertoire. Candidates might include a persistent change in cross-region coupling, loss or gain of available routes, a sharp gradient or reversal in a declared field, altered response to perturbation, or transfer of the new organization across tasks. Each candidate requires an observation, reference state, timescale, and failure condition.

A hypothetical longitudinal experiment shows what those requirements would add. Investigators could select one stepping task, vary its support conditions, and record contacts, ground forces, segment motion, activation, and a declared local receiver before, during, and after repeated exposure. The transition proposal would have to predict more than a retained pose. It might predict that a particular cross-region relation persists after the added support is removed, appears under a second task, and changes the measured or modeled exposure at the selected receiver. Ordinary variation within the existing repertoire would predict a return toward the earlier relations, no transfer, or no corresponding change at the receiver. A result would count against the proposal if the visible pattern remained but the declared coupling did not, if the coupling changed without the proposed local effect, or if both followed a common change in speed or instruction. Even a positive result would establish only a bounded transition in the tested participants and tasks. It would not identify a universal preferred state or establish a health consequence.

Longitudinal measurement is essential because a single image cannot show transition. The investigation would need to observe the earlier organization, the changing interval, retention or resolution, and competing causes such as development, training, injury, pain, disease, assistance, environment, and preference. A transition could be beneficial, neutral, costly, mixed, or irrelevant to the outcome being studied.

Book I does not need this stronger hypothesis in order for the multiscale result to stand. Regional mechanics can couple into task organization, and task organization can return demand toward local receivers, even if no general class of configuration transitions is ever established. Keeping the question non-load-bearing is what allows it to remain ambitious and testable.

## Sensation and control form one branch

Mechanical reorganization does not become sensory reorganization merely because both involve the same person. The sensory and control branch begins with defined objects: muscle activation, perceived effort, movement detection, position sense, interoceptive report, peripheral nerve behavior, or anticipatory and corrective action. These measures are not interchangeable.

Small contacts can affect control without carrying much structural support. In ten healthy adults standing with eyes closed, a haptic robot supplied approximately 1 N of velocity-dependent touch at the upper back. The contact reduced selected center-of-pressure sway measures, while added position-dependent vibration affected some outcomes differently.[^16-06] The result supports a task-specific response to a small information-bearing contact. It does not isolate a purely sensory carrier, because the contact force and the robot's mechanical behavior remained part of the condition.

Experimental pain supplies both a positive and a null. In thumb experiments completed by ten to twelve participants per condition, local muscle or skin pain impaired passive-movement detection at some anatomically relevant sites, while several control comparisons remained unchanged.[^16-07] In a separate experiment, twenty-eight healthy participants developed sustained forearm pain and hyperalgesia after nerve growth factor injections, yet the measured wrist joint-position error did not change over the observed days.[^16-08]

The correct conclusion is not that pain does or does not disrupt proprioception. It is that a local state can alter one selected sensory measure while another measure remains unchanged. Movement detection, position matching, effort, and protective action are different outputs. Pain may precede the sensory change. Attention or expectation may change control. Inflammation, medication, instruction, learning, or measurement error may affect both.

The project therefore retains a possible sequence in which a local mechanical state changes sensory information, altered information changes control, and control changes later mechanics. Every arrow remains open to a null or reverse result. No source here establishes a progression from strain to sensory uncertainty, chronic bracing, autonomic change, or illness. The branch is worth testing precisely because it has been divided into observations that can fail independently.

## Pressure, flow, and excursion divide into different objects

The next branch splits before it begins. Arteries, veins, lymphatic vessels, peripheral nerves, sliding interfaces, and pressure-bearing cavities do not share one outcome called flow. Their walls, contents, constraints, active behaviors, valves, pressure relations, and required motions differ.

Positional vascular findings demonstrate the problem. In nineteen healthy participants assessed bilaterally across seven arm positions, Doppler waveforms met the study's criterion for significant arterial compression in at least one position in 28.9 percent of participants. Peak systolic velocity and systolic rise time varied and did not correlate with that classification. Used as an isolated diagnostic result, the positional test would have produced many false-positive inferences about arterial thoracic outlet syndrome.[^16-09]

In forty-nine other healthy volunteers, a specified abducted, flexed, and externally rotated arm position increased the average measured cross-sectional area of the subclavian vein rather than narrowing it.[^16-10] That study addressed ultrasound visualization. It did not measure a complete venous-outflow result, symptoms, or benefit. The two vascular studies cannot be pooled into the claim that arm position either opens or closes the thoracic outlet. They measured different vessels, conditions, and outputs.

Peripheral nerve mechanics is different again. Ultrasound in healthy participants measured median nerve excursion during wrist, elbow, shoulder, and neck movements. Reported excursions ranged from 0.3 mm during contralateral neck side flexion at one observed location to 10.4 mm during elbow extension, with maneuver-specific estimates of additional forearm strain. The investigators judged the tested extended-limb strain likely below levels expected to impair nerve blood flow or conduction.[^16-11] Excursion is an ordinary function of the nerve in its moving bed; its presence is not evidence of entrapment, and its loss cannot be inferred from a symptom alone.

The causal order matters. A compliant tube that remains deformed may be held by external contact, surrounding geometry, wall tension, pressure difference, active tissue, or a combination. The deformation may alter lumen geometry; lumen and gradient may alter flow; flow may or may not alter a tissue outcome. The maintained stress and strain relation is not interchangeable with the narrower space it produces. At the same time, a hose analogy cannot supply vessel biology, active regulation, valves, branching geometry, or clinical meaning.

> **Text alternative.** Seven rows separate sensory and control, arterial or venous, lymphatic, peripheral nerve, sliding interface or cavity, tissue response, and energetic or regulatory questions. Each row names a possible mechanical input, a branch-specific intermediate measurement, a candidate output, and a null or alternative. No row connects automatically to another.

The lymphatic, sliding-interface, and cavity rows in the figure are research obligations, not conclusions drawn from the arterial, venous, or nerve studies. Evidence about one conduit cannot be lent to another. Evidence about a nerve cannot establish vessel flow. A pressure change does not by itself establish improved circulation, and altered excursion does not by itself establish a symptom.

Separating these branches does not claim that they are biologically isolated. It prevents evidence from one branch from doing unearned work for another. A sensory response could change muscle activity; muscle activity could affect a local pressure relation; that relation could coincide with a vessel-specific measurement. Demonstrating such an interaction would require the proposed quantities to be measured on compatible timescales, their order to be established, and alternatives such as a shared task change to be compared. The interaction is a larger claim than either observation alone.

The separation also preserves informative disagreement. A task may change perceived effort while leaving a selected vascular measure similar. A positional change may alter a waveform without changing symptoms or control. A local tissue response may occur without a detectable change in whole-task cost. Such combinations do not make the map fail. They identify which branches participated and which did not. Integration becomes stronger after the branches have earned their own measurements, chronology, and null results.

## Response and cost need their own denominators

Tissue response begins only after a receiver and exposure history have been identified. Sensitization, inflammatory signaling, remodeling, repair, fatigue, wear, degeneration, and injury are different possible responses. They occur over different times and depend on material state, biology, capacity, and recovery. A mechanical change can occur without any of them. A biological change can also precede and alter motion, activation, material behavior, and local exposure.

The responsible handoff is therefore explicit:

```text
named receiver and exposure through time
    -> measured material, cellular, or signaling intermediate
    -> time-resolved tissue response
    -> functional or clinical outcome, if supported

coequal branches:
    no measurable response / useful response / recovery /
    biology changes mechanics first / exposure was misestimated
```

This chapter does not fill those arrows with a general inflammation story. Their evidence depends on tissue, dose, state, and time. The later mechanobiology discussion will have to earn each transition independently.

Energetic demand is a separate and more directly measured branch in the experiments available here. Even so, *cost* is incomplete without a numerator, denominator, task, and interval. It may mean muscle activation over several strides, positive mechanical work by a segment, net metabolic power at a stated speed, oxygen cost per distance, perceived effort, recovery time, attentional demand, or loss of task options. A strategy can improve one and worsen another.

The arm-swing experiment already showed that reducing selected angular-momentum measures did not guarantee a lower metabolic cost at the greatest tested amplitude.[^16-05] External assistance supplies a beneficial branch. Seven participants walked at 1.5 m/s with a 6.8 kg rucksack while a multi-joint soft exosuit used person-specific control parameters. Powered individualized assistance reduced measured metabolic cost relative to both no-device walking and the unpowered device condition.[^16-12] The experiment was small, and it did not isolate individualized tuning from powered assistance. It nevertheless demonstrates that an altered route including technology can lower a defined cost.

Assistance can also reverse from helpful to costly within tested conditions. Twelve healthy adult men walked with an energy-removing exoskeleton under several assistance magnitudes. Moderate assistance reduced metabolic cost by 3.4 percent relative to ordinary walking and reduced measured hamstring activity. Under the higher tested magnitude, metabolic cost exceeded ordinary walking and selected muscle activity rose relative to lower-assistance comparisons.[^16-13] This is a task-, device-, timing-, magnitude-, and sample-specific reversal, not a universal threshold.

These findings block two shortcuts. A mechanically altered organization is not inherently inefficient, and a greater amount of assistance is not inherently better. *Reserve* likewise cannot be a hidden energy store. It can describe remaining ability to repeat, vary, recover, or respond to perturbation for a declared task, provided those abilities are actually measured. Fatigue, low resilience, and diffuse symptoms may motivate such measurement; they do not establish a mechanical cause.

The term *regulatory* should be treated with the same discipline. A candidate regulatory claim must name the variable—heart rate variability, blood pressure, ventilatory work, endocrine response, temperature control, attention, or another measure—and then establish its own mechanical and physiological bridge. Activation, effort, or posture does not become autonomic dysfunction through vocabulary.

## One map, not one cascade

Part III began by asking how living structure manages demand within changing capacity. It developed continuity and handoff, geometric concentration, maintained constraint, instability and discoordination, and compensatory rerouting. The final addition is scale: local, regional, and whole-task organization can interact in both directions.

That conclusion is affirmative. A selected change can recruit another region. Several regions can combine into a measurable task-level organization. Environmental support can become part of that organization. The resulting distribution can alter a named local exposure. None of those statements requires one lesion, one visible pattern, or one universal route.

The larger configuration-transition question also survives. A body may reorganize in a way that deserves multiregion and longitudinal description rather than a list of isolated local changes. Whether such transitions form a general class, how they should be represented, and whether any one is beneficial or adverse remain open. The present chapter does not hide that program, and it does not use the program to prove itself.

Beyond mechanics, there is no single next arrow. Sensation and control, pressure and flow, nerve or interface excursion, tissue response, and energetic or regulatory demand each require their own system, intermediate measure, chronology, alternative, and null. One branch may be important while another is unchanged. A mechanical pattern may be coherent and have no meaningful physiological or medical consequence.

That is where Part III should end: not with a verdict about health, but with a map of questions that can now be asked precisely. Part IV begins by examining situations in which medicine already measures or changes mechanical conditions. The immediate question is not whether mechanics belongs in medicine. It is what established endpoint reasoning can tell us—and what, if anything, a longitudinal account of routing adds.
