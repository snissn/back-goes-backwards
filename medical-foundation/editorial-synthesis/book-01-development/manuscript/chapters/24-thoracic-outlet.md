# Chapter 24 — Thoracic outlet: a form-receiving structure held under constraint

## A chain with a missing middle

The following account is a source-grounded teaching composite. Its central anatomy and findings come from a published arterial case; its population boundary and counterexamples come from reporting standards and specialist cohorts. The layers are combined to make the reasoning visible. They are not the complete history of one person, and they should not be read as a typical presentation of thoracic outlet syndrome.

The central record concerns a man in his twenties who had experienced increasing coldness in his right forearm and fingers for about six months. His measured systolic pressure was lower in the right arm than in the left. Radiography showed unusual rib-like structures on both sides. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography showed a much more specific right-sided sequence: a focal relation created by abnormal fusion of the first and second ribs, stenosis and post-stenotic dilation of the adjacent subclavian artery, a filling defect interpreted as local thrombus, and an occlusion extending from the distal axillary artery toward the brachial artery. Collateral vessels maintained some blood supply to the forearm.[^24-01]

The observations are striking because several links appear in one record. A relatively shape-preserving bony relation lies beside an artery with stenosis, post-stenotic dilation, and a luminal filling defect interpreted as thrombus. Those local abnormalities lie upstream of a distal occlusion. An objective side-to-side pressure difference accompanies the anatomical and vascular findings. This is far more informative than an image of a narrow corridor or a report that a pulse changed with arm position.

Yet the record has a missing middle. It did not watch the arterial wall change over months or years. It did not measure contact pressure at the rib–artery interface during the tasks that preceded the presentation. It did not establish the number, direction, duration, or recovery interval of prior exposures. Even the same-person comparison has a limit: the bony variation was bilateral, but technical factors prevented a detailed evaluation of the left subclavian vessels. The report documents focal anatomy, current arterial and luminal abnormalities, and a distal consequence. It does not directly observe wall injury or the history by which one state became the next; those relations must be reconstructed.

That distinction is the work of this chapter. A mechanically intelligible sequence can be strong without being complete. The useful question is not whether “the outlet was narrow.” It is whether a defined surrounding relation imposed a consequential demand on a particular structure, whether that demand altered the structure’s material state, and whether the altered state changed a separately measured function. Thoracic outlet pathology makes those questions unusually concrete—and therefore especially easy to overgeneralize beyond the record.

## One region, several systems

*Thoracic outlet syndrome* is an umbrella name, not a single mechanical object. Society for Vascular Surgery reporting standards distinguish neurogenic, venous, and arterial entities according to whether the brachial plexus, subclavian vein, or subclavian artery is at issue. The entities can coexist, but evidence for one does not establish another.[^24-02] This chapter therefore narrows its principal system to the right subclavian artery adjacent to the focal first-rib relation in the composite, the artery’s wall and lumen, the distal arterial tree, and the time over which the present lesion could have developed.

The principal example is narrower still. It concerns objective arterial pathology and an objective distal arterial consequence. A pulse change, transient waveform change, arm discomfort, or positional reduction in caliber is not enough to enter this category. That boundary matters because it makes the example unusually legible, but it also enriches the evidence by definition: cases enter specialist series because objective arterial disease has already been identified and attributed to a focal external relation. The series are then enriched again by referral and operative selection. Their strong anatomy–lesion correspondence cannot be read as an estimate of what bony variation means in an unselected population.

Two large-for-this-rare-condition retrospective series make the selection visible. One thirty-year operative series defined arterial thoracic outlet syndrome as subclavian pathology caused by extrinsic compression from a bony abnormality and included forty-one people and forty-five treated limbs. Thirteen limbs presented with acute limb ischemia, and eight of those experienced recurrent thromboembolic events before delayed outlet surgery.[^24-03] A separate high-volume referral series included forty people who underwent surgery. Thirty had cervical ribs, five had first-rib abnormalities, four had clavicular fractures, and one was a reoperation without a remaining bony abnormality. More than half had ischemic or embolic presentations, but seventeen had nonvascular presentations; six of those involved an asymptomatic neck mass or incidentally discovered subclavian dilation.[^24-04]

These cohorts show that focal bone and objective arterial pathology recur together in selected specialist practice. They also show why neither symptoms nor anatomy can stand alone. An arterial lesion may be discovered without an ischemic presentation. A bony feature may exist without a documented arterial lesion. A symptom may have another source. The right unit of analysis is the whole bounded chain, with the evidence state of each link kept visible.

It is tempting to imagine the thoracic outlet as one empty passage whose size can be read from a scan. Anatomically, the broad region contains the scalene triangle, costoclavicular space, and subcoracoid or pectoralis-minor space, each with different boundaries and contents.[^24-15] Arm position can change several of those measured relations at once. A measurement at one level, in one position, and at one phase therefore does not describe the whole route.

The word *corridor* is useful only if it preserves those relations. A corridor model should say which structure is passing, between which boundaries, in which direction, during which task, and over what time. Its relevant output might be minimum distance, cross-sectional shape, course curvature, contact, or excursion; those are not interchangeable. Two people can have the same minimum distance but different wall contact because the artery’s course, axial condition, material state, or surrounding support differs. One person can have a similar static geometry on two sides but a different focal relation, as the composite demonstrates.

Position-dependent imaging makes this relational character visible. In the magnetic-resonance comparison introduced later in the chapter, arm position changed several measured regional dimensions and the apparent compression of vascular structures, but venous findings overlapped substantially between symptomatic participants and asymptomatic volunteers.[^24-09] The study did not measure local stress or reconstruct a cumulative dose. It shows that the state being imaged depends on the measurement task and that geometry alone remains a low-specificity observation.

The 2026 arterial record adds another lesson about representation. Routine axial images suggested a relation involving the anterior scalene, but three-dimensional reconstruction exposed the focal first- and second-rib fusion adjacent to the artery.[^24-01] The additional representation did not create new anatomy; it made a spatial relation easier to inspect. Even then, the reconstruction established current co-location and deformation, not the history of force at the interface.

A useful corridor description therefore includes a reference state. In the present composite, the source acquired the consequential findings with the upper limbs adducted. That fact belongs to the measurement record, but it should not become the universal position in which the lesion was caused. The initiating exposures could have occurred under other tasks and states. Without a measured history, the arm position at imaging is an acquisition condition, not a dose reconstruction.

This distinction is especially important when a diagram appears cleaner than the body. A useful schematic removes irrelevant anatomy so that an argument can be inspected. It must not turn that simplification into a claim that one static distance controls a heterogeneous clinical region. The boundary in Figure 24.1 is narrow by design: it represents the source-grounded composite, not “the thoracic outlet” in general.

## Imposed demand comes before loss of space

Chapter 13 defined hard and soft as relative mechanical roles. In the composite's focal relation, the anomalous rib assembly is the candidate shape-preserving structure and the artery is the candidate form-receiving structure. The artery contains pressurized blood and conveys pulsatile flow while its wall sustains pressure-derived stress. Its wall is nonlinear and anisotropic, is loaded circumferentially and axially, and includes active smooth-muscle as well as passive material contributions.[^24-16] Calling this a soft role therefore does not call the artery passive, fragile, or unloaded.

The project's shorthand—load wants to travel through the hard body so that the soft body can remain soft—poses a specific question here: does the surrounding assembly resolve the task demand, or do its boundary conditions impose contact pressure, traction, or a bending moment on the artery? Those loads would produce wall stress and strain; bending or flattening would be deformations, not forces. Bone is not automatically causal because it is visible, and arterial deformation is not automatically adverse. The relation becomes consequential only through its direction, magnitude, rate, repetition, duration, material context, and separately measured effect.

Pressure, stress, and strain should remain distinct. Blood pressure is a fluid quantity that contributes a normal traction on the luminal surface. Stress describes internal force intensity within the wall; strain describes deformation relative to a declared reference state. External contact can alter all three through the wall's shape, support, and active response, but a smaller lumen does not disclose their values. Estimating them for one arterial segment would require more than an image: reference geometry, wall thickness and properties, axial constraint, luminal pressure, surrounding contact conditions, and the task state would all have to be declared or measured.

The hose analogy from Chapter 13 supplies only causal order. A boot–ground relation imposes forces on a hose wall; the wall deforms under external contact, wall tension, internal pressure, end constraints, and geometry; the resulting cross-section then enters the fluid problem. A living artery adds active regulation, a heterogeneous wall, pulsatile blood, collateral routes, moving surroundings, and biological change. Idealized collapsible-tube models support the narrower point that transmural pressure, wall properties, axial conditions, supports, and geometry jointly determine cross-sectional state.[^24-05] They cannot turn a measured diameter into wall stress, exposure history, or distal perfusion.

Even “compression reduces flow” is too coarse as a general physiological rule. In one experiment, distributed sleeve pressure between 13 and 23 mmHg increased measured forearm arterial inflow in nine healthy men under the studied conditions, an effect the investigators interpreted through autoregulatory response to altered transmural pressure.[^24-06] That experiment is not a model of focal rib–artery contact, and it says nothing about arterial thoracic outlet pathology. Its value is negative: external pressure does not have one directionally fixed effect on living perfusion. Geometry, pressure gradient, vascular regulation, collateral routes, exposure pattern, and tissue state must be separated.

For an extrinsic account of the composite, the candidate causal order is therefore:

1. a defined surrounding relation and task create boundary conditions;
2. those conditions impose a local exposure on the artery;
3. the arterial wall and lumen assume a state under that exposure and their internal conditions;
4. repeated or sustained exposure may change the wall’s material state; and
5. the changed wall, lumen, thrombus, and distal arterial tree may alter perfusion and clinical presentation.

The first case report observes the surrounding anatomy and the later arterial state. It does not directly observe steps two through four as they developed. The mechanical reconstruction proposes that the focal bony relation repeatedly deformed or contacted the artery and that cumulative exposure contributed to wall injury. Spatial co-location, post-stenotic change, local thrombus, distal occlusion, and recurrence of similar anatomy–pathology patterns in selected cohorts motivate that reconstruction. They do not measure its exposure dose or transition in wall state.

Keeping that interval reconstructed makes the account testable. If a later study showed no contact or deformation under the relevant tasks, the proposed extrinsic arrow would weaken. If pathology or history supported an intrinsic arterial process that preceded the focal relation, causal priority could reverse. If similar focal relations routinely existed without wall exposure or if comparable wall lesions occurred without them, the relation would need additional boundary conditions. The mechanism thus identifies observations capable of narrowing it.

The reconstructed interval can still be tested in pieces. Does focal geometry change arterial course or cross-section across relevant states? Can imaging or a validated model estimate contact pressure, traction, moment, wall stress, strain, or deformation at the implicated segment rather than merely report a smaller regional distance? Does a tissue-specific wall response align with that exposure rather than with an intrinsic process? Do task history or serial observations order exposure before a retained change?

Each piece needs an appropriate comparison. The opposite limb can be informative but is not automatically a control when anatomy, image quality, exposure, or tissue state differs. People with a similar bony relation but no arterial pathology can test sufficiency. People with arterial pathology but no comparable external relation can test specificity. Within-person imaging across tasks can show state dependence, but it cannot by itself establish cumulative injury. A model can estimate an otherwise inaccessible quantity, but its conclusion remains conditional on geometry, wall properties, boundary conditions, and validation.

The proposal would gain support if focal geometry, local exposure, tissue-specific response, compatible chronology, and a distal consequence converged while the comparison cases in which the sequence failed were also reported. It would weaken if relevant tasks produced no focal exposure, if an intrinsic process preceded the spatial relation, or if the proposed intermediate measurements did not discriminate the reconstruction from its alternatives.

## What the record shows—and what it does not

The arterial chain can now be written with the evidence state of each arrow visible:

> observed focal bony relation → **reconstructed repeated arterial deformation and candidate wall-state change** → observed stenosis, post-stenotic dilation, and a filling defect interpreted as thrombus → observed distal occlusion → observed collateralization and side-to-side pressure difference → reported presentation

The first and later nodes were observed in the source record. The middle arrow was not. The relation from the local filling defect interpreted as thrombus to distal occlusion is clinically coherent and spatially supported, but a single record cannot eliminate every other embolic source.[^24-01] The relation from distal arterial state to coldness is compatible with the findings, but coldness alone would not identify either the structure or the cause.

The bilateral anatomy provides an internal resolution check, not a disease null. Unusual rib-like structures appeared on both sides, while the specific first- and second-rib fusion and documented arterial chain appeared on the right. Because detailed left-vessel evaluation was not feasible, the case cannot establish anatomy without disease on the left. It can establish a narrower point: the broad category “rib-like anomaly present” does not specify the focal geometry seen on the right. Side, local geometry, actual tissue relation, task, and arterial state still have to be described.

An independent anatomical comparator supplies a null unavailable within the case. A meta-analysis of 141 studies reported cervical ribs in a pooled 1.1 percent of healthy participants, compared with 29.5 percent of participants selected for thoracic outlet syndrome.[^24-14] The association is substantial, but occurrence in the healthy groups shows that a bony variant is not sufficient to establish the syndrome or an arterial lesion. Cervical ribs are not identical to the composite's first-rib fusion, so the comparison controls the broad anatomy-to-disease shortcut rather than the exact focal mechanism.

A second counterexample comes from healthy provocative testing. In a cross-sectional study of nineteen healthy volunteers scanned bilaterally in seven arm positions, 28.9 percent showed a Doppler waveform classified by the study as significant arterial compression in at least one position. Only 21 percent of participants who reported discomfort in a position also showed the significant waveform change.[^24-07] The study was small, young, and protocol-specific. It does not estimate population prevalence, and it does not test people with the focal lesion in the composite. It does establish a crucial noncorrespondence: a position-dependent waveform change can occur without the stenosis, post-stenotic dilation, local thrombus, and distal arterial consequence that define this chapter’s anchor.

The specialist cohorts provide a third noncorrespondence. In the Vemuri series, some people with objective subclavian dilation did not present with ischemia.[^24-04] Arterial imaging findings, distal physiology, and symptoms therefore cannot be compressed into a single variable called “compression.”

A living artery is not its lumen. The phrase “loss of space” collapses several different objects. The corridor has geometry. The artery has a course, wall, cross-sectional shape, lumen, and axial condition. Blood has pressure, velocity, and flow. The distal limb has perfusion, collateral routes, and metabolic demand. The person has findings and symptoms. An image may describe one while leaving the others unknown.

This separation explains why a kinked-hose story is not complete when the lumen gets smaller. A changed lumen can alter hydraulic resistance, but the size and direction of a living response also depend on pressure conditions, pulsatility, wall behavior, active tone, collateral pathways, and segment geometry. An acquired arterial change, when independently established, can remain important after a provocative position is gone. A distal occlusion can dominate current perfusion after initiating contact has ceased. Collateral vessels can preserve flow and are compatible with prior vascular reorganization; they do not date that history by themselves.

The source measured a side-to-side systolic-pressure difference; it did not directly measure tissue perfusion, oxygen delivery, or the adequacy of the collateral supply. Coldness was a reported presentation, not a substitute for any of those variables. Even in this unusually concrete case, the distal functional state therefore remains only partly measured. The distinction prevents the observed collaterals from being read either as proof of adequate flow or as a timestamp for the initiating event.

Pressure and perfusion are distinct endpoints.

The composite illustrates all three times at once. The bony relation is a current boundary condition. The post-stenotic dilation and filling defect interpreted as local thrombus are a current arterial state that may encode prior exposure. The collateral circulation and distal occlusion are downstream states with their own history. A single static scan does not tell us which part of the current state still depends on present contact, which part persists intrinsically, and which part reflects compensation.

It is useful to distinguish four possibilities:

- A **transient state** appears under a task and reverses when the task ends, without a demonstrated retained lesion.
- A **repeatedly recreated state** reverses between exposures but returns under recurring boundary conditions.
- A **retained state** persists because wall injury, thrombus, scar, remodeling, or another intrinsic change now maintains it.
- A **mixed state** depends on both current external conditions and an acquired intrinsic change.

The categories refer to maintenance, not moral value. Ordinary arteries undergo transient deformation. Repeated deformation can be tolerated. A retained state may be stable and well compensated. The clinical importance depends on the tissue-specific function, dose, time, capacity, and measured consequence.

Alternatives belong inside this chain. A known upper-extremity arterial occlusion with suspected embolic origin requires a source evaluation that can include cardiac and noncardiac origins; the focal thoracic-outlet reconstruction does not replace that work.[^24-17] The mechanical account must also leave open intrinsic arterial disease, trauma, altered coagulation, distal disease, vasospasm, and a bony variant incidental to an independently diseased artery. This is a set of candidate alternative accounts for clinical review, not a differential diagnosis supplied by the chapter.

The 2026 source reported a sinus rhythm, no relevant smoking or medical history, normal inflammatory markers, and negative selected autoimmune serologies. Those observations lower support for some alternatives; they do not eliminate every intrinsic, embolic, inflammatory, or coagulation-related account.[^24-01] A transparent reconstruction says what was evaluated and what remains possible. It does not turn a negative list into proof of the favored origin.

Reverse direction is also an explicit hypothesis at some links: a thrombotic or ischemic state may alter movement and muscle use, and an acquired lesion may change the artery's response to later positional demand. The source record does not observe those transitions. Their role here is to prevent post-event geometry from being treated automatically as initiating geometry.

This plural account gives mechanics a precise place. The proposed external relation may be primary, contributory, modifying, consequential, or incidental in a given history. Observations should be capable of changing the relative support for those accounts; no universal ranking is needed in advance.

## Venous and neural boundaries

The neighboring subclavian vein cannot inherit the arterial story. Its pressure regime, wall behavior, outflow function, thrombosis process, collateral response, and diagnostic criteria are different. The venous comparison has one smaller job: to show how a proposed external exposure can become an intrinsic or mixed state through time.

For thrombotic venous thoracic outlet syndrome, the objective event is axillary-subclavian upper-extremity deep venous thrombosis (DVT). The mechanical attribution is separate. Current guidance distinguishes thoracic-outlet-associated thrombosis from catheter-associated, cancer-associated, and other upper-extremity thromboses, which makes etiology an explicit question rather than something supplied by the word *thrombosis*.[^24-08]

The proposed sequence is:

> relevant exposure plus individual susceptibility → reconstructed repeated costoclavicular venous deformation and altered local wall or flow state → objective thrombosis → retained thrombus, fibrosis, stenosis, occlusion, recanalization, or collateral adaptation → changed response to later demand

Only some parts of that sequence are directly observed. A 2003 magnetic-resonance study found positional venous compression in both symptomatic participants and asymptomatic volunteers. At the costoclavicular space, it was reported after the maneuver in 61 percent of the symptomatic group and 54 percent of volunteers.[^24-09] The protocol and populations limit the percentages, but the overlap is the point: positional venous geometry does not itself identify thrombosis or clinical attribution.

Later cohorts make the retained state visible. One retrospective series included 103 people with completely occluded axillosubclavian veins and grouped them by time from the recorded venous event. Later imaging showed a mixture of recanalization and persistent occlusion across all chronicity groups. More than half of the cohort had received thrombolysis before referral, and all were selected for an operative pathway, so the observations cannot reconstruct untreated natural history.[^24-10] Another treated series classified fifty-one veins after decompression as widely patent, patent after angioplasty, residually stenotic, or occluded. Symptomatic relief did not differ significantly across those completion-venography classes.[^24-11]

These studies do not show that geometry is irrelevant. They show that present lumen class is not the entire state. Thrombus, wall change, collateral routes, recanalization, prior treatment, function, and symptoms can evolve on different timelines. Once an event has altered the material system, removing or changing the proposed external condition does not rewind the history.

The neural story remains open. The brachial plexus passes through the same broad region, but it does not share the vascular variables. A neural account would need its own sequence: a defined boundary condition; imposed contact pressure, traction, or bending moment; neural stress, strain, course change, or lost excursion; an intraneural physiological or conduction change; a functional effect; and a relation to findings or symptoms. Each arrow requires evidence from the selected neural presentation.

An arterial image cannot measure neural contact pressure. A venous caliber change cannot establish nerve strain. Paresthesia cannot identify which structure was exposed or where. A provocative maneuver may alter symptoms through several routes and still fail to determine diagnosis or causal priority. The reporting standards’ separation of the three entities therefore keeps a compelling result in one tissue from lending unearned certainty to another.[^24-02]

This boundary is a useful result. The framework can specify what a complete neural claim would require even when the current chapter does not provide it. That is better than filling the open cells with a generic word such as *compression*.

## Transfer beyond a lumen

If the maintained-constraint model applies only to tubes, it is a conduit model rather than a general account of hard/soft mechanical roles. A deliberately nonvascular test is the middle-finger flexor digitorum superficialis tendon and adjacent subsynovial connective tissue at the wrist during controlled finger movement. In one study of twenty healthy wrists, ultrasound-measured tendon excursion, surrounding-tissue excursion, and a calculated relative-motion index changed when participants gripped tubes of three diameters.[^24-12] The result establishes task-dependent co-motion, not a preferred grip, an ideal relative-motion value, an adverse constraint, or a pathological threshold.

In the direction of tendon pull, the tendon can be modeled as a relatively tensile, shape-preserving carrier and the layered surrounding tissue as a form-receiving interface that moves and deforms while accommodating excursion. That role assignment is this project's interpretation, not the study's conclusion; the tendon still deforms and the surrounding tissue still bears load. What transfers are the questions: which movement is required, which boundary conditions and active drive create it, which structure moves relative to which reference, whether a difference is transient or retained, and whether any independent function changes.

The variables themselves do not transfer. Tendon excursion is not lumen area, and relative motion is not blood flow, local shear stress, frictional work, adhesion, injury, or tissue capacity. In an eight-cadaver experiment, ultrasound relative displacement and directly measured frictional work responded differently to wrist posture and tendon force.[^24-13] Finger flexion followed by extension is likewise kinematic reversal, not biological recovery after release of a harmful state. With no independently measured downstream function or adverse transition in the healthy calibration, those cells must remain open.

## What this lens contributes

The thoracic-outlet composite supplies a bounded example of how a surrounding relation, tissue state, and separately measured consequence can be assembled across time and changing task states into an inspectable mechanical account. Its scope is foundational: it neither turns a bony variant or waveform into a diagnosis nor converts a plausible reconstruction into observed history, a self-test, or an intervention.

It places imposed demand before the geometry that demand may help maintain. It assigns shape-preserving and form-receiving roles without reducing them to tissue classes. It distinguishes the corridor from the artery, the wall from the lumen, the lumen from flow, local pathology from distal perfusion, and all of these from symptoms and diagnosis. It allows an external relation to become an intrinsic or mixed state through time. It puts alternatives and null observations inside the mechanism instead of attaching them as disclaimers after the conclusion.

The resulting arterial account is strong at its endpoints and incomplete at its center. A focal right bony relation, right subclavian stenosis and post-stenotic dilation, a filling defect interpreted as local thrombus, distal occlusion, collateralization, and an objective pressure difference were observed together. Repeated deformation producing wall injury or another material-state change is the weakest, reconstructed arrow. The case's bilateral category shows why focal geometry must replace a generic anomaly label; independent cervical-rib occurrence and provocative waveform changes in healthy participants show that anatomy and dynamic geometry are not sufficient. The venous comparison shows why a later state cannot recover an initiating history. The tendon comparison shows that only the questions—not the vascular evidence—generalize to another form-receiving relation.

That is enough for an informative mechanical narrative. The load-routing lens adds a disciplined account of what bears, receives, maintains, changes, and persists. Diagnosis, causal certainty, and treatment selection remain separate clinical tasks.

The next chapter changes the failure signature. Here, the central question was whether a surrounding relation could maintain deformation and leave a retained state. Instability asks almost the inverse question: what happens when the system repeatedly fails to hold a relation, must correct it again, and does so under changing capacity?
