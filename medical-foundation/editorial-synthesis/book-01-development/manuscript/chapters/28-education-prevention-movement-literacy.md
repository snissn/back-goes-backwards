# Chapter 28 — Education, prevention, movement literacy, and environmental design

## Beyond “sit correctly”

Imagine a computer station shared by several people during a day. One person is tall and reads through bifocals. Another uses a wheelchair and needs open space below the work surface. A third is pregnant and finds sustained sitting uncomfortable. A fourth prefers forearm support because of fatigue. The task is nominally the same, but the available contacts, reaches, visual relations, assistance, schedules, and recovery opportunities are not.

The familiar response is a command: sit correctly. The command compresses a complicated system into a body shape and assigns the person the work of maintaining it. Even when kindly meant, it leaves essential questions unanswered. Correct for which task phase? Relative to which support? For how long? With what visual demand? Who can move the screen, change the schedule, obtain assistance, or leave the station? What outcome is the command expected to change?

A language of mechanical demand begins elsewhere. It describes what the task asks, which supports and alternatives exist, how long and how often the demand recurs, how the situation changes with fatigue, and what recovery is available. It keeps visible the difference between what can be observed and what remains unknown. A person may report pressure or fatigue; an observer may describe contact and configuration. Neither observation, by itself, reveals a unique internal load path, a tissue stress field, or the origin of a symptom.

That distinction matters because language changes how people interpret their bodies. In interviews about low-back pain, some clinical messages were remembered as reassurance, while others were understood as evidence of vulnerability and a need for protection; participants connected those interpretations with confidence, vigilance, worry, guilt, or frustration.[^28-01] The interviews do not prove that one phrase causes a particular outcome. They do show that education is not mechanically or psychologically neutral merely because it contains no exercise.

The purpose of *mechanical literacy* is therefore not to teach people to detect hidden faults. It is to make person–task–environment relations easier to describe while preserving uncertainty. The same discipline can inform public education, professional communication, environmental design, and carefully specified prevention programs. It does not yet authorize screening, diagnosis, individualized assessment, treatment, or personal correction.

## A public language for demand and options

Mechanical literacy uses a small set of ordinary questions. What does the task require? What carries or supports the person and the object? How long, how fast, and how often does the demand occur? What varies, and what remains constrained? What capacity and fatigue are relevant now? What recovery opportunities exist? Which alternatives are realistic in this setting?

These are not disguised measurements. *Demand* remains a broad task-level term when the internal force, moment, pressure, stress, strain, or deformation is unknown. *Support* includes the ground, furniture, tools, assistance, another person, or any other declared contact. *Option space* means the strategies a person can realistically use, not the maximum number of imaginable movements. Cost, safety, disability, pain, culture, schedule, work control, and competing duties can make a theoretical option unavailable.

Health literacy is not simply information deposited in an individual. The World Health Organization describes it as socially situated, shaped by communities, organizations, culture, and political conditions.[^28-02] The mechanical vocabulary proposed here should inherit that structural lesson, not the authority of the source. It has not yet been shown to improve comprehension, communication, agency, or health. It should be compared with simpler existing language and revised if people cannot use it without inventing hidden damage.

Education studies show why every step must be measured separately. A large, nonrandomized public back-pain campaign coincided with improved beliefs among members of the public and general practitioners, while descriptive changes in compensation claims could not be attributed securely to the campaign.[^28-03] A later cluster-randomized eHealth program did not improve back-pain beliefs, disability, or absenteeism relative to a digital information letter, despite a potentially favorable cost-utility estimate.[^28-04] Medium, audience, comparison, engagement, and outcome changed the result; neither study settles whether education in general “works.”

The same separation appears in a two-school-year program for children. Posture knowledge, observed handling behavior, and time in selected trunk and neck positions changed, while fear-avoidance beliefs did not and pain did not clearly improve.[^28-05] Knowledge, observed behavior, a posture-duration measure, belief, and symptom were five different outcomes. None inherited the result of another.

This chapter therefore distinguishes six destinations for the framework:

1. **Public education** asks whether an audience understands and can use a vocabulary without added fear, vigilance, stigma, or burden.
2. **Professional education** asks whether people in established disciplines communicate or reason more accurately within their scopes.
3. **Environmental design** asks whether changes to tasks and settings alter access, usability, exposure, performance, comfort, fatigue, or participation.
4. **Prevention** asks whether a named adverse endpoint changes in a named population over a stated time.
5. **Clinical use** would ask whether using the information in care improves a patient-facing decision or outcome relative to alternatives and harms.
6. **Research** asks whether constructs can be defined, measured, compared, and disfavored before wider use.

These destinations can inform one another, but they do not share an automatic promotion path. Comprehension is not behavior. Behavior is not internal exposure. Exposure is not injury. A clinical application is not validated because the public language is understandable. A research measure is not useful merely because it is reproducible.

That separation changes how teaching should be built. A first lesson might ask learners to distinguish the task from the person performing it, name supports and constraints, and mark internal quantities as unknown. Its immediate test is whether they understood that distinction. A later exercise might ask whether the language transfers to a different task without becoming a fault-finding routine. A still later study might examine whether recognized options become usable options in a real setting. Each stage can fail independently. A lesson that is memorable but increases fear is not rescued by recall. A vocabulary that improves description but adds no useful information can remain educationally interesting without being promoted into professional or clinical use.

Mechanical literacy also needs a positive account of uncertainty. “Unknown” should not mean that a dangerous process is presumed but hidden. It can mean that no internal quantity was measured, that several distributions remain mechanically possible, or that the available detail is unnecessary for the question at hand. A learner who can stop at a good task description has succeeded; the lesson should not reward the invention of a deeper route merely because the framework contains one.

Different modes of explanation can serve different audiences. Diagrams may clarify contacts and boundaries. Ordinary metaphors may make duration or recovery easier to discuss. Tactile and embodied examples may help a person report experience. None should be presented as a direct measurement of internal force, stress, strain, pressure, or a force line. The representational form changes how an idea is encountered, not its evidence state.

## Professional education without a new jurisdiction

Physicians, nurses, therapists, movement professionals, occupational-health teams, ergonomists, engineers, coaches, designers, educators, and researchers encounter different portions of the same person–task–environment system. Their scopes, methods, and responsibilities remain distinct. A shared vocabulary might help them state what was observed, which quantity was measured, what outcome matters, and where a causal sequence remains incomplete. That is a coordination hypothesis, not a claim that every profession needs a larger dose of mechanics.

The World Health Organization treats interprofessional education and collaborative practice as strategies that must be adapted to local workforce and system needs.[^28-06] It does not endorse this book's grammar. A legitimate development question would compare the proposed vocabulary with existing documentation and handoff practices: Does it improve question quality or agreement about task and evidence state? Does it reduce unsupported inference or duplication? Does it instead add time, confusion, or scope crossing?

A pragmatic cluster-randomized low-back-pain program illustrates the distance between professional and patient outcomes. Clinicians' knowledge, attitudes, confidence, and guideline-concordant behavior improved, but the groups did not differ on the primary six-month disability measure or secondary patient outcomes.[^28-07] The program also changed management, so it is not a pure test of education. Its useful lesson is narrower: professional learning can succeed by its own measures without demonstrating superior patient recovery.

Clinical use is a separate destination for exactly this reason. Converting Chapter 27's questions into a form, score, examination sequence, or decision aid would require a declared clinical purpose, intended users, comparison, measurement properties, patient-facing consequences, burdens, and harms. This chapter offers none of those tools. Shared language should support existing responsibilities only after it shows added value within them.

## Modern exposure is a sequence, not a verdict

Screen work, manual work, caregiving, sport, transport, sitting, standing, and assistive-device use are activity categories. They are not doses and they are not diagnoses. Each can combine long duration, specialized repetition, constrained visual or hand demands, high pace, low control, limited variation, or insufficient recovery. Each can also support valued skill, participation, livelihood, comfort, training, or independence.

The mechanically relevant object is the sequence. A technology or setting shapes the task. The task and organization shape contacts, pace, repetition, variation, control, and available strategies. Those conditions create an exposure relative to a changing capacity and recovery context. The possible responses branch: useful adaptation, capable specialization, stable tolerance, fatigue, symptom modulation, injury, reduced participation, or no important effect. A later change in behavior, care, work, sleep, or environment feeds back into the next exposure.

Measured changes in an early node do not guarantee later changes. In a cluster-randomized office study, a multicomponent intervention reduced sitting at 12 months by about 22 minutes per day without a height-adjustable desk and about 64 minutes per day with one, relative to control. Most measured cardiometabolic, fatigue, musculoskeletal, and work outcomes showed no noticeable intervention effects, although selected wellbeing and pain measures changed modestly.[^28-08] The intervention changed sitting. That result neither makes sitting inherently pathological nor proves disease prevention.

Design can also redistribute demand. In a large participatory-ergonomics trial, selected decision-control measures improved modestly, reported exposure to awkward trunk postures increased, most physical and psychosocial exposure outcomes were null, and loss to follow-up was substantial.[^28-09] A companion analysis found no reduction in low-back- or neck-pain prevalence, intensity, or duration and no primary prevention effect; low-back-pain recovery improved while neck-pain recovery did not.[^28-10] The same program produced a small organizational benefit, an adverse exposure result, several nulls, and one favorable recovery result. Pooling them into a single verdict would erase what the study actually found.

The opening workstation makes the sequence concrete. During one short session, the available arrangement may be comfortable and effective. Across a longer shift, visual precision, task pace, forearm support, opportunities to change position, and accumulating fatigue may alter the demand. If the worker has control over timing and setup, the response options differ from those available under a fixed schedule. If a symptom appears, it may modify later behavior and exposure; it does not retrospectively identify the initial internal route. If no symptom appears, that absence does not prove that every measured exposure was negligible. The history has to be reconstructed at the resolution the claim requires.

This is also why repetition and variability are not simple opposites. Repetition can build skill and capacity, contribute to valued specialization, or concentrate exposure. Variability can distribute demand, disrupt precision, increase cognitive burden, or have no consequential effect. Recovery can occur during a task, between tasks, during sleep, after a schedule change, or through adaptation across longer periods. The useful question is not whether a category is good or bad. It is which demand, receiver, state, interval, and outcome are being compared.

## Design changes the available system

Human factors and ergonomics already treats performance and wellbeing as properties of interactions among people and other system elements, spanning physical, cognitive, and organizational domains.[^28-11] Work-system guidance places people, tasks, tools, technologies, organization, and environment inside one design problem and emphasizes worker participation, individual differences, continuing evaluation, and redesign.[^28-12] This broader boundary prevents “ergonomics” from collapsing into chair dimensions.

For this chapter, the design system contains five coupled elements:

```text
person and changing state
    <-> task and performance requirement
    <-> assistance, tools, and supports
    <-> organization, schedule, resources, and control
    <-> built, social, and information environment
```

No element is presumed to be the fault or the correction target. A change in support may reduce one demand and increase reach, pressure, visual effort, cognitive burden, cost, or dependence on unavailable assistance. A standard arrangement can fit the tall bifocal wearer at the opening station while blocking the wheelchair user's approach. Moving the screen may improve one person's visual relation and worsen another's. The lesson is not that standards are useless. It is that standardization, fit, reported comfort, measured exposure, access, and prevention are different objects.

Control should follow agency. A person cannot choose staffing, production pace, procurement, transport, classroom furniture, or whether a public counter is accessible. Occupational-health guidance therefore prioritizes eliminating, substituting, or redesigning harmful work conditions before relying only on education that asks individuals to manage them.[^28-13] Education can still help people name demands and participate in redesign. It should not become a low-cost substitute for equipment, assistance, schedule control, or source reduction.

The intervention evidence again resists a slogan. In a one-year randomized trial among call-center operators, forearm support was associated with less selected upper-body pain and a lower incidence of a physician-ascertained neck or shoulder disorder.[^28-14] This is a meaningful positive result for a specified support, workforce, disorder definition, and period. It does not identify universal equipment dimensions or a unique internal mechanism.

Other programs produced null or mixed results. A participatory intervention in 119 kitchens generated and implemented hundreds of changes but did not improve pain, local fatigue, musculoskeletal sick leave, or perceived physical workload during intervention or follow-up.[^28-15] In a childcare trial, physical exertion and several pain outcomes did not differ, sickness absence decreased, and self-efficacy worsened slightly.[^28-16] Activity, participation, and implementation are not themselves efficacy. Nor does a null trial establish that participation or design can never help.

External support deserves particular care in this model. Support changes the boundary conditions under which a task is performed; it does not automatically reduce total demand or signal incapacity. A handrail can enable participation. A backrest can make sustained attention possible. A lifting device can shift demand from one person while introducing coordination, time, or access requirements elsewhere. Removing support can be useful in a training setting and exclusionary in a public one. The value belongs to the declared task and outcome, not to an abstract ranking of supported and unsupported bodies.

The same applies to option count. More theoretical alternatives do not always improve a system. Too many adjustments can increase cognitive work, delay a time-sensitive task, or place the burden of solving a design problem on the user. One stable strategy may be highly capable. What matters is whether the available strategies are usable, compatible with the person's priorities, and revisable when state or task changes. Option space is a description of feasible relations, not a health score.

Accessibility changes the model rather than decorating it. The United Nations distinguishes universal design from reasonable accommodation in a particular case and explicitly preserves assistive devices for people who need them.[^28-17] The World Health Organization's classification of functioning places participation and environmental factors inside its account of functioning and disability.[^28-18] Its disability policy calls for consultation and inclusion across design, implementation, monitoring, and evaluation, while joint WHO–UNICEF guidance treats assistive technology as support for functioning, participation, and inclusion amid large access gaps.[^28-19] None of these sources supplies a mechanical outcome. Together they prevent unsupported movement, symmetry, youth, or independence from becoming the hidden design standard.

## Prevention has six different objects

The word *prevention* is unfinished until its object is named. A defensible claim also names the population, baseline state, intervention, comparator, time at risk or follow-up, measurement, implementation, adverse outcomes, and relevant competing events. Lower-burden outcomes such as comprehension, recognized options, comfort, fatigue, or task tolerance can matter greatly. They should be called by their names rather than promoted into prevention of an unspecified harm.

The six prevention objects used here are exposure, symptom, injury or defined disorder, disability, recurrence, and participation. They are not steps in one guaranteed chain and they are not ordered by human value. Each has a positive or mixed example, a null, adverse, or boundary result, and a conclusion it cannot inherit.

The required record changes slightly with each object. Exposure needs a defined quantity or behavior and stated measurement conditions. A symptom needs its incidence, severity, duration, interference, or recovery identified. An injury or defined disorder needs a case definition, ascertainment method, exposure opportunity, and competing events. Disability needs a specified function and context. Recurrence needs a recovered baseline, exact event definition, surveillance interval, and censoring rules. Participation needs a person-valued life situation, access conditions, and some account of sustainability. Those differences are not clerical. They determine what event has occurred and which comparison is legitimate.

> **Text alternative.** Exactly six rows separate exposure, symptom, injury or defined disorder, disability, recurrence, and participation. Two examples show why the last three cells must remain distinct. An integrated workplace and graded-activity program for workers with chronic low-back pain shortened time to sustainable return to work and improved function, while pain did not differ.[^28-20] Participation and disability changed without a corresponding pain result, and the multicomponent design does not isolate an ergonomic mechanism.

In another trial involving people with work disabilities, a participatory workplace intervention did not improve sustainable employment, mental-health scores were slightly worse, and delivery was low because the workplace component generally could not begin until employment had already been obtained.[^28-21] The result does not argue against accommodation or disability inclusion. It shows how program logic and implementation can fail before a desired participation outcome is reached.

Recurrence requires its own baseline and event definition. In a randomized trial of adults recovered from nonspecific low-back pain, an individualized progressive walking-and-education program delayed activity-limiting recurrence, while lower-extremity adverse events were more numerous in the intervention group and the contributions of walking and education could not be separated.[^28-22] This is evidence for that intervention and that recurrence endpoint. It is not an instruction to walk, a universal dose, primary prevention, or evidence for a preferred body configuration.

An honest prevention narrative can therefore end in several ways. A proposal may change the intended outcome. It may change an early exposure while leaving later outcomes null. It may improve one outcome and worsen another. It may be sound in principle but delivered too weakly to test. It may transfer demand or burden to another person or part of the system. Or it may add no value compared with simpler existing practice. Those endings are information, not inconveniences to be hidden behind a broad claim of benefit.

The figure deliberately does not add a generic disease cell. A disease-prevention claim would require a condition-specific causal and intervention chain, including biological, psychological, social, developmental, environmental, and medical alternatives appropriate to that condition. It cannot be manufactured by extending a favorable exposure or symptom result upward. Population benefit would add another layer: reach, distribution of benefit and harm, cost, adoption, sustainability, and the consequences of displacing other programs. Naming those higher burdens preserves the ambition without pretending that the present studies have met it.

## Access, control, and the cost of awareness

The practical option space depends on who controls the conditions. Disability, pregnancy, aging, chronic illness, pain, fatigue, body diversity, language, culture, job security, caregiving, finances, digital access, and safety can change both the feasible strategies and the meaning of an outcome. A design that increases theoretical choice while requiring money, disclosure, unpaid time, or unavailable assistance has not necessarily increased realistic options.

Participation is an outcome in its own right. It is not a proxy for symptom relief, tissue state, independence, or conformity to one movement strategy. Assistance, rest, sitting, stable specialization, and external support can be capable solutions. A person should not have to display or correct a body to earn access.

Participatory design is valuable as a way of discovering needs and sharing authority; it is not an efficacy guarantee. Review must ask who chose the outcome, who could refuse, who controlled resources, which proposed changes were implemented, and whose costs increased. An average benefit can coexist with exclusion or harm in a subgroup.

The distinction among universal design, accommodation, and assistance helps keep responsibility distributed. A broadly accessible baseline may reduce the number of barriers many people encounter. It will not eliminate every need for an individual modification or a particular support. Accommodation responds to a person's actual relation with a task and setting. Assistance can be part of an effective configuration rather than a temporary concession on the way to unsupported performance. The three approaches overlap in practice, but treating them as interchangeable can hide whose needs remain unmet.

Institutional control also changes the ethics of advice. A suggestion that is low burden for someone with flexible time and private space can be impossible for a worker whose pace is monitored, a student who cannot leave class, or a caregiver who cannot pause a transfer. Asking such a person to manage the exposure personally can create guilt without changing the relevant condition. The analysis should move upstream until it reaches a variable that an individual, team, organization, or public body can actually alter.

Monitoring deserves the same scrutiny. Digital systems can help recognize hazards, but occupational-safety guidance also identifies risks involving surveillance, privacy, data ownership, discrimination, algorithmic management, and replacement of collective controls with individual monitoring.[^28-23] Public-health-surveillance ethics adds duties of necessity, proportionality, autonomy, equity, transparency, and engagement.[^28-24] These principles do not make every sensor harmful. They do rule out the assumption that more awareness is automatically benign.

A monitoring proposal should name the hazard and outcome, explain why direct design change is insufficient, identify who owns and can act on the data, and include refusal, correction, deletion, and redress. It should test false alerts, missingness, unequal performance, stigma, vigilance, and whether feedback expands options or commands conformity. A posture alert that makes a worker continuously correct the body while production pace remains unchanged may transfer responsibility without controlling the source.

The same adverse pathway applies to public education. Mechanical language can enlarge agency when it helps people describe demand and request change. It can narrow agency when it teaches them to scan for invisible damage, interpret fatigue as failure, or feel responsible for conditions they cannot control. Comprehension, burden, vigilance, shame, and blame therefore belong among the first outcomes of any mechanical-literacy program.

## What belongs to development, not personal correction

Book I can responsibly offer a descriptive language and a set of design questions. It can argue that duration, repetition, variability, support, capacity, recovery, and control deserve explicit attention. It can show that environments alter boundary conditions and that prevention claims must name their outcomes. These are substantive claims, but they do not complete the applied program.

Individual tests, correction cues, posture prescriptions, equipment specifications, movement doses, exercise progressions, posterior-engagement instruction, force-line imagery, breathing practices, bandhas, traditional correspondences, and self-experimentation belong to later development and later books. So do therapeutic use and condition-specific prevention. Their preservation matters: deferral is not rejection. It prevents general literacy from acting as unearned evidence for a personal intervention.

The larger institutional ambition also remains alive. Schools, workplaces, communities, professional curricula, health systems, and public agencies are possible sites of development. Work on complex interventions emphasizes context, program theory, stakeholders, uncertainty, refinement, evaluation, and implementation rather than a one-way march from idea to scale.[^28-25] Applied here, the sequence begins with co-defining an audience and problem; testing comprehension, accessibility, and unintended effects; examining feasibility and burden in a bounded setting; comparing the proposal with existing practice; and evaluating only the outcome actually claimed. Revision or withdrawal remains a legitimate result.

Research has a distinct role across that sequence. It can define constructs, compare plain language, test whether observers classify the same task similarly, measure exposure under declared conditions, and examine whether a proposed change reaches the intended endpoint. It can also discover that the framework adds no value, works only in a narrower setting, excludes an audience, or produces an adverse interpretation. Those results should route back to the component that failed. A null comprehension result changes the teaching object; it does not refute all environmental design. A null health result after a successful exposure change weakens the later link; it does not erase the measured exposure result.

Therapeutic development and health-system development require still different records. A therapeutic proposal needs a population, mechanism, dose, comparator, contraindications, adverse events, and patient-valued outcome. A health-system proposal needs governance, workforce, workflow, cost, equity, and comparison with existing services. Chapter 29 will separate those tracks so that a promising educational result cannot be used as unearned support for treatment or system change.

Return to the shared computer station. Mechanical literacy does not reveal one correct arrangement. It makes the system legible enough to ask better questions: which task phase matters, who can use the support, what can be adjusted, what cannot, which demand moves elsewhere, and what outcome will show whether the change helped. One response might alter the environment. Another might provide assistance or accommodation. Another might leave the arrangement alone because it already works for the person and task. None requires a hidden diagnosis.

The present contribution is a vocabulary for demand and options, a systems boundary for design, and an outcome-specific discipline for prevention. Its strongest claim is not that education or ergonomics will produce health. It is that a serious program must keep comprehension, behavior, exposure, injury, symptom, participation, and system outcomes from silently substituting for one another. Chapter 29 inherits the next question: how should the separate research, measurement, education, therapeutic-development, implementation, and health-system tracks be organized so that each can fail, learn, and advance without borrowing another track's result?
