# Chapter 13 — Constraint-maintained deformation and loss of excursion

Take an ordinary garden hose and bend it gently. While your hand holds the bend, one side of the wall flattens and the open cross-section may become smaller. Release it, and the hose tends toward its earlier shape. Now place the same bend between two fixed objects. The narrowed region can look much the same, but the explanation has changed: the surrounding geometry continually maintains it. Damage or lasting material change could produce a third case in which the altered shape remains after the surrounding objects are removed.

A still image might show a similar narrow region in all three cases. It cannot, by itself, tell us what keeps that region narrow. Shape is evidence about the current geometry. It is not a complete account of the forces, pressures, material state, contacts, or history that produced and maintain that geometry.

This distinction matters in the body. A structure may bend, flatten, slide, expand, change course, or move less during one phase than another. Much of that deformation is ordinary and useful. When a state persists, however, or when a needed movement is repeatedly unavailable, the mechanically informative question is not merely, “Where is there less space?” It is, “What relation maintains this shape or limits this excursion, on what timescale, and with what measured consequence?”

The question is deliberately more specific than a diagnosis. It asks us to reconstruct a mechanical state before assigning biological or clinical importance to it. That reconstruction begins with a reference, a system boundary, and time.

## A shape is not its cause

*Deformation* means a change in shape or in the relative positions of material points, compared with a declared reference. It is not another word for force, stress, damage, or pain. A visible contour may document gross deformation, but it does not reveal the complete strain field inside a material or the stress field maintaining it.[^13-01]

The reference need not be an ideal posture or a truly unloaded state. Living tissues may already be prestressed, pressurized, actively tensioned, or supported by their surroundings. A useful reference can therefore be a reproducible task phase, an earlier observation, the other side of a within-person comparison, or a stated model configuration. Its job is to make the comparison inspectable, not to smuggle in an assumption of perfection.

*Maintained* also requires a clock. A state can be maintained continuously while a set of boundary conditions remains present. It can be recreated each time a task returns to the same phase. It can also persist because the material or active state now supports it even after the conditions that first produced it have changed. Maintained does not mean motionless or permanent. The forces and contacts may vary while a relationship is repeatedly re-established.

A *constraint* is a boundary condition or material relation that limits a relevant degree of freedom. Ordinary contact is not automatically a constraint in this sense, and not every reduction in motion is a loss. The degree of freedom must matter to the declared system and task. A door is constrained from translating through its hinge while remaining free to rotate; the hinge does not simply make the door “less mobile.” Biological structures likewise depend on guided freedoms, contact, and restraint.

*Excursion* names motion of a structure relative to a reference or to its surroundings during a stated task or phase. It may be measured as displacement, rotation, change of course, relative slide, or another system-specific quantity. Less excursion in one task than another is merely a difference until we establish which freedom is required, how much variation and measurement error are ordinary, what maintains the difference, and whether any function changes.

Finally, a *restoring tendency* is anything that tends to alter or reverse the state under the conditions being considered. Elastic recoil, an internal or external pressure difference, active muscle action, inertia, fluid movement, or a change of support can all contribute. Observing a narrowed or displaced structure without accounting for its restoring tendencies leaves the equilibrium unexplained.

These definitions give us a practical sequence:

```text
declared task, reference, and boundary conditions
    -> contact, traction, pressure, bending, tethering, or active force
    -> maintained or repeatedly recreated material state
    -> shape, course, contact, or available excursion
    -> receiver-specific function, tested separately
```

The first line does not uniquely determine the second, and the last arrow is never supplied merely by drawing the earlier ones. Each relation needs an observable, a model, or an explicitly labeled hypothesis. A null result—changed geometry without changed function, or changed boundary conditions without the predicted change in geometry—is a meaningful outcome.

### Maintenance in a moving system

The word *equilibrium* can be misleading if it suggests a frozen arrangement. A person breathes, shifts support, changes muscle activity, and moves through phases. Internal pressure and contact can vary continuously. A maintained state can therefore be a dynamic balance: the particular forces change while their combined relation keeps a structure within a similar range of shapes. It can also be periodic. If every repetition of a task recreates the same restriction during the same interval, the relevant exposure may be intermittent even though no constraint is present between repetitions.

This changes how a maintaining hypothesis should be tested. One useful comparison is to alter a proposed boundary condition while measuring the mechanical state of interest. If a specific contact changes and a structure's course changes with it, that observation can support a relation between the two. It does not show that the earlier course was harmful, that the contact was the only contributor, or that a later biological result must follow. A second comparison must still ask whether the change exceeds ordinary variability and method error.

Failure to return immediately is also ambiguous. Another surrounding term may remain. Active force may change more slowly than the visible position. The material may have rate-dependent or history-dependent behavior. The reference may be wrong, or the observation interval may be too short. Persistence after one proposed term changes therefore does not automatically prove an intrinsic state; it narrows the possibilities only if the other conditions have been controlled or measured.

Most importantly, restoration of geometry is not synonymous with recovery. A tube can reopen, a tendon can reverse direction, or a joint can return to its starting angle without demonstrating restoration of material state, capacity, physiology, comfort, or long-term function. In this chapter, *return* describes a kinematic observation. *Recovery* is reserved for a separately defined endpoint measured after a defined exposure or state. The distinction prevents an easily observed movement from completing an unobserved biological interval.

## Hard and soft describe a division of mechanical labor

This book uses a compact formulation:

> Load wants to travel through the hard body so that the soft body can remain soft.

It is an organizing proposition, not a literal law of motion. Here *load* is broad language for mechanical demand. A technical application must replace it with the force, moment, contact pressure, traction, stress, strain, displacement, or other quantity actually at issue.

The distinction is motivated by traditional Chinese medicine's hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts. Book I develops a flexible mechanical translation of that motivation; it does not claim that the traditional and mechanical categories are definitionally identical. The terms name roles in a declared relationship, not permanent tissue classes.

A *hard role* is comparatively shape-preserving, force-transmitting, constraining, or structurally resolving for the task, direction, scale, and timescale under discussion. Bone is an obvious candidate, but a joint assembly, active muscle, tendon, ligament, fascia, cartilage, a pressurized cavity, or an externally supported region can perform such a role. A *soft role* is comparatively form-receiving and shape-conforming. It yields enough to accommodate surrounding form and retain a needed freedom: glide, expansion, containment, pressure response, course change, or local reshaping.

Form-receiving does not mean weak, passive, unimportant, or unloaded. A vessel wall must carry stress while changing caliber. A tendon can transmit high tensile force while bending and sliding. An interface can deform and carry shear while permitting relative movement. Even bone deforms. The proposed division is therefore not “hard tissue carries force; soft tissue carries none.” It concerns which parts of an assembly organize gross geometry and which parts must remain sufficiently able to receive form for their particular functions.

The same structure can change roles. Muscle that deforms easily when inactive may help preserve a joint relationship when active. A compliant chamber can accept changing contents in one phase and, through internal pressure and boundary tension, help support an assembly in another. Tendon may act as a tensile carrier along its length while participating in a form-receiving, sliding relation at an interface. Direction matters: relative resistance to axial stretch says little by itself about bending, transverse deformation, or glide.

The hard/soft proposition becomes testable only when it predicts more than a relabeling of stiffness. In a bounded task, we can identify a candidate shape-preserving assembly, measure the relevant freedom of a form-receiving structure, alter or compare the surrounding relation, and ask whether that freedom changes beyond ordinary variation and method error. We can then ask whether the change reverses when the relation changes and whether a separately defined function changes with it. If the same observations are explained just as well without the role distinction, the distinction has added organization to the language but not yet explanatory power.

The negative form of the hypothesis is now clearer. A problem may arise when gross structural demand is resolved by keeping a form-receiving structure in an imposed geometry or by requiring it to substitute for a shape-preserving role. The phrase *load entering the soft body* refers to this proposed substitution. It does not condemn ordinary stress, deformation, or pressure in compliant tissue. Whether the substitution is present—and whether it matters—remains a question for a specified system.

## The hose shows causal order, not biological proof

Return to the hose. Its wall does not have one geometry independent of its surroundings. Internal pressure, external pressure or contact, bending, axial conditions, supports, wall thickness, and material behavior contribute to its state. The difference between internal and surrounding pressure is especially important for a compliant wall, but it is not the only variable. Idealized collapsible-tube models show nonlinear relationships among these terms, including dependence on wall properties, length, axial prestretch, and the direction of external loading.[^13-02]

In an extrinsically maintained kink, the surrounding relation comes first in the explanatory order. Contact and bending change the wall's mechanical state. The wall assumes a changed shape and course, and its cross-sectional area may change. That geometry then becomes one boundary of a fluid problem. It is more precise to say that the external relation alters wall equilibrium and that the altered wall changes the pressure–flow relation than to say that “less space strains the flow.” Fluid stress has a technical meaning, and a velocity or pressure field cannot be inferred from the size of one pictured cross-section.

Nor does a smaller radius provide a universal answer. The familiar strong dependence of resistance on radius belongs to a restricted rigid-tube model with stated assumptions about geometry, viscosity, steady flow, and boundary conditions. A deformable biological conduit may be curved, branching, pulsatile, viscoelastic, actively regulated, tethered, and embedded in surrounding tissue. Inlet and outlet pressures, wall motion, gravity, network alternatives, and the state of the fluid or contents can all affect the result. Lumen geometry matters, but it is both an outcome of the wall problem and an input to the subsequent transport problem.

This separation preserves the important intuition behind the hose. If an elastic tube would tend to open when a surrounding relation is removed, then the continuing contact, pressure difference, bend, or tether belongs in the explanation of why it stays narrowed. “Loss of space” describes part of the geometry. It does not name the maintaining interaction. Conversely, an internally retained wall state may remain without current external contact. The same visible geometry can therefore arise through different causal orders.

The downstream direction is not guaranteed. In one small experiment in healthy young men, mild distributed pressure around the forearm increased measured arterial inflow under the studied conditions, while higher pressures reduced it in limited observations.[^13-03] That study is not a model of a focal kink, venous obstruction, or any named disorder. Its narrow value here is methodological: external compression does not have a single monotonic effect on living perfusion. Geometry, regulation, pressure, and network response must be measured in the system being discussed.

> **Figure 13.2 — What holds the hose kinked?**

The analogy stops early. A garden hose is passive, manufactured, and simple. Living conduits can actively change wall state, remodel, branch, exchange material, and participate in regulation. The hose teaches us to put the maintaining relation before the maintained geometry in the extrinsic case. It cannot tell us which anatomical structure supplies that relation, whether the state is unusual, or whether changing it would improve anything.

## Excursion can matter without a lumen

The same reasoning applies to structures whose relevant freedom is not cross-sectional area. A nerve may change course relative to surrounding tissues. An airway or cavity may change shape across a phase. Adjacent tissue layers may slide. A tendon must move through a bounded anatomical region while surrounding tissues move with it to some degree. The shared question is modest: what freedom does this system require for the declared task, and what maintains any observed change in that freedom?

To make that question measurable without importing flow, consider the middle-finger flexor digitorum superficialis tendon and adjacent subsynovial connective tissue at the wrist during controlled finger flexion and extension. Longitudinal ultrasound can track displacement of the tendon and a neighboring layer of subsynovial connective tissue. The two move together to a degree and also move relative to one another. Studies in healthy participants show that their absolute and relative movements change with the declared task, including grip diameter, active or passive drive, and neighboring finger configuration.[^13-04][^13-05]

Three quantities must remain separate. First is the tendon's absolute excursion relative to the image or another declared reference. Second is excursion of the selected subsynovial connective tissue region. Third is their relative displacement, sometimes summarized as a calculated ratio or index. A difference between the first two is not direct measurement of local shear stress, friction, tissue injury, or capacity.

This distinction is not merely verbal. Ultrasound tracking has been compared with imposed or marker-measured tendon movement in experimental systems, supporting its use for displacement while also revealing method-dependent error.[^13-06] Thin layered tissue is harder to track than the tendon itself. Image plane, probe pressure, region selection, frame rate, motion speed, and out-of-plane movement can change an estimate. A technical review of tendon speckle tracking likewise emphasizes dependence on imaging orientation and analysis choices.[^13-07] The measurement is useful precisely because its object and limits can be named.

The neutral finding is task dependence. It does not establish that one grip diameter, one relative-motion index, or more excursion is better. Co-motion can be part of ordinary function. Less motion in one phase can reflect a smaller joint excursion, different active drive, anatomical variation, an appropriate coupling of tissues, or measurement error. The selected studies do not identify an adverse surrounding constraint or a threshold at which relative motion becomes inadequate.

The example also prevents two tempting substitutions. First, motion reversing during finger extension is a *return phase*, not evidence of recovery after release of an adverse constraint. Recovery would require a prior adverse state, removal or alteration of its maintaining relation, a defined timescale, and a meaningful endpoint. Second, experimental work comparing ultrasound-based relative displacement with mechanically measured frictional work found that the quantities did not behave interchangeably across changes in posture, force, and velocity.[^13-08] A displacement trace cannot inherit the meaning of friction or tissue stress.

The tendon–surroundings relation gives the hard/soft model a fair but limited test. Along its length, the tendon can perform a comparatively tensile, force-transmitting, shape-preserving role. At the interface, surrounding tissue can perform a form-receiving role that deforms and permits relative movement. Both are loaded, both move, and neither has one permanent identity. This is the book's interpretation of the relation; the ultrasound studies do not validate the traditional provenance or a body-wide law.

A stronger claim of constraint-maintained loss of excursion would require more than the neutral example provides:

1. a task-specific reference and required freedom;
2. a named surrounding, active, or intrinsic maintaining relation;
3. a change larger than measurement error and ordinary task variation;
4. persistence or reproducible recreation over a stated interval;
5. a response when the proposed maintaining relation changes; and
6. a separately measured functional consequence.

The last two cells remain open here. That is not a weakness to conceal. It makes the example a calibration: it shows what can be measured before the word *loss* is justified and preserves the null branch in which variable relative motion is ordinary.

## Intrinsic, extrinsic, and mixed states make different predictions

Calling every maintained deformation “outside-in compression” would repeat the mistake this chapter is trying to prevent. Three broad accounts must remain available.

In an *extrinsic* account, a current surrounding relation is necessary to maintain the state. Change the contact, support, course, active boundary, or pressure relation, and the model predicts a corresponding change in shape or excursion. That prediction still requires measurement; apparent release in a photograph is not enough.

In an *intrinsic* account, the structure's own material, contents, active state, or retained geometry maintains the state under the conditions being observed. Removing the proposed surrounding term does not restore the comparison state. The current finding does not, by itself, reveal how that intrinsic state developed.

In a *mixed* account, current surroundings and retained internal state interact. A structure may respond differently to an ordinary external demand because its present material state differs, while the surrounding relation still contributes. Neither branch alone predicts the observed behavior.

> **Figure 13.3 — Three maintaining accounts**
>
> | Account | Necessary current term | Informative observation | Required alternative |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Extrinsic | Surrounding contact, traction, pressure, bend, tether, active boundary, or support | State changes with the named surrounding relation | Ordinary task variation or a correlated but noncausal change |
> | Intrinsic | Current material, contents, active state, or retained geometry | State persists when the proposed surrounding term changes | An unmeasured external term or an unsuitable reference |
> | Mixed | Both current surroundings and internal state | Joint model predicts better than either account alone | A simpler single-term or measurement explanation |

These categories describe what maintains the state now, not whether the state is good or bad and not what originally caused it. A current external constraint may be ordinary or protective. An intrinsic state may be transient. A mixed state may adapt without consequence. The categories earn their place by generating different observations, not by making every case fit the book's preferred vocabulary.

## Every downstream arrow must remain visible

Once a maintaining relation and a mechanical state have been distinguished, the argument can proceed—but only one arrow at a time.

For a compliant conduit, the candidate chain is: surrounding and intrinsic boundary conditions alter contact, pressure, bending, or tethering; those terms alter wall stress, strain, course, or cross-sectional geometry; the resulting wall and fluid problem may alter pressure and flow; a separate biological and functional argument would then be needed for any further consequence.

For a sliding interface, the candidate chain is: task and boundaries alter contact, traction, active drive, or path geometry; those terms alter the motion of each structure and their relative excursion; a separate relation would be needed to connect that kinematic difference to friction, work, tissue response, or function. For an expandable boundary, the relevant intermediate might be phase-dependent volume, wall displacement, or pressure rather than lumen. There is no universal dependent variable called “soft-tissue strain.”

At each arrow, the object of measurement changes. Visible position is not contact pressure. Contact pressure is not strain. Strain is not injury. Excursion is not conduction or flow. A functional difference is not a diagnosis, and a symptom cannot retrospectively prove the proposed mechanical route. A mechanically coherent story can therefore remain incomplete in a useful way.

Alternatives belong beside the arrow they challenge. An observed shape may be ordinary for the task. The reference may be unsuitable. An apparent motion difference may be created by image plane or a moving reference tissue. A retained state may be intrinsic rather than externally imposed. Regulation or another available route may preserve function despite local geometry. A change in behavior may precede and produce the observed configuration rather than result from it. These are not generic disclaimers; each one changes what would need to be measured next.

The most informative nulls are equally specific:

- the proposed maintaining term changes, but the geometry or excursion does not;
- geometry changes, but pressure, flow, movement, or the chosen function does not;
- the same downstream finding occurs without the proposed mechanical state;
- ordinary comparison states span the observed difference; or
- a simpler intrinsic, active, task, or measurement account predicts the result as well.

This discipline does not weaken the hypothesis that unresolved structural demand can be expressed as maintained deformation of a form-receiving structure. It identifies what would make that hypothesis more than a compelling picture. The hard/soft formulation contributes a proposed division of labor. The constraint model specifies a maintaining relation. The receiver-specific chain states the consequence to be tested. None is allowed to borrow the evidence of the next.

## From less space to a testable mechanical account

“There is less space” can be an accurate description. It becomes explanatory only when placed inside a larger account: less space compared with what, produced and maintained by which relation, opposed by which restoring tendencies, affecting which material variable or excursion, for how long, and with what separately measured result?

The garden hose makes the causal order easy to see but proves nothing about a living structure. The tendon–subsynovial connective tissue relation removes lumen and flow, showing that excursion can be measured without being declared deficient. The intrinsic, extrinsic, and mixed branches prevent a surrounding-load hypothesis from becoming compulsory. Together they define a mechanical family, not a diagnosis, treatment, or universal rule.

The next chapter examines a different failure family. Instead of a geometry that is maintained, an assembly may repeatedly lose and recover a relationship, recruit participants in the wrong sequence, or spend demand on continual correction. Later chapters compare form-receiving systems and test the present model against a named anatomical corridor. They inherit one obligation from here: explain what maintains the state before treating its shape as its cause.
