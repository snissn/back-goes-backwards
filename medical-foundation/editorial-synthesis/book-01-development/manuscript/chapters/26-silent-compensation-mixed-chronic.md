# Chapter 26 — Silent compensation in a mixed chronic presentation

## A history with no declared cause

Consider a deliberately sparse hypothetical. An adult whose work includes substantial desk and computer tasks cannot identify one decisive injury at the beginning of a low-back-pain history. Symptoms fluctuate for a time and later become more persistent or more important to daily function. The routine findings available in the story do not supply one sufficient cause. Someone looking backward wonders whether the person began completing familiar tasks in a different way before the persistent phase.

This is not a patient, a disguised patient, or a composite assembled from several patients. It is a reasoning construction. Its few details are stipulated so that we can examine what a plausible mechanical history would require. Studies cited in this chapter can show that a component of the construction occurs in a defined population or experiment. They cannot establish that the imagined person has that component, that the components occurred in the proposed order, or that the full sequence is typical.

The construction is sparse for another reason. *Office work* is not yet a mechanical exposure. It says nothing definite about uninterrupted duration, force, support, variation, pace, vibration, task control, recovery opportunity, or movement. *Low-back pain* does not by itself identify a structure, stress field, material state, or causal history. *No decisive finding* does not mean that no recognized condition could later be identified, and it does not make an invisible mechanical explanation more probable.

Current guidance for chronic primary low-back pain places the problem in person-centered, biopsychosocial care rather than assigning it to one mechanical correction.[^26-01] That clinical context does not remove mechanics. It sets the level of responsibility for using mechanics: a proposed history must coexist with biological state, sleep, expectation, behavior, work organization, social constraint, care, and the possibility that another explanation matters more.

The attractive version of the mechanical story is easy to tell. Perhaps demand changed. Perhaps the person preserved work by altering support, motion, timing, or effort. Perhaps the altered strategy became a new starting state, and later demand was distributed differently. Perhaps the resulting exposure contributed to later symptoms or reduced function. Each *perhaps* names a different proposition. A smooth story can hide how little of the interval was observed.

The chapter therefore asks a narrower question: what can a mechanical history legitimately add when the initiating event, internal route, and causal priority are all uncertain? The answer begins with time. A chronic presentation is not one posture, one scan, or one symptom rating. It is a sequence of changing tasks, states, responses, recoveries, and interpretations. Mechanics earns a role in that sequence only at the resolution supplied by chronology and measurement.

## Put time on the page before mechanism

A useful chronology starts with intervals, not a backward arrow from today's symptoms. It separates what happened in work and life from what was physically measured; what the person reports from what another observer records; and what is known about symptoms from what is proposed about internal distribution. Unknown intervals stay unknown.

Low-back-pain courses give good reason to resist a single snapshot. In a primary-care cohort of 1,082 people who reported weekly for one year, different statistical models produced different numbers of course subgroups, and the common patterns were neither uniform recovery nor constant severe pain.[^26-02] The study supports fluctuation and heterogeneity. It does not convert a trajectory into a biological subtype or reveal why one person followed it.

Retrospective summary can also compress away the very changes a mechanical history needs. In a cohort of 719 care-seeking people who supplied weekly ratings for fifty-two weeks and then chose a visual summary of their year, the retrospective choice reflected pain intensity better than episodic, fluctuating, or persistent pattern.[^26-03] A remembered phrase such as “it gradually got worse” can be meaningful and still fail to date episodes, recoveries, exposure changes, or the first appearance of a task strategy.

Recovery does not end the chronology. Among 250 people followed monthly after recovery from a recent low-back-pain episode, the estimated recurrence rate changed with the definition of recurrence: any new episode, an activity-limiting episode, and an episode leading to care were different outcomes.[^26-04] A later episode therefore needs its own definition and exposure window. It cannot automatically be treated as the continuation of one hidden process.

The table does not pretend that every chronic history can be reconstructed. Its purpose is to prevent one kind of information from silently supplying another. Task completion is not a measurement of internal force. Pain is not a measurement of tissue strain. A scan is not a record of prior exposure. A visible configuration is not a dated compensation. If a relevant row was never observed, it can enter the account only as a reconstruction or hypothesis.

Several evidence states can occupy the same date without becoming equivalent. The person might report that a task felt more difficult. A video might show a change in segment motion. A pressure-sensitive surface might measure a change in external contact. A model might estimate a different internal moment from measured inputs. A historian might infer that the motion change helped preserve the task. These are five different objects: report, observation, measurement, calculation, and inference. Their temporal alignment can motivate a relation, but the inference still needs a carrier and a competing explanation.

The same discipline applies to absence. No report of a changed strategy does not show that the strategy was unchanged. No visible change does not show that internal distribution was unchanged. No measured difference under one task does not show that every other task was mechanically identical. But each absence also refuses a positive claim. A possibility that escaped a particular measure remains possible; it does not become more likely because it escaped measurement.

The timeline should also include care and environmental changes. A new chair, a different schedule, time away from work, medication, reassurance, a change in activity, or spontaneous fluctuation could precede a later change in symptoms or function. This chapter does not evaluate any of those as interventions. It records them because an apparent exposure–outcome relation is incomplete when another simultaneous change could account for the transition. The history becomes more useful as it contains more well-dated alternatives, not as it becomes more mechanically elaborate.

That distinction changes how uncertainty behaves. Missing information does not merely reduce confidence in a completed story. It can interrupt the story before the next causal step. If the candidate task change cannot be dated, it cannot establish temporal priority. If the internal strategy was never measured, it cannot be declared persistent. If no local receiver or quantity is identified, “redistribution” has no physical object yet. Chronology is not administrative detail around the mechanism; it is one of the mechanism's necessary conditions.

## Office work is context, not exposure

Occupational measurement guidance separates force, repetition, posture, intensity, frequency, duration, vibration, work organization, and environmental conditions.[^26-05] These are not interchangeable dimensions of one scalar called *load*. A history of desk work may involve long periods of low external force, intermittent carrying, sustained visual demands, constrained reach, variable support, travel, deadlines, or frequent task switching. The relevant combination has to be stated rather than inferred from the occupational label.

Studies of other work make the metric problem concrete. A case-control study in automotive work combined task observation, physical measurement, and modeled lumbar quantities; peak and cumulative exposure metrics did not carry identical information.[^26-06] In a three-year prospective cohort, workers assigned to the highest category of modeled cumulative low-back load had higher odds of low-back pain than those in the lowest category, but the estimates depended on sampled tasks, group assignment, extrapolation, and model assumptions.[^26-07] Among professional drivers, vibration-dose and duration metrics related differently to pain and disability outcomes.[^26-08] None of these populations supplies the imagined office exposure or its internal route. Together, they show why an exposure proposition must name its task, metric, method, and outcome.

Retrospective reconstruction adds another layer of error. In one method comparison, self-report, video observation, and direct measurement differed in precision for selected upper-extremity work exposures, and the best method depended on the quantity being estimated.[^26-09] In a larger occupational cohort, reproducibility of self-reported ergonomic exposure varied with the factor and recall interval and was poorer for most long-term comparisons.[^26-10] A work history remains useful, but its resolution should not improve magically when it enters a causal narrative.

Sitting provides an especially important positive-and-null pair. A systematic review of occupational sitting found no high-quality support for sitting as an independent cause of low-back pain in the worker populations studied and found no persuasive dose-response relation.[^26-11] A later review of reviews likewise found positive and null associations across spinal postures and physical exposures, inconsistent prospective evidence, and no causal consensus.[^26-12] These findings constrain a claim that years at a desk independently produced the presentation. They do not establish that task mechanics never matter.

At a shorter timescale, a review of studies with objectively timed sitting found immediate increases in pain in most included studies, while explicitly declining to infer clinical low-back-pain episodes.[^26-13] Immediate modulation and long-term origin are different propositions. A person can report that symptoms change during a seated task without that observation identifying the original cause, accumulated damage, one harmful posture, or a corrective workstation arrangement.

The hypothetical should therefore be expanded only by questions that create inspectable variables. What task changed? How long was it sustained without interruption? What external contacts and supports were present? What other tasks occurred during the same period? Was variation reduced, or merely remembered that way? Did the symptom course track duration, pace, support, sleep, or work control? Were those relations repeatable, and were competing changes measured? Until such questions are answered, *office work* remains a boundary around possible exposures, not an exposure result.

Even a well-described external exposure does not supply a tissue exposure directly. A duration at a desk, an external force, or a measured trunk angle must still pass through configuration, active state, support, contact, and available paths before it becomes a local force, moment, pressure, stress, strain, or excursion. Different internal strategies can meet a similar external task. The same strategy can have different local consequences when tissue state, fatigue, support, or task phase changes. That translation is the mechanical work of the account; it cannot be replaced by a broad occupational category.

Metric choice also determines what kind of accumulation is being proposed. Summed time, peak force, force–time integral, modeled moment, uninterrupted duration, number of transitions, and opportunity to recover answer different questions. A positive relation for one does not validate the others. If the proposed mechanism concerns sustained deformation, a count of workdays may be a poor proxy. If it concerns repeated high-rate events, total seated time may be nearly beside the point. The metric should follow the proposed carrier and receiver rather than being selected because it is easy to remember.

## What silent compensation would have to mean

*Silent compensation* can sound like a hidden pathology that the framework has special access to. Here it means something more limited: a task-preserving change that was not reported, recognized, or measured at the time. Silence describes the historical record. It does not establish damage, inefficiency, or a later cost.

At this first causal reconstruction, the imagined person remains a hypothetical reasoning device, not a patient or composite. The reconstruction proposes that a strategy changed before persistent symptoms; it does not stipulate that the change occurred. To raise that proposal above an attractive possibility, the account would need a dated pre-symptom observation under a relevant task, a named change in motion, support, activation, contact, pressure, force, or moment, and evidence that the change persisted or recurred across the exposure window.

The first question is what the changed strategy preserved. It might have preserved task completion, reduced immediate discomfort, maintained balance, protected a sensitive relation, accommodated fatigue, or enabled participation under a new constraint. It might also have preserved nothing identifiable. A person can move differently without the change being compensatory in any useful sense.

The book's hard-and-soft vocabulary can organize this question without classifying tissues. The distinction is motivated by traditional Chinese medicine's hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts, but the mechanical roles used here are not definitionally identical to the traditional categories. In a declared interaction, a relatively shape-preserving role maintains a boundary, contact, span, or relation. A relatively form-receiving role yields, conforms, glides, contains, cushions, or accommodates. Bone, muscle, tendon, ligament, fascia, fluid, an external support, and a contact surface can exchange or share these roles with task, direction, phase, and scale.

That polarity gives the hypothetical a sharper question: did maintaining the task require a different structure or assembly to preserve shape, while another element received more deformation, pressure, excursion, or active demand? The answer requires a measured relation. It cannot be inferred because the person sat, appeared asymmetrical, or had pain. Nor does a form-receiving structure become a weak link merely because it yields; yielding may be its capable mechanical contribution.

Reverse order must be equally available. In an experiment with thirty healthy adults, movement-contingent lumbar pain during repeated lifting produced direction-specific movement changes; under one condition, a change remained after the induced pain resolved.[^26-14] In another experiment, pain-related fear predicted reduced lumbar flexion during a reaching task after induced trunk soreness but not before soreness.[^26-15] These experiments do not model a chronic office history. They establish that pain and pain-related state can precede changed movement, which is enough to stop a present movement pattern from being projected backward as an origin.

Changed recruitment can also preserve output. During experimentally induced deep-tissue pain, participants maintained a matched low-force task while recruitment shifted among motor units.[^26-16] The result does not prove benefit, harm, energetic cost, or a chronic compensation. It demonstrates the narrower point that stable external performance need not imply an unchanged internal strategy—and that a changed internal strategy need not imply failed performance.

The six routes are not mutually exclusive for all time. A strategy can begin as useful protection, persist after its original job disappears, and later correspond with a different exposure. Pain can change movement, and the changed movement can then modify subsequent symptoms. A common work or health change can affect both. The question is not which label owns the person. It is which order best explains a defined endpoint over a defined interval, and where feedback changes that order.

This is why *compensation* should be earned rather than assigned. A changed movement is not yet compensation. The account must identify a function, relation, or participation outcome that remained possible because the strategy changed. If nothing identifiable was preserved, the more neutral phrase *changed strategy* is preferable. If preservation is supported, the account can then ask whether the strategy was temporary, retained, task-specific, or generalized—and whether any additional exposure was measured.

Cost is another separate step. More muscle activity under one measurement is not automatically greater mechanical work, metabolic cost, fatigue, tissue damage, or reduced future capacity. Less variation is not automatically harmful, and more variation is not automatically capable. A strategy can consume more of one resource while protecting another relation; it can also use less effort after learning. A cost proposition needs its own denominator, receiver, dose, and time course.

The role can change through time without making the earlier role mistaken. A strategy that was protective during a painful episode may remain after the episode, become unnecessary under one task, and remain useful under another. If later exposure changes, the retained strategy can become newly consequential. Conversely, a strategy that appears effortful can become more efficient with practice or be abandoned without consequence. A chronic account should date these role changes instead of naming the entire history *maladaptation* from its ending.

## Repeated demand meets a changing receiver

Repetition is not a damage counter. The same external task is received by a state that can change with prior demand, recovery, training, illness, sleep, medication, pain, sensitivity, confidence, environmental support, and participation. The relevant capacity is the current ability to tolerate and recover from a defined exposure, not a fixed amount possessed by a person or tissue.

Demand and capacity are therefore multidimensional. An account might track uninterrupted task duration, peak force, cumulative modeled moment, movement variation, local excursion, task endurance, sleep opportunity, symptom-limited function, or recovery time. Those quantities cannot be added into one invented curve without a defensible model. A rise in one component can coexist with improvement in another. More exposure can coincide with greater capacity; reduced exposure can coincide with poorer function; a repeated task can produce adaptation, stable tolerance, fluctuation, recovery, or an adverse response.

Material state can also depend on loading history and measurement time without settling clinical meaning. Under specified ex vivo axial loading and unloading, human lumbar discs showed time-dependent creep and recovery, with recovery proceeding more slowly than loading under the protocol.[^26-17] In a small same-day imaging study of sedentary workers, selected disc-composition indices differed between morning and evening scans.[^26-18] These findings support time-sensitive material description. They do not establish that ordinary sitting damaged a disc, caused pain, or dictates a recovery interval for a person.

Sleep belongs within the chronology because it can change both response and interpretation. In a seven-day diary study of people with low-back pain, poorer sleep measures preceded higher next-day pain, and pain preceded selected subsequent sleep measures; the result depended partly on how sleep was measured.[^26-19] In a separate fourteen-day intensive diary study, poorer prior-night sleep preceded next-day differences in pain, function, and catastrophizing, especially earlier in the day.[^26-20] These are short observational windows, not a complete causal model. They do show why sleep cannot be reduced to a final qualifier after the mechanical story has already been chosen.

Work context is similarly active. A large cross-sectional survey found associations among low-back pain and selected physical, psychosocial, and organizational work variables, but its design could not assign temporal order.[^26-21] A systematic review of occupational psychosocial exposures found small pooled associations, very low certainty, and substantial methodological limitations.[^26-22] Work control, pace, job security, economic constraint, caregiving, and access to rest may change exposure, recovery opportunity, reporting, and available choices. Some may matter little in a particular history. The point is to measure their proposed role rather than invoke “stress” as either a universal cause or an irrelevant distraction.

This interactive model allows causal priority to change. Pain can reduce activity, which changes future exposure and capacity. Sleep disruption can follow pain and then affect next-day function. A demanding period can alter both task behavior and recovery. Care can change symptoms without changing the proposed mechanical variable. Training can increase capacity despite continued repetition. An account that insists on one arrow for the entire history is often less mechanical, not more: it ignores that the system receiving demand has changed.

The term *mechanical homeostasis* can help if it is kept dynamic. It names the maintenance of relevant variables within viable ranges while demand and state change. It does not name one posture, one internal route, or a fixed set point. In the hypothetical, a viable range might concern task completion, local excursion, pressure, repeated-force tolerance, or recovery over a declared interval. A variable can leave one range briefly and return; another can remain altered without an adverse outcome. The range and consequence must be defined rather than inferred from visual alignment.

Local response is another row, not the inevitable consequence of exposure. A tissue or interface can accommodate, recover, remodel, sensitize, remain unchanged, or respond adversely depending on the quantity, magnitude, rate, repetition, duration, material state, and recovery context. The chapter does not assign any such response to the imagined person. It insists only that a medical interpretation requires this intermediate link. Jumping from a work history to a symptom skips both internal distribution and biological response.

Capacity also belongs to a receiver and an outcome. Task endurance, tolerance of a local pressure, recovery of a material deformation, confidence during participation, and ability to vary a strategy are not one reservoir. A person may gain capacity in one domain while losing it in another. The phrase *low resilience* therefore cannot function as an unexplained global variable here. The account has to say what recovers, after which exposure, over what interval, and by what observation.

## The regional bridge that is not there

The original intuition behind the hypothetical includes a local workaround becoming a wider reorganization. That is a legitimate research question. The present construction does not establish it. No task-specific observation connects a proposed lumbopelvic change to a thoracolumbar, hip, contralateral, or distal receiver, and no physical quantity has been measured along such a route.

A regional proposition needs more than coexisting differences. It must identify the local state, the task and boundary conditions, the carrier, the physical quantity, the receiving region, the timescale, the observation or model, and a result that would stop the proposed link. Without those fields, a line drawn across a body is not an internal force path. It is an invitation to define one.

An experimental gait study illustrates the required structure without supplying this chapter's route. Seventeen healthy men walked normally and with experimentally restricted right-knee extension. Measured motion and ground-reaction data entered a musculoskeletal model that estimated named lumbar moments, muscle forces, and joint-reaction forces; selected modeled quantities changed with the imposed restriction.[^26-23] The study defined a manipulation, task, boundary condition, intermediate measurements, model, receiver, and quantities. It did not study office work, spontaneous compensation, chronic pain origin, tissue strain, delayed harm, or treatment.

The correct response to the missing bridge is not to abandon cross-scale mechanics or to complete the route with anatomical plausibility. It is to state what would have to be observed. A future study might define a repeated desk task, manipulate a support or task demand, measure segment motion and external contact, estimate an internal quantity under explicit assumptions, identify a local and regional receiver, and follow the relevant response. A null difference, a model-sensitive result, or a route that fails to track the course would then be informative.

For this hypothetical, the route stops. That stopping point protects rather than diminishes the wider project. It keeps a possible whole-body reorganization available as a testable program while preventing an unmeasured kinetic chain from becoming the explanation of one person's symptoms.

The stopping rule applies even when the proposed anatomy is continuous. Fascial continuity, muscular attachment, articular contact, and shared neural control can make a regional influence possible without identifying which quantity was transmitted during the task. Anatomical connection is not a measurement of force distribution, and synchronized motion is not a measurement of tissue stress. A cross-region model becomes stronger when it names several carriers and tests which one, if any, accounts for the receiving quantity.

The term *force line* requires the same care. It can refer to an external line of action, an inferred relation among several internal carriers, or an embodied experience of continuity. Only the first has a straightforward geometric definition from an applied force. The second requires a model and measurements; the third can motivate a hypothesis but is not itself a force measurement. The hypothetical supplies none of these as a regional result.

This does not require the body to behave as disconnected parts. It requires a whole-body claim to contain whole-body evidence at the relevant scale. A local result can remain meaningful when the regional arrow stops. Conversely, a measured regional change can be real without establishing harm, chronic origin, or a later symptom. Each successful bridge earns one step, not the remainder of the chain.

## Findings, symptoms, and mechanism do not collapse

Routine findings can matter without settling the history. A review of thirty-three studies including 3,110 asymptomatic people found that several spinal degenerative imaging features were common and increased with age.[^26-24] A separate meta-analysis of adults aged fifty or younger found that selected MRI features were more prevalent in symptomatic groups, while other comparisons were null or imprecise.[^26-25] These findings belong together. Common asymptomatic findings prevent a scan feature from identifying an individual pain source by itself; group differences prevent “common” from meaning “always irrelevant.”

The hypothetical does not invent a scan, examination result, or laboratory value. It stipulates only that the available routine findings have not selected one sufficient cause. Before an actual finding could alter the competing routes, the account would need the method, indication, timing, anatomical object, comparison, uncertainty, and clinical interpretation. It would also need to say which arrow the finding addresses.

Symptoms without a decisive structural finding do not establish an unmeasured mechanical route. The failure of a routine measure to observe a proposed quantity can mean that the quantity was not measured, that it was measured poorly, that it was absent, or that it does not matter to the declared endpoint. Poor observability motivates a measurement problem; it does not count as positive evidence.

The converse boundary matters as well. A present finding cannot reconstruct years of exposure, and persistence of a finding after functional improvement would not prove that the finding is currently causal. Symptoms can change while structure appears stable, and a selected structure can change without symptoms tracking it. The relevant objects—exposure, internal distribution, local response, finding, symptom, function, and clinical interpretation—must remain separate even when they correspond.

This separation also disciplines task modulation. If symptoms reliably change with a task, support, duration, or state, the observation can raise the priority of a mechanical contribution at that timescale. It may help identify a relevant boundary condition or capacity relation. It does not by itself establish the first cause, the tissue involved, the regional route, or the appropriate response. Attention, expectation, generalized sensitivity, fatigue, and simultaneous changes in several physical quantities can predict modulation too.

The tissue-response bridge, when proposed, must be more specific than “mechanics affected biology.” It should name the receiver, local quantity, material or physiological response, timing, and observed endpoint. A modeled joint moment is not local tissue stress. Local stress is not tissue injury. A tissue response is not a symptom. A symptom is not a diagnosis. These propositions can connect, but every connection needs evidence appropriate to its scale and timeframe.

Nor should a biological account be used only to absorb unexplained variance after the mechanical route has been drawn. Inflammation, disease state, medication, sleep, sensitivity, and prior injury can change the receiver before the exposure occurs. They can change behavior and task selection, alter recovery, or produce the movement pattern being interpreted. Biological and psychological variables are therefore upstream, downstream, and feedback variables according to the history—not competing decorations around a privileged mechanical cause.

This makes a mismatch productive. If symptoms change while the proposed exposure does not, the mechanical route loses priority for that change. If an exposure changes without the predicted response, the exposure-to-response bridge weakens. If a finding corresponds at group level but not across the person's course, the finding may still matter clinically while contributing little to this causal reconstruction. Noncorrespondence tells us which arrow failed; it does not require an all-or-nothing verdict about mechanics.

## Six legitimate endings

The hypothetical does not have enough information to select an ending. That is intentional. Its purpose is to show the evidence pattern each ending would require and to make clear that a mechanical method has not failed when mechanics receives a partial or low ranking.

**A substantial mechanical contribution** would require more than a plausible desk history. A relevant task or exposure change would precede the declared transition. A task-preserving strategy would be dated and repeatable. A named carrier and receiver quantity would change under the relevant conditions. The proposed quantity would correspond with local response, symptoms, function, or recovery with useful specificity, while competing orders were actually measured. This conclusion would give mechanics high priority for that endpoint and interval. It would not establish a diagnosis, a total cause, or a preferred correction.

**A mixed contribution** would arise when mechanical and whole-person variables share the work. Perhaps task duration and support correspond with symptom fluctuation, while sleep, health, expectation, and work control also precede important changes. Perhaps a strategy alters local exposure but began partly in response to pain. The result would be an interactive account in which mechanical organization is consequential without being independently sufficient or temporally first at every link.

**Reverse-causal priority** would be appropriate if pain, illness, sleep disruption, fear, medication, or another state clearly preceded the changed strategy and the strategy tracked that state. Mechanics would still describe how the person responded and how later demand was received. It would not deserve primary status as the origin of the endpoint under consideration.

**Adaptive or successful compensation** would be supported if a changed strategy followed a perturbation or constraint, preserved the declared task or participation, and carried no demonstrated adverse consequence over the observed dose and interval. The absence of a measured cost cannot prove that no later cost is possible. It does prevent a speculative future cost from overruling observed success. Protection comes before cost in the interpretation.

**Low mechanical priority** would be warranted if the selected exposure or strategy failed to track the course, task modulation was weak or nonspecific, no defensible carrier or receiver was found, and another account explained the chronology and function better. This is a local result for a stated endpoint and period. It does not imply that bodies cease to be mechanical or that mechanics is irrelevant in other histories.

**Unresolved plurality** would remain when dates are absent, measures conflict, several causal orders predict the same observations, or the regional bridge cannot be observed with adequate validity. The route may be worth studying, but no ranking is licensed. Unknown remains unknown rather than becoming indirect support for the most coherent story.

> | Reverse-causal priority | Pain, illness, or another state precedes strategy | Strategy tracks the preceding state | Mechanical change is response or mediator | Mechanics describes consequence more than primary origin |
> | Adaptive compensation | Changed strategy preserves a declared function | Output, protection, or participation is maintained | No relevant adverse consequence is demonstrated over the interval | The change is compatible with successful adaptation |
> | Low mechanical priority | Proposed mechanical history does not track the transition | Weak, null, or nonspecific | No defensible route; another account explains more | Routing adds little here at present |
> | Unresolved plurality | Dates or measures are absent or conflicting | Incomplete | Several routes remain observationally equivalent | No causal ranking is warranted |
>
> **Caption.** The same method can produce six evidence-proportionate conclusions. None is a diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment selector, and the substantial branch is not the default destination.
>
> **Text alternative.** A six-row matrix compares chronology, task-specific measurement, route support, and the permitted conclusion for substantial, mixed, reverse-causal, adaptive, low-priority, and unresolved outcomes.

These endings clarify what a prespecified null looks like. Route A loses priority if the strategy appears only after symptoms, if the proposed quantity does not change under the task, if the same strategy occurs without the course, or if the course changes while the quantity does not. A regional proposal stops if it has no carrier, if its modeled output is too assumption-sensitive, or if the receiving quantity fails to correspond. A cumulative-demand account weakens if adaptation and recovery explain the sequence better than retained cost.

The endings also prevent false competition between mechanical and whole-person accounts. Reverse causation can still produce mechanically important consequences. A shared context can alter both physical exposure and sensitivity. Successful adaptation is a mechanical result. Low priority for one endpoint is not low priority for every endpoint. The comparison asks what each model explains, what it predicts, and where it stops.

The ranking should be link-specific. A history could support a substantial mechanical contribution to immediate task modulation, a mixed account of recurrent symptoms, reverse-causal priority for a later movement change, and no conclusion about tissue degeneration. Assigning one global label would throw away that resolution. The same evidence can raise one route and lower another because the routes address different intervals or endpoints.

A good comparison also declares what would change its mind. If prospective observation shows that the proposed strategy begins only after pain, Route A should lose temporal priority. If a manipulated task variable reliably changes a named receiver quantity but not symptoms, the mechanical model gains at one link and fails at another. If symptoms improve while the proposed mechanical variable remains stable, the variable may still describe the body but explains less of that improvement. Disfavoring results refine the map instead of threatening the existence of the framework.

Finally, the unresolved ending has content. It identifies the dates, quantities, receivers, comparators, and methods that are missing, and it distinguishes gaps that could be closed from propositions that remain too vague to test. “More research” is not the conclusion. The conclusion is which observation would discriminate the routes and which claimed arrow must remain undrawn until then.

## What the lens added

The person in this chapter has never been a patient or a composite. The hypothetical was built to expose a reasoning problem, and it remains deliberately unresolved. No silent compensation, regional redistribution, tissue response, imaging mechanism, or original cause has been assigned to anyone.

The mechanical lens nevertheless added something concrete. It turned *office work* into a set of task and exposure variables. It turned *silent compensation* from hidden damage into one temporal hypothesis among six. It required task preservation to be named before cost, and it preserved shape-preserving and form-receiving roles without assigning permanent identities to tissues. It refused to draw a regional route across a missing carrier. It placed sleep, health, expectation, work control, and social constraint inside the causal graph. It separated findings, symptoms, function, and mechanism. Most importantly, it made substantial, mixed, reverse-causal, adaptive, low-priority, and unresolved conclusions equally legitimate products of the method.

That is the completion of Part V. The four worked narratives have not converged on one mechanical answer. They have shown how geometry, maintained constraint, instability under repeated demand, and an ambiguous chronic history require different evidence and stop at different links. Part VI can now ask how to carry this discipline into a portable sequence of questions—one that improves inquiry without becoming a score, a hidden diagnosis, or a treatment system.
