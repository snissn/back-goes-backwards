# Chapter 20 — Comparing conduits, nerves, sliding interfaces, and pressure systems

Place six living structures side by side. A blood vessel changes caliber as its wall and surroundings change. A collecting lymphatic fills, contracts, opens and closes valves, and propels fluid. A peripheral nerve moves through its bed as nearby joints move. A finger tendon glides relative to the connective tissue around it. An upper airway changes shape across pressure, posture, muscle activity, and state. An abdominal boundary deforms while pressure rises and falls within it.

Now impose what looks, in a simple drawing, like the same surrounding change: two boundaries move closer together. It is tempting to call all six objects compressed and then to infer one common consequence. But the drawing has not yet told us what matters. A vessel may need a pressure–flow relation to remain within a functional range. A lymphatic needs filling, valve action, and propulsion. A nerve needs excursion and physiological integrity. A tendon interface needs relative glide. An airway needs a viable relation among caliber, pressure, resistance, flow, and active control. A cavity boundary needs to contain and redistribute pressure while changing shape.

This is a schematic comparison, not a depiction of disease. Each structure normally deforms. Each may restore, actively compensate, or function adequately despite a conspicuous shape change. Each can also be limited by a relation that persists. The useful common question is therefore not, “Is the soft structure deformed?” It is: **What bounded freedom does this structure need for this task, what relation could limit that freedom, and what receiver-specific output would show that the limit mattered?**

That question develops one of this book’s central intuitions. Load wants to travel through the hard body so that the soft body can remain soft. The sentence is deliberate shorthand, not a claim that force has intention or that one class of tissue should carry all demand. Its mechanical meaning is a proposed division of labor. For selected tasks, shape-organizing relations may carry or distribute enough demand that form-receiving structures retain the yield, caliber, excursion, glide, containment, or pressure accommodation their functions require.

The comparison is strongest when it remains capable of separating six different answers—and of finding that mechanics has low priority or no consequential effect in a particular case.

## Hard and soft describe roles in a relation

Earlier chapters introduced *shape-preserving* and *form-receiving* as flexible mechanical roles. The distinction is motivated by traditional Chinese medicine’s hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts. Book I develops a mechanical translation of that motivation; it does not claim that the traditional and mechanical categories are definitionally identical.

The roles belong to a task, frame, direction, and interval. They are not painted permanently onto anatomical materials. Bone often helps preserve regional shape, yet bone deforms and can receive load from its surroundings. Tendon can carry large tensile force and help organize a joint while also participating in an interface that must yield enough for relative motion. Muscle, fascia, fluid, and pressure can transmit demand or help preserve form. A vessel wall is compliant relative to one surrounding boundary and shape-preserving relative to the fluid it contains. The same structure can change roles between phases of one task.

Form-receiving therefore does not mean weak, passive, unloaded, or unimportant. Yield is often the work. A vein must change shape. A nerve must move relative to its bed. A tendon and its surrounding connective tissue must share motion without becoming one rigid body. An abdominal wall must deform while containing a changing pressure field. The relevant contrast is not force versus no force. It is the division of mechanical work between relations that organize shape and relations that accommodate change.

The shorthand about load traveling through the hard body can now be stated as a hypothesis with conditions. Under a declared demand, a shape-organizing relation may preserve a receiver’s necessary freedom better than a comparator relation does. Better must name an output: more usable excursion, an appropriate caliber response, preserved relative glide, a pressure–volume relation compatible with the task, or another measured function. The hypothesis does not say that maximum softness is best. An airway that is too collapsible, a valve that cannot maintain closure, or a boundary that cannot contain pressure demonstrates why form-receiving freedom must be controlled rather than unlimited.

This formulation also creates a valid negative result. If ordinary variables—geometry, material state, pressure, contact, active control, and task—fully explain the selected output, hard/soft may remain useful language without adding a distinct predictive model. If a proposed shape-organizing route bears no reliable relation to the receiver’s freedom, the hypothesis narrows for that system. Its value comes from discriminating relations, not from renaming every interaction.

## Controlled freedom begins with the maintaining relation

Controlled freedom is the bounded deformation or relative motion a structure needs for a named function. The word *controlled* matters. A vessel does not require maximum caliber at every moment. A lymphatic does not require absence of external pressure. A nerve does not need unlimited translation. A tendon interface does not need frictionless motion. An airway does not need maximal openness regardless of phase and state. A cavity does not need the lowest possible pressure. Each system operates through changing boundary conditions.

When a surrounding mechanical account is proposed, the sequence begins before the visible narrowing. The external relation applies contact pressure, bending, tension, tethering, or another boundary condition. The receiver deforms or loses excursion. Its own mechanical output may then change. Only after that come any tissue response, functional effect, finding, symptom, or clinical interpretation.

The garden hose is useful for this order and little else. A hose held in a kink remains deformed because the surrounding relation maintains bending and contact. The smaller internal passage is an intermediate geometry; it is not an independent agent that reached out and reduced the flow. Remove the maintaining relation and an elastic hose may restore. Keep it long enough, damage the wall, change its material state, or place it under different internal pressure, and the later behavior can differ.

Living structures add active muscle, valves, internal and external pressure, anchoring, growth, remodeling, inflammation, perfusion, nervous control, changing tasks, and redundant routes. A visible constriction does not reveal how much stress the wall carries. It does not establish the pressure gradient, flow, transport, signaling, or function. It also does not show whether the cause is outside the receiver. The hose establishes only that, in an extrinsically maintained chain, the imposed mechanical relation precedes the lumen change and any consequent transport effect.

This order resolves an otherwise subtle mistake. “Loss of space” often sounds like the active mechanism. Space is a geometric description. In an extrinsic account, a relation among surrounding structures produces stress, strain, contact, bending, or tethering in the receiver and maintains the changed geometry. The consequential object may be wall deformation, local pressure, lost excursion, altered shear, or another receiver-specific quantity. Less available space can help describe that relation, but it cannot replace it.

The complete chain has several independent links:

**maintaining relation → receiver deformation or lost freedom → receiver-specific mechanical output → biological or functional response → possible finding or symptom**

Any link can be absent. The deformation can restore. The output can remain adequate. Regulation or another route can compensate. A measured output can change without a consequential biological response. A symptom can have another origin. The purpose of the sequence is not to make the mechanical story longer. It is to keep each claim from borrowing certainty from the one before it.

The distinction also changes how stress and strain enter the account. A surrounding structure does not simply occupy space beside a receiver. Through contact, attachment, pressure, or movement, it can impose a force or moment that produces stress within the receiver and deformation relative to a reference state. The observed lumen or corridor is one consequence of that mechanical relation. It may be an important measurement, but it does not tell us the full stress field, the receiver’s material response, or which neighboring structure supplied the demand. In another task, internal pressure or active contraction can oppose the same external relation and produce a different geometry.

This is the precise content behind the claim that load should be organized through the hard role. The proposal is not that demand disappears from form-receiving structures. It is that their exposure should remain compatible with the deformation their function requires. A vessel wall still bears stress; a nerve still accommodates strain; a sliding interface still carries shear; a cavity wall still receives pressure traction. The division of labor concerns the kind, distribution, magnitude, and duration of that exposure, not a fantasy of unloaded tissue.

Controlled freedom therefore has two sides. The receiver needs enough motion or deformation to perform its work, and the surrounding system needs enough organization to keep that motion within a viable task-specific range. The chapter can compare these sides without defining the ideal range. It asks which quantities would be needed to do so.

## One observed state can have four histories

Persistence needs its own classification because the same image can follow different histories.

A **transient and restored** state is ordinary deformation followed by adequate return when the phase, position, contact, or pressure changes. A **maintained extrinsic** state continues because a surrounding boundary, repeated relation, tether, contact, or external pressure continues to impose or recreate it. An **intrinsically retained** state persists because the receiver’s own material, wall, tone, swelling, lesion, thrombosis, scar, adhesion, or other system-specific condition now maintains it. A **mixed** state develops when an external exposure changes the receiver, or an intrinsic change alters how the surroundings load it.

A response to repositioning answers a narrow question: the selected change modulated the selected output at that time. It does not prove what initiated the state. Conversely, persistence after repositioning does not prove intrinsic disease. The proposed boundary may not actually have changed, another external relation may remain, the interval may be too short, or the measurement may be insensitive. Time, dose, and receiver state decide what the comparison can mean.

Restoring behavior is therefore something to observe, not something to assume from the word *elastic*. A useful perturbation must specify the relation that changed, the amount and duration of that change, the variable measured before and after it, and the interval over which restoration was expected. Immediate return, delayed return, partial return, overshoot, and no detectable return describe different histories. Repeated tasks can recreate an extrinsic relation faster than a receiver restores, making a state appear continuous even when it changes between repetitions. Conversely, one brief position can make a normally variable structure look fixed when the relevant range was never sampled.

The same time logic applies to mixed states. An external relation can precede a material response, but the material response cannot be inferred from duration alone. An intrinsic change can make an ordinary surrounding relation newly consequential, but chronology alone does not quantify its contribution. The history becomes informative when at least one observation distinguishes what changes with the boundary from what remains in the receiver.

This classification prevents the hard/soft hypothesis from absorbing every persistent finding. If an intrinsic wall state dominates a vessel’s caliber, a surrounding shape-organizing route may have low explanatory priority. If ordinary movement changes a nerve’s position and it restores without a physiological consequence, the deformation is function. If repeated external contact has altered an interface and the material change now limits glide, the history may be mixed even after the original task stops.

The next comparisons therefore ask the same four questions in each system: What freedom is required? What maintains the observed state? What output belongs to this receiver? What alternative or null would defeat the proposed account?

## Blood vessels and lymphatics are two different transport systems

Blood vessels make the hose analogy tempting because they have a lumen and carry fluid. Even here, pressure–flow behavior is not a simple conversion from area to consequence.

Experimental collapsible tubes show that external pressure, wall behavior, upstream and downstream pressures, geometry, and flow can form nonlinear relations. Low and Chew observed flow limitation during a collapsed phase in a declared apparatus, including conditions in which further increases in driving pressure did not yield the expected increase in flow.[^20-01] Griffin and colleagues tested water moving through an unsupported silicone tube under a gravity-generated pressure gradient. In that apparatus, the lumen-area reduction had to become very large before flow fell by more than half, and changing the tested length of a 74 percent area reduction did not materially change mean flow.[^20-02]

These studies clarify what must be specified: the material, internal and external pressures, boundary support, geometry, fluid, and driving conditions. They do not reproduce pulsatile blood, vascular tone, branching, collateral routes, autoregulation, or living perfusion. The lesson is not that narrowing is harmless or harmful. It is that the magnitude and even the form of the pressure–flow result belong to a defined system.

Healthy positional studies add another constraint. In nineteen asymptomatic participants, provocative arm positions produced Doppler waveforms that a study protocol classified as significant arterial compression in at least one position in 28.9 percent of participants. The authors emphasized the resulting false-positive problem for arterial thoracic-outlet assessment.[^20-03] In a different study of forty-nine healthy volunteers, a specified abducted, flexed, and externally rotated arm position increased the mean cross-sectional area of the subclavian vein rather than narrowing it.[^20-04] The protocols, vessels, and outputs differ, so the findings should not be pooled. Together they demonstrate position-dependent geometry and waveform variation without allowing position alone to become diagnosis, benefit, or harm.

A vascular account therefore separates at least five stages. A surrounding relation interacts with wall state and internal and external pressure. Lumen and wall geometry change. Local gradient, resistance, waveform, or flow may change. Collateral and regulatory responses may modify the result. Tissue perfusion, venous return, or a clinical consequence requires additional evidence. Wall disease, thrombosis, valves, systemic pressure, volume state, autonomic regulation, and ordinary anatomy remain alternative explanations at different stages.

Lymphatics cannot inherit that chain merely because some lymphatic structures also have a lumen. Collecting lymphatics possess valves and active wall contraction. Filling, ejection, reflux, and propulsion depend on intrinsic pumping, pressure relations, tissue-fluid input, movement, and network state.

In isolated rat mesenteric lymphangions, Davis and colleagues independently controlled input and output pressures while measuring intralymphangion pressure, diameter, valve position, and contraction. Contractility increased over time as afterload rose, and segments with different strength reached pump limits differently.[^20-05] In another isolated-vessel study, selectively increasing downstream pressure produced pressure-dependent constriction and changes in valve behavior.[^20-06] A smaller diameter in these preparations could therefore be part of an active response, not evidence that a passive tube had simply been crushed from outside.

External movement can also assist transport. In eight men, dynamic and isometric knee-extension tasks increased clearance of radiolabeled albumin from an injection region in the vastus lateralis compared with rest, with differences between the tested muscle configurations.[^20-07] The measure was regional tracer clearance, not complete network flow, fluid balance, or a clinical endpoint. It nevertheless shows why “external load” cannot be assigned one sign in advance. Surrounding deformation and muscle activity can participate in filling and propulsion as well as obstruction.

For lymphatic transport, the controlled freedom includes filling, wall contraction, valve operation, and movement within a changing tissue-pressure environment. The relevant outputs include segment diameter, ejection, reflux, propulsion, pump work, regional clearance, and, at a wider scale, network transport. Increased afterload can provoke compensation before failure. An intrinsic or systemic lymphatic state can dominate. A local measurement can miss another route. The hard/soft question is whether the surrounding division of labor preserves or disrupts those specific freedoms under the task—not whether all pressure on a lymphatic is adverse.

The vessel and lymphatic rows thus share a question about form-receiving structures within surrounding boundaries. They do not share equations, thresholds, active control, or medical conclusions.

They also show why transport is not one endpoint. A local lumen, waveform, segment contraction, tracer-clearance rate, network throughput, tissue-fluid balance, and organ-level consequence occupy different scales. A change at one scale can motivate the next measurement without establishing it. A study that measures a local geometric response may be exactly adequate for a local mechanical question and wholly inadequate for a claim about downstream tissue state. The honest result is not “mechanics does not matter” or “transport is impaired.” It is that one link has been measured and the next remains open.

## Nerves require a different mechanical grammar

A peripheral nerve is not an electrical hose. It is a living, vascularized, viscoelastic structure that moves relative to surrounding tissue, experiences contact and strain, contains intraneural circulation and axonal transport, and carries signals. Those quantities can interact, but none is a synonym for another.

Ordinary movement establishes the positive function first. In healthy participants, Dilley and colleagues used ultrasound to measure median-nerve excursion during movements of the wrist, elbow, shoulder, and neck. Excursion varied with the maneuver and measurement location, while additional forearm strain remained small in several tested movements.[^20-08] A nerve’s changing position is not, by itself, tethering or entrapment. It is part of how the nerve accommodates movement across several interfaces.

Controlled compression supplies a different link. Rydevik, Lundborg, and Bagge directly observed intraneural microcirculation in exposed rabbit tibial nerves while applying graded local pressure. Venular, capillary, and arteriolar flow changed at different pressures, and high, prolonged compression produced persistent impairment under those experimental conditions.[^20-09] This is direct evidence that sufficiently imposed pressure can change intraneural blood flow in that preparation. It does not supply a human postural threshold, show that reduced visible space produced the same local pressure, or explain a symptom.

A neural chain must therefore keep joint motion, bed length, tether, contact, and applied pressure upstream of excursion, elongation, transverse deformation, local strain, and shear. Those mechanical outputs remain separate from intraneural pressure, circulation, axonal transport, and conduction. Sensory or motor function comes later; a symptom or diagnosis later still.

Less visible excursion can mean several things. The nerve may begin with different slack. The reference tissue may move. Contact may redirect rather than prevent motion. Intrinsic swelling or material change may alter the response. Pain or neural sensitivity may change the person’s movement before the measured excursion changes, reversing the assumed direction. Probe pressure, image-plane loss, and protocol differences can alter the measurement. A corridor can make a neural-mechanics question intelligible without establishing neuropathy.

The shape-organizing hypothesis remains meaningful here. A surrounding relation could preserve a nerve’s ability to move and distribute strain, or it could maintain contact and reduce that freedom. But the required observation is neural: excursion, strain, pressure, circulation, conduction, or another defined output. Vessel evidence cannot fill that cell.

## Sliding interfaces test the idea without a lumen

The flexor tendon and subsynovial connective tissue in the carpal tunnel provide a decisive comparison because the primary freedom is relative glide, not patency.

In twenty healthy wrists, Yoshii and colleagues tracked the middle-finger flexor tendon and adjacent subsynovial connective tissue during gripping around three cylinder diameters. Both structures moved, and the relative-motion pattern changed with task geometry.[^20-10] The result establishes task-dependent co-motion. It does not establish one ideal amount of glide or a pathological threshold.

A cadaver experiment makes the measurement distinction sharper. Across wrist postures, tendon forces, and movement velocities, ultrasound-derived relative displacement and mechanically measured frictional work did not behave as interchangeable quantities.[^20-11] Reduced relative displacement is not automatically a measure of shear stress, adhesion, tissue damage, capacity, pain, or functional loss.

The chain begins with tendon drive, joint and task geometry, contact, and the material state of the surrounding interface. Tendon excursion and surrounding-tissue excursion are measured separately. Relative glide, shear, and frictional work require their own definitions and methods. Finger motion or another declared function follows. A tissue response or symptom requires another bridge.

Two nulls matter. Geometry can change while usable relative motion remains. Relative motion can be reduced primarily by an intrinsic adhesion or material state rather than an ongoing skeletal boundary relation. Tendon itself can carry high tensile demand while the tendon–surroundings relation remains form-receiving in a transverse or sliding direction. This is why “soft” cannot mean unloaded and why controlled freedom transfers better than the hose.

## Airways and cavities expose the limits of comparison

An upper airway again has a lumen, but it is an actively controlled collapsible passage. Its behavior depends on wall and surrounding tissue, head and neck geometry, lung volume, internal and external pressure, sleep or anesthetic state, and pharyngeal muscle activity.

Walsh and colleagues studied fifteen healthy volunteers under propofol anesthesia while measuring pharyngeal critical closing pressure with the head flexed, neutral, extended, or rotated. Three participants were excluded from the main analysis because genioglossus activity persisted. Under the analyzed passive conditions, flexion increased collapsibility and extension reduced it relative to neutral; rotation did not show the same group effect, and the site of collapse varied in some participants.[^20-12] This is direct evidence that a surrounding geometric relation changed a defined airway-mechanics output under a narrow protocol. It is not a general posture recommendation or an explanation of obstructive sleep apnea.

Even the apparent passivity is state-dependent. In adults with and without obstructive sleep apnea, Carberry and colleagues found differences in critical closing pressure and genioglossus activity across selected sleep stages. Pharyngeal muscle activity also increased after the pressure reductions used to estimate critical closing pressure.[^20-13] Measurement and active response interacted. Airway caliber, critical pressure, resistance, airflow, gas exchange, sleep state, and clinical outcome must remain separate nodes.

Pressure-bearing cavities stretch the comparison further because their principal output is neither lumen flow nor relative glide. Pressure acts as distributed traction on a boundary. The result depends on the pressure difference, geometry, volume, wall behavior, active muscle, contained material, external demand, and phase.

In a bounded experiment involving three prone participants, experimentally elevated intra-abdominal pressure increased a measured lumbar stiffness response.[^20-14] That small study provides an existence proof that pressure can contribute to support; it does not establish a breathing method, an optimal pressure, or a whole-body organizing driver. Pressure is not merely a threat to soft structures. It can participate in shape preservation.

Ordinary activity demonstrates wide variation. Using an ingestible intragastric sensor, Soucasse and colleagues measured pressure during supervised tasks and daily life in twenty healthy volunteers. Peak values and rates differed by task, and brief high values occurred during ordinary activity.[^20-15] The sensor supplied a pressure proxy over time. It did not measure abdominal-wall stress, spinal support, pelvic-floor load, organ deformation, injury, or an ideal range.

Joppin and colleagues combined dynamic magnetic resonance imaging with an ingestible pressure sensor in eighteen healthy participants during breathing, coughing, and Valsalva tasks. Relations between abdominal-wall displacement and pressure varied across people, tasks, and muscle regions, producing widely varying estimates of pseudo-compliance.[^20-16] One pressure value did not specify one boundary deformation. One visible expansion did not recover the pressure field or the distribution of mechanical work.

For a cavity, controlled freedom means enough deformation and pressure accommodation to perform a declared function while the boundary also contains and shares demand. A compliant boundary may be appropriate; greater stiffness may preserve shape in another phase. High pressure can be brief and ordinary. Low pressure does not automatically mean inadequate support. The comparison can identify pressure, displacement, volume, boundary stiffness, and task as measurable objects. It cannot derive pelvic, respiratory, spinal, or systemic health from them without additional evidence.

The matrix blocks several seductive substitutions. Geometry is not lumen. Lumen is not flow. Pressure is not one body-wide scalar. Neural excursion is not strain, and strain is not signaling. Relative tendon motion is not frictional work. Airway critical pressure is not gas exchange. An abdominal pressure proxy is not wall stress or organ load.

These separations do more than constrain language. They determine study design. If the hypothesis concerns an external maintaining relation, measuring only the receiver’s final geometry leaves the proposed input unmeasured. If it concerns lost freedom, a static image may miss excursion and restoration. If it concerns transport or signaling, geometry alone stops too early. If it concerns a symptom, a mechanical output and a symptom score do not by themselves establish the biological and temporal links between them. Each additional claim needs an observation appropriate to that arrow.

The matrix is also allowed to remain asymmetric. Some rows have tractable local measures and controlled perturbations; others have indirect proxies, changing active state, or inaccessible internal quantities. A blank cell is not proof that no relation exists. It records that the comparison has reached the edge of current measurement or of this chapter’s evidence. Filling it with a result from a neighboring row would make the table visually complete while making the science less coherent.

It also shows where a mechanical account can have low priority. An intrinsic receiver state may explain the output better than the selected surrounding relation. Active control may dominate. A redundant route may preserve function. The observed deformation may be ordinary and fully restored. Measurement error may be as large as the proposed difference. The downstream effect may simply be absent. These are not escape clauses added after the mechanical story fails. They are affirmative branches that the comparison is designed to identify.

Anatomical overlap does not change the rule. A vessel and a nerve may pass through the same regional corridor, but a vascular waveform cannot establish neural strain, and neural symptoms cannot establish altered vascular flow. A later chapter will examine the thoracic outlet precisely because several unlike receivers traverse related spaces. The present matrix supplies the discipline for that case; it does not pool its presentations or decide their causes.

The same restraint protects the larger research program. Pelvic passages, nerve roots, craniofacial geometry, organ mobility, venous return, lymphatic transport, inflammation, regulation, energy, and chronic disease remain legitimate questions. They become stronger when each is decomposed into a maintaining relation, a receiver-specific output, a tissue or physiological bridge, a chronology, alternatives, and a null. They do not become smaller merely because Book I refuses to fill those links by analogy.

## A useful hypothesis must predict where freedom is preserved

The division-of-labor hypothesis becomes more than explanatory language when it predicts a measurable difference. A study would first declare the task and external demand. It would measure or operationally define the proposed shape-organizing relation. It would then measure the receiver’s needed freedom and a prespecified output while accounting for active control and intrinsic state.

Support would grow if greater participation of the proposed shape-organizing relation reliably accompanied preserved excursion, glide, caliber response, or pressure accommodation under comparable demands. It would grow further if changing that relation changed the receiver-specific output in the predicted direction, if restoration followed when the effective relation changed, and if the model distinguished extrinsic, intrinsic, and mixed histories better than conventional variables alone. A demanding test would ask whether the addition improves a predefined explanation or prediction in new data.

The comparator is essential. “More organized” cannot be defined after seeing which condition produced the preferred result. A study must state in advance what counts as participation by the proposed hard role, which receiver freedom should be preserved, and what range or direction of change is expected. It must also preserve the possibility that two different organizations support the function equally well, that the useful relation changes across phases, or that the selected receiver does not constrain the task. Without those possibilities, the language can describe any result and predict none.

Measurements should span the relation rather than merely collect more endpoints. One observation characterizes the candidate shape-organizing contribution, another characterizes the receiver’s exposure or freedom, and a third tests the functional output if that is part of the claim. Sampling before, during, and after a controlled change can distinguish modulation from restoration and retained state. Repeating the comparison across task, demand, or phase can test whether the role assignment is genuinely relational. None of this requires a whole-body measurement before local work can begin.

The hypothesis must also be allowed to lose. It narrows if the proposed hard-role measure has no reliable relation to the required freedom. It narrows if intrinsic state, active control, ordinary task geometry, or measurement error explains the result. It can reverse for a task if greater participation of the proposed shape-organizing relation reduces the relevant function. It adds no independent model if geometry, pressure, contact, material state, and active behavior predict the output equally well without the polarity.

These outcomes do not erase the authorial insight. They locate it. The phrase “the soft body can remain soft” points to a real class of mechanical questions: whether living structures retain the bounded ability to yield, glide, contain, change shape, and restore. The test determines which supporting relations preserve that ability, in which systems, under which demands, and over which times.

The stronger directional proposals of this series remain ahead. This chapter does not establish one preferred global route, a universal posterior requirement, an exact TCM coordinate mapping, or breath as the driver of organization. It establishes the receiver-side question those proposals will eventually have to answer. A preferred route would need to preserve specified freedoms better than defined alternatives, not merely look coherent or align with a traditional correspondence.

## From mechanical intelligibility to causal scope

The comparison began with six objects under a similar-looking constraint. It ends with six different mechanical accounts.

What transfers is a disciplined sequence. Name the task. Identify the structure’s controlled freedom. Distinguish the shape-organizing and form-receiving relations without assigning them permanently to tissues. Place the maintaining relation before the deformation, narrowing, or lost excursion. Decide whether persistence is transient, extrinsic, intrinsic, or mixed. Measure the receiver’s own output. Then ask whether that change crossed into tissue response, function, finding, or symptom.

What does not transfer is equally important. A hose does not prove vessel behavior. Vessel behavior does not prove lymphatic transport. Neither proves neural signaling, tendon glide, airway function, cavity support, or a medical outcome. Ordinary deformation, active compensation, adequate restoration, an intrinsic primary, low mechanical priority, and no consequential change remain complete results.

The hard/soft intuition survives this comparison in a more useful form. Load wants to travel through the hard body so that the soft body can remain soft: for a declared task, demand may be better organized when shape-preserving relations carry enough of the mechanical work that form-receiving structures retain the controlled freedom their functions require. That is an affirmative hypothesis. It is neither a fixed tissue taxonomy nor a conclusion that every compliant structure should be protected from force.

Mechanical intelligibility is now available without medical sufficiency. The next question is how much explanatory work one of these partial chains should do in an account of a whole person. In one bounded question, a mechanical relation may explain much of the selected output. In another, it may modify one link, follow from another change, have low priority, or add nothing useful. These are not mandatory bins. Relevance has to be earned at the particular node, in the particular history, against the alternatives that remain.
