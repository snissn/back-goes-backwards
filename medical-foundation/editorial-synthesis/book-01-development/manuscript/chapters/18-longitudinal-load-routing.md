# Chapter 18 — The proposed addition: longitudinal load routing

Imagine a research visit at which a local interface shows less excursion during a particular task than it did in an earlier record. The change is measured now. The task is also harder now. Those two observations may matter, but they do not contain their own history.

Perhaps a documented local event altered the interface, after which movement and support changed. Perhaps a repeated task changed first, a successful compensation became habitual, and the interface encountered a different exposure over time. Perhaps illness, inflammation, medication, sleep, stress, development, or another change in capacity came first and mechanics changed in response. Perhaps several of these occurred, or the decisive interval was never observed.

This is a hypothetical comparison, not a patient history or diagnostic exercise. Its purpose is to make a missing object visible. The endpoint can be measured precisely while more than one prior sequence remains compatible with it. A longer sequence may help distinguish those possibilities. It may also remain too incomplete to do so.

We use *longitudinal load routing* for a structured record of how a defined task and environment, available mechanical paths, demand distribution, local exposure, compensation, and changing state relate across time. The record can combine direct observations, measurements, calculations, models, reports, and explicit unknowns. It is ordered forward in time even when some of it is assembled retrospectively.

The proposed value is not a recovered hidden cause. It is an inspectable account in which observations held at different scales and by different disciplines can be placed in temporal relation without losing how each became known. Such an account can generate a better exposure question, distinguish competing histories, reveal a missing measurement, or organize prospective research. It can also add nothing.

That last possibility is not a ceremonial disclaimer. It is the standard the proposal must survive. Mechanics, ergonomics, occupational exposure science, rehabilitation, and medicine already study important parts of mechanical history. A routing record earns a place only when it adds a defined result beyond those existing accounts.

## A present endpoint underdetermines its history

Chapter 17 distinguished an endpoint record from a history record. The endpoint record asks what condition is present, what quantity was measured, and whether a selected intervention changed it. The history record asks what demands preceded that condition, what paths and supports were available, what changed, what exposure reached a receiver, and what happened next.

Neither record is automatically more rigorous. A current pressure, excursion, alignment, motion, or interface distribution may be measured well. A proposed whole-body history may be assembled from memory and an untested model. Conversely, repeated measurements may document much of a history while a present endpoint remains ambiguous. The relevant comparison is between the questions the records can answer, not between local simplicity and systemic sophistication.

The inverse problem is many-to-many. Similar endpoints can follow different events, tasks, tissue states, compensations, and recovery conditions. One documented event can also lead to different later distributions, responses, findings, and functions. A current endpoint constrains the candidate histories; it does not usually select one by itself. Causal reasoning therefore requires a defined contrast, chronology, assumptions, and observations capable of distinguishing alternatives. A coherent sequence is not an identified effect merely because every arrow can be narrated.[^18-01]

Each branch points toward different evidence. The local-event account becomes stronger when the event, immediate mechanical change, and endpoint were documented in the right order. The repeated-exposure account needs samples before the endpoint changed, a quantity that represents the proposed receiver, and evidence that the strategy altered that quantity. The biology- or context-first account needs chronology showing that capacity or behavior changed before the mechanical pattern under discussion. The mixed account may be narrowed by those observations without ever resolving to one origin.

The histories need not be equally probable. A clear event can dominate the evidence even though other influences remain possible. A long period without observation can leave several routes open even when one sounds anatomically persuasive. The framework's job is not to maintain artificial symmetry among candidates. It is to show why one becomes better supported and where the remaining uncertainty resides.

A useful reconstruction starts by separating times. At an initial time, it records the person, environment, task, capacity, and available paths relevant to the question. At a later time, it records a dated event, repeated demand, or change in context. It then identifies the measured or proposed distribution and a named local receiver. Subsequent entries record compensation, assistance, recovery, response, or no detectable change. The current endpoint is the last entry, not the source from which every previous entry is inferred.

The order matters because the same fact can occupy different causal positions. Pain can follow a changed strategy, precede it, or share another cause with it. A device can respond to a problem and then become part of later exposure. A tissue finding can limit motion, while the resulting motion change alters another receiver. The record preserves those directions rather than forcing one of them to be globally upstream.

## The record preserves how each link became known

A longitudinal routing record is not a seamless arrow. It is a set of dated propositions with different kinds of support.

Task and environment may be documented in a work record, observed directly, reported later, or represented by a job-level estimate. Configuration and contact may be visible on a dated image or video, measured during a selected phase, recalled, or modeled. External force may be measured, while a joint moment is calculated from measured inputs and a local tissue field is estimated in a model. A person's felt change is real as reported experience without becoming an instrumented internal force. Missing intervals remain missing.

In the figures, we shorten these distinctions to observed (`OBS`), measured (`MEAS`), calculated (`CALC`), modeled (`MOD`), inferred (`INF`), hypothesized (`HYP`), embodied or reported experience (`EMB`), and unknown. These labels describe the basis of an entry. *Prospective*, *contemporaneous*, and *retrospectively reconstructed* describe when and how it entered the record. A retrospectively reconstructed measurement remains a measurement if the instrument record survives; a recalled exposure remains a report; a modeled route remains a model.

This structure blocks several common substitutions. A recorded posture cannot silently become an internal force. A calculated force cannot silently become tissue stress. A modeled local exposure cannot silently become a biological response. A current finding cannot retrospectively establish the demand that preceded it. Relief after support can show that support changed a selected outcome without proving the initiating route.

The correct record can therefore be partial. Unknown cells reveal where inference begins and where a future measurement might matter. Filling them with anatomically plausible arrows would make the story smoother and the evidence worse.

Resolution should match the question. A research study of one repeated industrial task may need phase-specific contact, force, motion, and exposure estimates over several shifts. A device follow-up may need task samples before and after a fit or alignment change. A broad developmental history may initially support only dates, environments, major changes in assistance, and selected functional states. Calling all three *longitudinal* does not imply that they contain the same temporal density.

Interventions also belong inside the chronology. Rest, medication, surgery, rehabilitation, a new tool, a changed schedule, or environmental support can alter capacity, exposure, reporting, and the available paths. Omitting them can make a later state appear to follow mechanically from an earlier one when the intervening system had already changed. Recording them does not make the history clinically complete; it prevents the mechanical sequence from claiming an uninterrupted interval it did not observe.

## The proposal enters an occupied landscape

Longitudinal routing does not begin on an empty map. Existing frameworks already connect body state, activity, participation, task, environment, exposure, function, and care.

The World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health supplies a shared language for bodily function, activity, participation, and environmental factors.[^18-02] It is not a mechanical routing model, but it prevents this project from claiming that medical reasoning has never related bodily state to environment and participation.

Human factors and ergonomics explicitly study interactions among people and other elements of a system. The International Ergonomics Association includes tasks, jobs, products, environments, organizations, tools, and technology within a systems approach that spans physical, cognitive, sociotechnical, and environmental factors.[^18-03] A person–environment boundary and an interest in work design are therefore established common ground.

Rehabilitation also has integrative frameworks. The Rehabilitation Treatment Specification System separates treatment targets, ingredients, hypothesized mechanisms of action, and more distant aims.[^18-04] It answers a different question from an exposure history, but it demonstrates why a local target, an intermediate mechanism, and a later outcome should not be collapsed.

Biomechanics contributes kinematics, kinetics, contact analysis, musculoskeletal models, and local estimates. Occupational exposure science contributes duration, repetition, force, posture, vibration, and job history. Rehabilitation contributes function, capacity, adaptation, support, learning, and repeated response. Medical specialties contribute tissue states, diagnoses, exclusions, procedures, immediate risks, and local endpoints. The person and their support network contribute goals, chronology, felt changes, assistance, access, and participation.

The candidate increment is a shared *mechanical history object*: a chronology that relates selected observations across those boundaries while retaining their provenance and conflicts. It might show, for example, that an exposure estimate belongs to a job group, a configuration was measured during only one task, an internal distribution was modeled under a declared assumption, and the local endpoint appeared later. That relation can be useful even if no discipline owns it.

The record has two possible organizing advantages. First, it can keep scale transitions explicit. A job demand, an external force, a calculated joint moment, a modeled tissue exposure, and a medical finding can appear in one chronology without being treated as one variable. Second, it can preserve disagreement. A person's report, a device log, a brief laboratory sample, and a group-level estimate can remain side by side rather than being merged into a false average. In both cases, the gain is inspectability, not comprehensiveness.

But breadth is not novelty. If an ergonomic assessment, rehabilitation record, biomechanical analysis, or ordinary medical history already performs the required job, the new name adds nothing. The proposal is not that every relevant observation must be centralized. It is that, for selected questions, their temporal and mechanical relation may deserve explicit representation.

## Cumulative exposure is real—and metric-dependent

The concern with repeated demand has strong precedent. Occupational biomechanics and epidemiology have studied peak, cumulative, and time-varying physical exposures for decades. That work supports the importance of history while warning against treating cumulative load as one natural scalar.

In a case–control comparison of 104 automotive workers reporting low-back pain and 130 controls, Norman and colleagues examined peak and cumulative variables derived from task observations, hand-force estimates, trunk kinematics, and modeled lumbar loads. The retained factors included an integrated lumbar moment over a shift, usual hand force, peak modeled shear, and peak trunk velocity.[^18-05] The result supports mixed peak-and-history exposure models. It does not identify one universal accumulation rule or recover an individual's internal route.

The German EPILIFT study reconstructed lifetime occupational histories for 564 people with symptomatic lumbar disc herniation, 351 with symptomatic disc narrowing, and 901 population controls. Interviews, expert task surveys, and biomechanical calculations were used to estimate cumulative lumbosacral compression in selected work situations, and positive dose–response relations were reported for selected measures.[^18-06] This is already an ambitious longitudinal reconstruction. Its job histories were not continuous direct observations, its modeled compression was not a local tissue stress field, and its case–control associations do not identify one person's causal history.

In a prospective study of 537 professional drivers followed for two years, Bovenzi combined measured vehicle vibration, exposure duration, cumulative vibration formulas, interviews, and temporal modeling. Different exposure metrics performed differently: selected dose and lifetime cumulative measures predicted some low-back-pain outcomes better than an eight-hour root-mean-square measure.[^18-07] History mattered, and the rule used to describe history mattered.

These examples establish a demanding comparator for the proposed routing record. Exposure science already handles repetition, duration, peaks, cumulative quantities, and changes through time. A routing account might add changing contacts, carriers, regional substitutions, or a named local receiver. It must say which of those variables is expected to improve which result. If the added fields cannot be measured or do not alter a declared explanation, prediction, or research design, *body-wide* means only that the narrative contains more places.

The quantity must remain stable as the history is assembled. Repetition count, force–time integral, cumulative joint moment, vibration dose, modeled compression, local stress, and recovery interval are not interchangeable merely because each contains time. A record can contain several of them, but it must not let one inherit the evidence or biological meaning of another.

Peak and cumulative measures are therefore not rivals from which one winner must be chosen. A brief high-magnitude event and a lower-magnitude repeated exposure can ask different questions about the same task history. Rate, direction, variability, rest, sequence, and current state can also matter. The routing proposal does not solve this by adding every possible dimension. It requires the investigator to choose the exposure description appropriate to the receiver and outcome, then preserve what that description leaves out.

Capacity changes during the same interval. Practice, fatigue, sleep, illness, treatment, development, equipment, and work organization can change what the next exposure means. A cumulative demand record without a changing receiver state describes only one side of the history. Yet a capacity narrative without a named exposure is equally incomplete. Their relationship becomes biological in Chapter 19; here the mechanical record merely ensures that both have dates.

## Reconstruction can be useful without becoming observation

Retrospective information is not a defective version of perfect continuous measurement. It is a different source with a resolution that depends on the variable, interval, task, and method.

Spielholz and colleagues compared self-report, video observation, electrogoniometry, and electromyography-derived hand-force estimates for eighteen people performing three tree-seedling jobs. Self-reports were least precise and consistently overestimated the selected exposures; the best-performing method differed by posture or motion variable.[^18-08] The study does not supply one universal ground truth. It shows why the source must travel with the estimate.

In an automobile-manufacturing cohort interviewed at baseline, one year, and six years, d'Errico and colleagues found fair-to-good one-year reproducibility for a composite exposure index and some items, but poor five- to six-year reproducibility for the composite and almost every individual exposure. Work changes and regression to the mean complicated interpretation, and the analytical samples narrowed markedly when comparisons required the same job across longer intervals.[^18-09] Recall was not uniformly useless; its fidelity decayed unevenly.

Job-exposure matrices make another trade. They replace some individual detail with scalable group-level estimates. Korshøj and colleagues compared five occupational physicians' ratings across sixteen job titles with technical or observed measures. Job-title rank correlations ranged from 0.55 for standing or walking to 0.83 for kneeling or squatting, while estimated lifted mass exceeded observation by an average of 909 kg per workday with wide limits of agreement.[^18-10] A job title can provide an informative prior without becoming a person's measured exposure.

A defensible reconstruction therefore records dates, aggregation level, missing periods, units, uncertainty, model assumptions, and the receiver to which an exposure is supposed to apply. It can combine a work record, a person's report, a video sample, a force measurement, and a model. Combining them does not promote them to the same evidentiary state.

Prospective field methods can strengthen selected links. A two-dimensional computer-vision method developed by Greene and colleagues displayed the frequency, speed, and duty-cycle components of selected repetitive hand tasks across space and time. It depended on an analyst-selected hand region and manually labeled exertion states, and it was a method demonstration rather than validation of a whole-body route or injury risk.[^18-11]

OpenCap uses synchronized smartphone video, pose estimation, inverse kinematics, and physics-based simulation to estimate selected movement dynamics. Its laboratory validation involved ten healthy adults; a separate one-hundred-person field experiment demonstrated a clinician-led workflow rather than expanding the laboratory-validation cohort.[^18-12] The output is a chain from video to keypoints to a musculoskeletal model, not direct measurement of muscle force or tissue stress.

A 2026 simultaneous comparison of smartphone-based markerless capture and optical motion capture included forty-one participants performing eight movements. Agreement was stronger in the sagittal plane, while out-of-plane errors, correlations, systematic differences, and task performance were more variable.[^18-13] Accessibility did not erase plane- and task-specific validity.

The prospect is therefore narrower and more useful than continuous capture of everything. A study can predeclare the task and outcome, the few routing variables expected to distinguish candidate histories, the required times, the relevant measurement error, and the comparator record without those variables. Prospective sampling then tests a relation instead of manufacturing a more detailed archive.

Sampling must also be placed where change is expected. A single baseline and final visit may miss a temporary compensation, early fatigue, recovery between shifts, or a strategy that appears only under one demand. Continuous measurement may be unnecessary or impractical, but the chosen intervals should be capable of observing the proposed transition. The study should state what pattern would support the route, what pattern would favor the alternative, and what degree of disagreement would be indistinguishable from error.

This makes prospective work selective rather than maximalist. The aim is not a permanent sensor record of a person. It is a design in which a small number of measurements can defeat at least one plausible account. If no feasible observation can distinguish the candidate routes, the framework has identified a limit rather than earned permission to infer the missing path.

## Six families can index change without becoming diagnoses

Part III developed six families of mechanical organization. In a longitudinal record, they can function as a compact vocabulary for where a relation changed. They do not classify a person or diagnose a condition.

>
> | Proposed family | Place in the record | Question that keeps it testable |
> |---|---|---|
> | Global or regional redistribution | configuration and distribution across several regions or contacts | Which named contribution changed, and did the proposed local exposure change with it? |
> | Local concentration | distribution-to-receiver relation | Was a declared stress, strain, pressure, contact, or other field more focused than under the comparator? |
> | Segmental or task-specific instability | repeated departure, correction, or variable support | Did variability, correction, contact, or control demand change beyond measurement noise? |
> | Timing, coordination, or handoff failure | transfer among carriers or task phases | Was demand transferred late, early, abruptly, incompletely, or through another declared carrier? |
> | Constraint-maintained deformation or lost excursion | persistent boundary condition and receiver state | Did deformation or excursion change when the maintaining relation changed? |
> | Compensatory rerouting | a strategy that preserves function and changes later allocation | What remained possible, what route changed, and did a named later exposure differ? |
>
> **Caption.** The six families describe candidate relations at nodes and transitions in a dated record. They can overlap, change with task or time, or be absent. The table is not a diagnostic tree, and the proposed classifications do not yet have established reliability or validity.
>
> **Text alternative.** Six rows map redistribution, concentration, instability, timing or handoff failure, maintained deformation, and compensatory rerouting to places in a longitudinal record. Each row ends with a discriminating measurement question. The caption permits overlap, change, and absence and states that the taxonomy is proposed rather than validated.

Scale transition is not a seventh adverse family. It describes a change in the boundary of analysis: a local relation may alter regional participation, or a task-level change may alter a local receiver. The intermediate quantities still have to be shown.

Clear definitions are not sufficient evidence that different observers can use the families consistently. Reporting guidance for reliability and agreement studies emphasizes the design, rater and subject populations, measurement error, agreement statistics, and conditions under which a classification was applied.[^18-14] A bounded comparison illustrates the problem. In one study of forty-five people with shoulder pain, two trained therapists using a movement-system-impairment classification achieved fair agreement for broad subgroups and an overall kappa of 0.11 for the set of subcategory judgments.[^18-15] That result does not test this book's taxonomy. It shows why intuitive legibility and training cannot substitute for an independent reliability program.

The research questions are concrete. Can raters identify each family from the same record? Can they distinguish one family from another and from none? Does agreement survive changes in task, region, population, and record completeness? Does a family predict a prespecified measurement or transition beyond ordinary variables? If not, the family must be narrowed, revised, or replaced.

## Compensation makes the history branch

A routing history becomes distorted if compensation enters only as a prelude to failure. Compensation begins with successful function. A new contact, timing strategy, active pattern, device, assistance, or change in excursion enables something to continue under present conditions. That solution may resolve after recovery, persist because it remains useful, transfer to another task, or change a later exposure.

The possible negative path is important precisely because it is conditional. A changed task or available path can lead to useful redistribution. Repetition can then change the starting state or exposure of a later task. That later exposure can interact with capacity and recovery at a named receiver. A material or biological response may follow. But recovery, learning, environmental redesign, support, adequate capacity, and ordinary variation can interrupt or reverse every step.

The absence of a discrete injury proves neither branch. It does not rule out cumulative exposure, and it does not establish cumulative injury. A later finding can follow the compensation, cause it, share another cause with it, or be incidental. An image that is common, absent, delayed, or nonspecific cannot fill the unobserved interval.

This is where the phrase *silent compensation* needs discipline. Silence means that function continues or no selected symptom or finding is present while a strategy differs. It does not mean hidden damage. Three records can look quiet: the relevant exposure was never measured; it was measured and did not change meaningfully; or it changed while remaining compatible with recovery and current capacity. The first is unknown, the second is a mechanical null, and the third may be a capable adaptation. A useful framework must preserve all three.

The record can still make delayed consequence testable. It can name the protected function, the recurring strategy, the exposure quantity, the receiver, the recovery interval, and the later state. It can ask whether the receiver covaries with the strategy, whether changing support changes the proposed exposure, and whether the later observation actually followed the route change. The hypothesis becomes stronger by being allowed to lose.

Functional reserve is one candidate reason to care about the period before a symptom or discrete event, but it cannot remain a hidden store. For a declared task, reserve might mean the remaining ability to vary a strategy, repeat the demand, respond to a perturbation, or recover within a stated interval. Continued task completion does not prove that reserve is intact or depleted. A routing history becomes informative only when a selected reserve measure changes with the proposed exposure and predicts the task or recovery result in question.

The same discipline protects the project's negative intuition. Repetition can plausibly matter before a conventional finding appears, and a successful strategy can change the next exposure. Neither proposition requires a dramatic initiating injury. But *before symptoms* is a temporal description, not evidence of occult disease. The record has to show the mechanical change first; Chapter 19 then asks whether a receiver responded.

## The record is shared, not owned

No single discipline is likely to observe every node. That fact motivates integration, but it does not create a new jurisdiction.

The person can describe chronology, goals, effort, felt change, assistance, participation, and access. Biomechanics can measure motion and external forces and model selected internal distributions. Rehabilitation can evaluate function, capacity, adaptation, support, learning, and repeated response. Ergonomics and occupational inquiry can describe tasks, tools, duration, organization, and environmental constraints. Medical specialties can characterize tissue state, diagnosis, exclusions, procedures, and local endpoints. Psychological, social, and developmental inquiry can identify changes in behavior, expectation, opportunity, stress, learning, and life course that alter the record.

Those contributions can conflict. A person's report may not agree with a job-level exposure estimate. A video sample may not represent the most demanding period. A modeled internal force may not track the symptom or endpoint under consideration. A medical finding may precede the mechanical change that is easiest to observe. The shared record should expose disagreement rather than average it into one authoritative story.

The word *upstream* is useful only in a limited sense. A routing question may be asked earlier than a rehabilitation episode or across a wider interval than a local intervention. It is not higher in professional authority, and it is not necessarily earlier in the causal chain. For each proposed use—exposure research, recurrence, device or surgical context, environmental design, recovery, functional reserve, or prevention research—the record needs a named comparator and outcome.

Those uses impose different tests. In exposure research, the comparator may be duration, force, posture, or job history without internal routing variables; the added record should improve a prespecified exposure or outcome model. In recurrence research, it should distinguish a later episode better than established clinical and functional variables. In a device or surgical context, it might clarify how a changed assembly redistributed a selected demand through follow-up, without determining whether the device or procedure was indicated. In environmental design, it might identify which constraint or support changed a receiver's exposure relative to an ordinary ergonomic assessment.

Prevention and ambiguous chronic presentations demand even more restraint. A mechanical history could help formulate what to measure before a problem develops or organize a mixed chronology after it has developed. That does not establish a preventive rule, a mechanical primary cause, or a treatment target. The proposal remains useful only if it makes a better question than existing history taking, risk modeling, rehabilitation assessment, or whole-person reasoning.

Chapter 22 will ask how much of this belongs in an actual workflow. Here the product remains a research representation: a structured hypothesis about what was observed, what was reconstructed, what route is proposed, what alternative fits, and which next observation could discriminate.

## Incremental value is the survival test

The proposal should be evaluated in stages. First comes feasibility: can the necessary fields be collected without unacceptable burden or exclusion? Next come reliability and agreement: do repeated measurements or trained observers produce stable variables under declared conditions? Construct and discriminant validity ask whether the route variables correspond to appropriate direct or model-based comparisons and separate the histories they are supposed to separate.

Only then does the record face its central comparison. Do the added variables improve a prespecified explanation or prediction beyond an ordinary history, ergonomic assessment, biomechanical model, rehabilitation evaluation, or local medical account? If the intended benefit is communication or research design, does the shared chronology reveal a missing measure, reduce ambiguity, or coordinate a study better than existing documentation? If a decision is proposed, predictive accuracy alone is not enough; the consequences of acting, not acting, error, burden, and opportunity cost also matter. Decision-curve analysis was developed around this general distinction between model performance and net decision benefit, although no one evaluation method is mandated here.[^18-16]

A no-added-value case can be entirely positive. Suppose a dated local event is well documented, the relevant endpoint is clear, and the immediate question can be answered from that record. A body-wide reconstruction may contribute no useful information. The local account has succeeded; the wider proposal has not earned a role for that purpose.

Other negative results are equally substantive. Required fields may not be collectible. Several histories may remain indistinguishable. A mature discipline may already capture the useful relation. Added variables may fail to improve prediction, explanation, communication, or study design. Collection burden may distort the task. The larger story may increase confidence without increasing evidence.

Different aims must not borrow one another's success. A record that helps researchers agree on what is missing has communication value even if it does not predict an outcome. A model that predicts a selected endpoint more accurately has not thereby shown that acting on it helps anyone. A history that changes a decision in one setting has not established a body-wide causal explanation. Naming the intended gain in advance prevents one modest success from promoting the entire framework.

These outcomes narrow contexts rather than denying mechanical history. The framework may prove useful prospectively but weak retrospectively, useful for research coordination but not decisions, or useful for one task and receiver but redundant for another. Such a result is more informative than claiming universal relevance.

## A bounded history, then an open biological arrow

Return to the hypothetical endpoint. The documented-event history may need little wider reconstruction. The repeated-task history may become stronger if exposure and compensation were sampled before the endpoint changed. The biology- or context-first history may predict a different chronology or response to the same task. The mixed history may remain unresolved. The next useful result is the observation that separates them, not the story that feels most complete.

Longitudinal load routing therefore adds a candidate object, not a conclusion. It is a dated, provenance-preserving relation among task, environment, available paths, distribution, local exposure, compensation, recovery, and later state. Its strongest immediate product is a testable question. It does not by definition recover a unique cause, validate the six families, establish a diagnosis, or yield a treatment.

The proposal survives where the relation can be measured or reconstructed well enough to improve a declared result beyond an existing comparator. It narrows where the record is unreliable, redundant, non-discriminating, burdensome, or unnecessary. Both outcomes make the program more precise.

Even an excellent routing history stops at a boundary. A defined local exposure does not tell us what a living tissue did with it, and the same nominal demand cannot be assumed to mean the same exposure or response in bone, tendon, cartilage, or another receiver. Chapter 19 takes the bounded exposure handed forward here and asks what tissue state, dose, recovery, and biological process are required before a mechanical history can become a response.

[^18-04]: Tessa Hart et al., [“A Theory-Driven System for the Specification of Rehabilitation Treatments,”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apmr.2018.09.109) *Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation* 100, no. 1 (2019): 172–180. The article supports an existing cross-diagnostic rehabilitation framework separating targets, ingredients, hypothesized mechanisms, and distal aims; it specifies interventions rather than reconstructing mechanical exposure histories.

[^18-05]: R. Norman et al., [“A Comparison of Peak vs Cumulative Physical Work Exposure Risk Factors for the Reporting of Low Back Pain in the Automotive Industry,”](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0268-0033(98)00020-5) *Clinical Biomechanics* 13, no. 8 (1998): 561–573. The comparison included 104 cases and 130 controls and combined task observations and physical measurements with modeled lumbar loads. It supports established peak-and-cumulative exposure analysis, not individual causation or a universal accumulation rule.

[^18-06]: Andreas Seidler et al., [“Cumulative Occupational Lumbar Load and Lumbar Disc Disease—Results of a German Multi-Center Case-Control Study (EPILIFT),”](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2474-10-48) *BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders* 10 (2009): 48. The study supports a substantial lifetime occupational-history reconstruction combining interview, expert task surveys, and modeled compression; it does not identify an individual's unique route.

[^18-07]: Massimo Bovenzi, [“Metrics of Whole-Body Vibration and Exposure–Response Relationship for Low Back Pain in Professional Drivers: A Prospective Cohort Study,”](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00420-008-0376-3) *International Archives of Occupational and Environmental Health* 82, no. 7 (2009): 893–917. Five hundred thirty-seven drivers were followed for two years. The study supports metric-sensitive cumulative exposure and temporal modeling; it does not recover one whole-body mechanical history.

[^18-08]: Peregrin Spielholz et al., [“Comparison of Self-Report, Video Observation and Direct Measurement Methods for Upper Extremity Musculoskeletal Disorder Physical Risk Factors,”](https://doi.org/10.1080/00140130118050) *Ergonomics* 44, no. 6 (2001): 588–613. Eighteen participants performed three jobs. The study supports method-specific exposure error and self-report overestimation in the tested tasks; no method was best for every variable.

[^18-09]: Angelo d'Errico et al., [“Medium- and Long-Term Reproducibility of Self-Reported Exposure to Physical Ergonomics Factors at Work,”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2006.03.002) *Applied Ergonomics* 38, no. 2 (2007): 167–175. The cohort began with 1,283 workers; 507 same-job workers contributed the one-year comparison, while 163 and 162 matched-job records contributed the two long-term comparisons. The study supports variable- and interval-dependent reproducibility; it does not make every retrospective history unusable.

[^18-10]: Mette Korshøj et al., [“Agreement between an Expert-Rated Mini Job Exposure Matrix of Occupational Biomechanical Exposures to the Lower Body and Technical Measurements or Observation: A Method Comparison Study,”](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2022-064035) *BMJ Open* 12, no. 12 (2022): e064035. The comparison used sixteen job titles and data from a convenience sample of 766 participants, with sharply different contributing samples by exposure. It supports the scalable value and individual-resolution limits of job-level estimates; correlation and agreement remain different properties.

[^18-11]: Runyu L. Greene et al., [“Visualizing Stressful Aspects of Repetitive Motion Tasks and Opportunities for Ergonomic Improvements Using Computer Vision,”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2017.02.020) *Applied Ergonomics* 65 (2017): 461–472. The article is used as a method demonstration for spatial and temporal decomposition of selected two-dimensional task exposures, not as validation of longitudinal body-wide routing.

[^18-12]: Scott D. Uhlrich et al., [“OpenCap: Human Movement Dynamics from Smartphone Videos,”](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011462) *PLOS Computational Biology* 19, no. 10 (2023): e1011462. The laboratory validation involved ten healthy adults; the separate one-hundred-person field experiment demonstrated workflow and selected discrimination tasks rather than adding one hundred laboratory-validation participants. The source supports accessible kinematic and model-based dynamic estimation, not direct observation of internal force or tissue exposure. Scott D. Uhlrich and Antoine Falisse reported cofounding Model Health, Inc., which supports commercial use of the software.

[^18-13]: Huaqing Liang, Curtis Justin Grant, and Yekaterina Bolgarskaya, [“Validity of Using a Smartphone-Based Markerless Motion Capture System for Quantitative Analysis of Human Dynamic Movements,”](https://doi.org/10.1080/14763141.2026.2689518) *Sports Biomechanics* (online June 19, 2026): 1–23. The study supports plane-, task-, and metric-specific validity limits and was online ahead of print when this chapter was drafted.

[^18-14]: Jan Kottner et al., [“Guidelines for Reporting Reliability and Agreement Studies (GRRAS) Were Proposed,”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2010.03.002) *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology* 64, no. 1 (2011): 96–106. The guidelines support the measurement and reporting obligations of classifications and ratings; they do not specify which statistic or threshold would validate this taxonomy.

[^18-15]: Patitta Torwichien et al., [“Intertester Reliability of a Movement Impairment-Based Classification System for Individuals with Shoulder Pain,”](https://doi.org/10.1142/S1013702520500067) *Hong Kong Physiotherapy Journal* 40, no. 1 (2020): 51–62. Two therapists assessed forty-five people. The study is a bounded example of variable and sometimes poor classification agreement, not a verdict on all movement classifications or a test of the six families in this book.

[^18-16]: Andrew J. Vickers and Elena B. Elkin, [“Decision Curve Analysis: A Novel Method for Evaluating Prediction Models,”](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X06295361) *Medical Decision Making* 26, no. 6 (2006): 565–574. The article supports the principle that model accuracy and decision consequences are different evaluations; its prostate-cancer example does not establish clinical utility for routing.
