# Chapter 17 — Established mechanical reasoning in medicine

A pressure trace moves across a monitor. A ventilator supports a pressure-and-volume cycle. A plate holds the fragments of a broken bone in a selected relation. A surgeon releases a local constraint. A prosthetic socket transfers forces and moments between a device and living tissue.

None of these scenes is outside medicine. Each depends on physical quantities, geometry, material behavior, and time. Each also depends on biology, judgment, and the person receiving care. The mechanical account matters because it describes part of what the intervention is intended to change—not because it replaces the rest of medicine.

This gives a skeptical reader a strong objection to the premise of this book: *Isn't this already medicine?*

The honest answer is yes. Mechanics is not a neglected foreign language waiting to be introduced into medical care. Biomechanics, rehabilitation, surgery, cardiopulmonary medicine, orthotics and prosthetics, ergonomics, and many other fields already measure and alter pressure, flow, stability, contact, motion, support, and force distribution. Book I inherits that work.

A narrower question remains. A present mechanical endpoint does not always contain the history that produced it. The pressure measured now does not record every earlier task and tissue state. A stable fracture construct does not disclose the entire injury and healing history. A changed interface distribution does not reveal one unique way demand arrived there. Local endpoint reasoning and longitudinal routing reasoning are related, but they are not the same record.

This chapter establishes the common ground. It asks what medicine already does mechanically, what those endpoints can legitimately establish, and what they leave open. The next chapter will have to show whether following demand, substitution, compensation, and exposure across time creates an additional object worth keeping.

## A mechanical endpoint can be a medical endpoint

Medicine regularly makes distinctions that are mechanical before they are diagnostic. Pressure is not flow. Flow is not perfusion. Volume is not tissue strain. Stability is not healing. Component strength is not interface tolerance. A treatment can influence several of these at once, but their coexistence does not make them one variable.

During cardiac arrest, for example, external chest compression supplies part of a pump action that the circulation is not providing. Current American Heart Association guidance relates compression, recoil, interruption, ventilation, forward flow, and coronary perfusion pressure while keeping return of spontaneous circulation and survival as later outcomes.[^17-01] The relationships are physical and medically consequential. They are also incomplete. Compression depth is an input that can be controlled; perfusion pressure is an intermediate; survival depends on additional events and states.

This example matters because it is difficult to caricature as posture coaching. External contact with the body is intentionally used to support circulation. Yet no one needs to reconstruct a person's complete mechanical history before beginning that support. The cause of the arrest, its duration, rhythm, oxygenation, medication, injuries, and system of care remain separately important. Immediate endpoint reasoning is not inferior because it is local and urgent.

Assisted ventilation supplies another example. A device can help generate pressure differences and volume change when a person cannot sustain adequate ventilation alone. In acute respiratory distress syndrome, authoritative guidelines distinguish tidal volume, plateau pressure, positive end-expiratory pressure, oxygenation, recruitment, extracorporeal support, medication, and the state of the lung and circulation.[^17-02][^17-03] These are not interchangeable settings on one scalar called respiratory mechanics. They are coupled quantities and decisions within a living cardiopulmonary system.

Mechanical support can therefore have two truths at once. It can be necessary, and it can create a new exposure. A ventilator supports gas exchange while pressure, volume, timing, regional heterogeneity, and tissue state affect what the lung experiences. This is not a contradiction. It is ordinary medical reasoning about a physical intervention in a biological receiver.

The point of the table is not that all medicine can be placed inside a mechanical grid. It is that some medical questions already have a mechanical object precise enough to measure or change. The biological and personal context does not weaken that precision. It tells us what the mechanical endpoint means and what it cannot mean by itself.

An endpoint record becomes useful by declaring its boundary. In resuscitation, the boundary may include the chest, circulation, external contact, ventilation, and a short interval of urgent care. In fracture fixation, it may include the fragments, construct, surrounding tissue, applied load, and a phase of healing. At a prosthetic interface, it may include the person, device, ground, selected task, and the surface across which contact is measured. Redrawing the boundary changes the question. It does not expose one finally correct system hidden beneath the others.

The record also needs a verb. A pressure was *measured*. A flow was *supported*. A relative motion was *limited*. A local constraint was *released*. A force was *calculated* from measured inputs. A tissue exposure was *modeled* under stated assumptions. These verbs preserve the difference between what instruments recorded and what an analysis inferred. “Mechanical improvement” is too broad if the text cannot say which of those events occurred.

Time is part of the boundary as well. An immediate change can establish that an intervention affected a selected condition. It does not determine whether the change persisted, whether a living receiver accommodated it, or whether the person's function changed. A longitudinal follow-up can add those observations without reconstructing every event before the intervention. Present state, intervention, short-term endpoint, biological response, and longer outcome remain connected but distinct entries.

This discipline prevents an odd reversal. The endpoint is sometimes dismissed as superficial because it does not explain origin. Yet a well-defined endpoint may be measured more reliably than the history proposed to explain it. The proper comparison is not local simplicity against whole-body sophistication. It is one bounded record against another, each judged by the question it can answer.

## Substitution supports a function without reproducing its cause

The resuscitation and ventilation examples share a useful structure: an external action or device temporarily performs part of a physical job. We can call this *mechanical substitution* as long as we name the substituted function.

The word does not imply that a machine becomes the organ, that normal physiology has been recreated, or that the initiating problem has been repaired. Chest compression supports a circulation-related function. Assisted ventilation supports movement of gas through pressure and volume change. An orthosis can limit a selected motion or change contact. A prosthesis can make a valued task possible through a new person–device assembly. Each substitution has a declared boundary and an incomplete resemblance to the function it supports.

Substitution is often discussed as if it were a temporary compromise on the way back to an unaided original state. That assumption is unnecessary. Some supports are used briefly. Others become capable and valued parts of a person's continuing mechanical system. The medical question is whether the assembly serves the person's selected function under its actual conditions—not whether it resembles an unsupported body.

This view also clarifies why support deserves its own exposure analysis. An added route can reduce one demand while increasing another. A brace can constrain motion while changing contact. A prosthetic socket can transmit an external resultant while creating spatially and temporally varied pressure and shear at the residual limb. A ventilator can sustain gas exchange while its pressure-and-volume cycle enters the tissue's mechanical history. The substitution cannot be judged from the supported endpoint alone.

None of this requires the claim that substitution is generally beneficial or harmful. It creates a comparison. What function became available? What physical intermediate changed? What new demands entered the assembly? What biological receiver encountered them? What did the person value? Those questions are already familiar in clinical engineering and rehabilitation. They will later constrain any claim that a body has compensated or rerouted demand.

## Fixation creates a condition for healing

Fracture fixation makes the cooperation between mechanics and biology unusually visible. A construct can restore length and alignment, provide restraint, alter interfragmentary motion, and transfer force across a disrupted region. Those are intentional mechanical changes. They do not heal the fracture by themselves.

AO fracture-fixation principles distinguish compression plating intended to create absolute stability from bridge plating intended to create relative stability while preserving length and rotational and transverse alignment.[^17-04] In a bridge construct, force can pass through the plate and screws while the fracture zone remains comparatively undisturbed. As continuity develops and a gap closes, sharing between construct and bone can change.

The word *stability* therefore needs a specific comparison. It can refer to the motion permitted between fragments under a declared load and construct, not to a universal absence of movement. The chosen condition depends on the fracture and healing strategy. Blood supply, soft-tissue state, infection, repair biology, time, and the person's activity remain part of the result.

Experimental work can separate some of these variables without turning them into a universal rule. In a ten-sheep tibial-osteotomy model, active fixators generated a spatial gradient from 2.5 to 25 percent interfragmentary strain. Five animals were loaded from the first postoperative day and five from day 22. Computed-tomography measures at five weeks found that the distribution of repair tissue varied with strain magnitude and loading time: lower tested strains favored more tissue within the gap, while higher strains shifted calcified repair tissue toward external callus.[^17-05] The study does not establish a numerical treatment threshold for people. It demonstrates that a construct-defined mechanical field, its timing, and a biological repair distribution can be studied as different objects.

Fixation supports three modest conclusions. Medicine intentionally creates mechanical paths. The relative motion and distribution within those paths can matter to healing. And the mechanical environment remains one participant in a biological history.

Those conclusions are stronger than the generic claim that “structure matters.” They are weaker than the claim that a restored path determines recovery. Their strength comes from stating the boundary clearly.

They also reveal several meanings hidden inside the word *success*. A construct can achieve its selected alignment and restraint. Repair tissue can develop over time. The person can regain a valued function. Complications can remain absent or occur despite the earlier mechanical result. These outcomes can support one another, but no one of them substitutes for the others.

Suppose a radiograph shows maintained alignment while healing is delayed. The mechanical endpoint should not be retroactively declared unreal simply because the biological outcome was unfavorable. The account should ask whether the measured stability was adequate for the intended strategy, whether local motion or distribution differed from what the image could show, and which biological or contextual conditions participated. The reverse also holds: healing after a particular construct does not prove that only that mechanical environment could have succeeded.

This is why fixation is a useful foundation for the book rather than proof of a universal routing doctrine. It shows that the path through an assembly can be intentionally changed and that the change can matter. It also shows that mechanics becomes medically meaningful through receiver state, time, and outcome rather than by displacing them.

## Decompression changes a local relation

The term *decompression* can sound as though a procedure proves a complete mechanical explanation. It does not. It names an intended change in contact, pressure, deformation, or available space within a particular system. Whether that change identifies cause, predicts recovery, or explains symptoms is an additional question.

Carpal-tunnel pressure studies provide a measured example. In a prospective study of 103 women aged twenty to fifty with unilateral idiopathic carpal tunnel syndrome and 25 controls, canal pressure varied with wrist position and was higher in the clinical group before surgery. It fell markedly immediately after open release, rose somewhat by two months, and remained within the control range during one year of follow-up.[^17-06] The operation changed a local pressure condition. That is a meaningful mechanical result.

Another operative study measured 48 wrists in 39 people before and after endoscopic release. Maximum static pressure with the wrist neutral fell from 56.7 to 7.4 mm Hg. Preoperative maximum pressure did not correlate with electrodiagnostic severity or median-nerve cross-sectional area, although the latter two measures correlated with each other.[^17-07] A large pressure change, an anatomical measure, and an electrophysiological measure did not collapse into one severity scale.

This example preserves the distinction developed in Chapter 13. In an extrinsic account, a maintaining relation precedes the geometry and pressure it produces. In an intrinsic account, tissue state may persist within the receiver. In a mixed account, both participate. Release can demonstrate that changing one boundary changes a selected local quantity. It cannot retrospectively observe which combination initiated the condition or how every tissue responded through time.

Treatment response must be held to the same rule. Symptom improvement after a mechanical intervention can support the relevance of the targeted relation to that outcome. It does not prove that the relation was the sole cause, that a body-wide route produced it, or that the same intervention would have the same meaning elsewhere. Conversely, imperfect symptom recovery does not erase a measured pressure change. Endpoint, tissue state, symptom, and history are different records.

Decompression is thus not a metaphysical restoration of “space.” It is an intervention with an anatomical boundary and one or more measurable mechanical consequences. The precision of that local account is exactly what prevents it from becoming proof of a larger story.

## Living interfaces make distribution visible

A device can survive while the tissue receiving its load does poorly. This fact makes distribution a medical-engineering concern rather than a refinement added after component strength.

Consider a prosthetic socket. Ground contact and inertia produce forces and moments that must cross an interface of finite area. Socket geometry, suspension, alignment, material, residual-limb volume, active control, and the chosen task all affect how that transfer occurs. Pressure and shear vary by site and phase. Comfort, skin state, participation, and long-term tolerance are later, non-equivalent outcomes.

In one study, ten men with unilateral transfemoral amputation used three socket designs during level walking, ramp ascent and descent, and stair ascent and descent. Sensors recorded pressure in anterior, lateral, posterior, and medial regions. Patterns and extrema varied with location and task; small design groups and sensor failures limited the comparison.[^17-08] The result establishes local and task-dependent distribution, not a universal socket ranking or tissue threshold.

A single-person transtibial study changed sock layers to simulate looser and tighter fit while measuring both pressure and shear at three sites during standing and level walking. Small fit changes produced site- and phase-specific differences, and pressure and shear did not rise or fall as one uniform quantity.[^17-09] The observation is illustrative rather than population-level evidence. Its value here is conceptual: a map of pressure alone does not contain the complete interface traction state.

Time adds another dimension. In a single-participant alignment study, peak pressure, duration above 90 percent of that peak, and time–pressure integral changed differently across sagittal alignment conditions.[^17-10] A peak, a duration, and an integrated exposure answer different questions. None independently determines comfort, tissue perfusion, injury, or person-valued use.

The interface example connects established practice to this book's language of routing. Care already asks where externally applied demand goes at a local boundary. It measures parts of the distribution, changes geometry or fit, and checks consequences. But a local pressure map is not a whole-body route. The person's gait, assistance, environment, tissue state, and prior history can all influence it without being recoverable from it.

A complete-looking color map can hide that incompleteness. Sensors sample selected locations and frequencies. A measurement made during level walking does not automatically represent stairs, slopes, prolonged standing, heat, fatigue, or a change in residual-limb volume. Pressure at the surface does not directly measure strain inside every tissue layer, and pressure alone omits tangential traction. The map can still be valuable. Its resolution and task define what it can resolve.

The person supplies information the map cannot replace. Comfort, confidence, security, participation, effort, and the value of a device are not errors added to an otherwise objective mechanical result. They are separate outcomes of the person–device system. A low pressure peak is not a sufficient definition of a good interface, just as a strong component is not a sufficient definition of a usable prosthesis.

The same logic applies beyond prosthetics. A surgical plate transfers demand through screw–bone and plate–bone relations. A support surface distributes contact over skin and deeper tissue. An orthosis changes motion and pressure at several boundaries. Established practice therefore already contains a local routing question: how does an external or reconstructed assembly share demand with living material? The proposed longitudinal framework must add something beyond that mature question rather than merely renaming it.

## An endpoint does not contain its route

The examples now permit a general distinction. An *endpoint record* asks what the present mechanical condition is, which quantity can be changed, and whether the selected change occurred. A *history record* asks how the condition developed: which demands entered, which paths were available, what substituted or compensated, what exposure reached a receiver, and what changed next.

The endpoint record can be rich. It may include repeated measurements, tissue state, symptoms, function, and response to intervention. Calling it local does not make it simplistic. The history record can be poor. It may be assembled from memory, current appearance, and a model whose assumptions were never tested. Calling it whole-body does not make it comprehensive or true.

Several histories can converge on a similar endpoint. Elevated interface pressure can reflect anatomy, swelling, device fit, alignment, task, active strategy, or combinations. A fracture construct can face similar mechanical demands after different injuries and within different biological states. Canal pressure can vary with position and local tissue conditions without uniquely encoding the events that produced them.

Several endpoints can also follow one history. The same injury can lead to different repair environments, compensations, symptoms, and functional results. The same period of ventilatory support can expose heterogeneous regions differently. The same device alignment can interact with different tissue states, strategies, and goals. A history therefore does not behave like a code that deterministically generates one endpoint, any more than an endpoint behaves like a recording of its past.

This many-to-many relation is the real reason to distinguish the records. It is not an argument that causation is unknowable. It is a warning that causal resolution must come from chronology, discriminating measurements, intervention, comparison, and explicit assumptions—not from the smoothness of a narrative. In favorable cases, those sources can strongly narrow the history. In others, a plural account is the most accurate result.

The inverse is also possible: a well-documented event can make part of the history unusually clear. A recorded trauma, a known experimental manipulation, or repeated prospective measurements may identify the earlier state better than a later reconstruction. Even then, the route from event to tissue response and outcome can contain uncertain intermediate steps.

This is why an elaborate story is not automatically an advance. A history earns relevance only if it changes a defined result. It might improve explanation, prediction, recurrence analysis, measurement, communication, or research design. It might reveal that two apparently similar endpoints arose under different exposures. It might also add nothing, create false confidence, or duplicate work already performed by biomechanics, rehabilitation, ergonomics, or occupational medicine.

## The proposal belongs inside the existing landscape

The project can now state its proposed addition without accusing medicine of forgetting the body. Mechanical reasoning is already distributed across specialties. Biomechanics measures and models motion, force, moments, contact, and internal distribution. Rehabilitation studies function, capacity, learning, support, response, and adaptation. Ergonomics and occupational health study tasks, environments, and exposure. Surgery and device fields intentionally alter local geometry, stability, contact, and transfer. Medical specialties define tissue states, diagnoses, immediate risks, and endpoints.

The proposed question is whether a more explicit relationship among those observations is sometimes useful. What demand entered the person–environment system? Which paths and supports were available? What changed across phase, region, and time? What strategy preserved function? Which local exposure is observed or modeled? What biological response is separately supported? Which alternative history fits the same endpoint?

At this stage, that is an integration proposal, not an established new framework. Its components have substantial precedent. Its added value has to be shown relative to those precedents. A valid result may be that an existing discipline already contains the needed record. It may be that the history cannot be reconstructed reliably. It may be that the local endpoint is already explained and managed well enough for the purpose at hand.

Those outcomes do not make mechanics less medical. They determine where the longitudinal proposal earns a role.

## Common ground, then a harder test

Medicine already reasons in pressure, flow, volume, stability, contact, geometry, support, and distribution. It substitutes for physical functions, constructs mechanical environments for healing, changes local constraints, and designs interfaces that must transmit demand through living tissue. These are not metaphors. They are established forms of care and research.

Their limits are equally clear. A pressure change is not a complete nerve history. A stable construct is not healing by itself. A device-interface map is not tissue tolerance or person-valued function. A successful endpoint does not uniquely identify its cause. And a complete upstream reconstruction is not always required for effective action.

Book I therefore does not propose to add mechanics to medicine. It proposes to ask whether mechanical observations already held in different places can be related across time without losing their provenance, system boundary, or alternatives. That relationship must do more than sound coherent.

Chapter 18 takes up the harder test. It will define a longitudinal routing record, compare it with existing biomechanics, exposure science, rehabilitation, and clinical frameworks, and identify results that would support, narrow, duplicate, or disfavor it. Only then can the book claim an addition rather than a restatement.
