# Chapter 6 — Active, tensile, compliant, pressure-bearing, and tension-network systems

The object is partway between two supports. Chapter 5 could trace a changing skeletal graph from the hands through linked joints toward the floor. That graph made geometry, contact, and restraint visible, but it remained deliberately incomplete. Bones cannot accept the object by themselves. A joint surface does not decide where to move next. No skeletal line explains how the hand closes around the object, how a segment is held or decelerated, how contact remains viable while surfaces move, or why the same visible pose can feel and respond differently after a recent contraction.

Return to departure, the phase in which the first table is progressively giving up support. Muscle changes force and the resistance of the assembly to displacement. Tendons transmit tension while deforming and changing the timing of energy exchange. Ligaments and capsules become recruited according to configuration. Cartilage distributes contact through a fluid–solid material response. Skin, sheaths, bursae, and fascial planes receive form and permit relative movement. Pressure acts on bounded surfaces. Sensory information and prior mechanical state affect the active response that follows. None of these is an accessory layer around the real skeleton. Together they make skeletal possibilities usable.

This chapter develops that cooperation without turning it into a tissue inventory. The same transfer remains in view while the division of labor changes. A simple handoff from active or form-receiving tissue toward tensile and skeletal resolution will remain visible, because it captures an important first approximation. The complete event, however, branches, feeds back, and changes through time. That difference is the boundary between a useful trace and a claim that one force packet travels through one body-wide cable.

## Muscle changes both force and mechanical state

Muscle is not merely a motor pulling on a passive frame. An active muscle can shorten, remain at approximately the same length, or lengthen while producing force. Through those states it can accelerate a segment, hold a relation, brake motion, redirect a resultant, alter joint contact, or change the apparent stiffness of a larger assembly. The force available at a given instant depends on activation, muscle length, shortening or lengthening velocity, architecture, passive elements, recent contraction history, and the boundary conditions of the muscle–tendon unit.[^06-01]

Those dependencies matter during the transfer. At first contact, relatively small active forces can position the hand and establish the object relation. As support leaves the table, active force changes in magnitude and direction. During a steady-looking hold, object acceleration may be small while muscle force remains necessary. During placement, active muscle can absorb or redirect energy as the second surface progressively takes over the object's support. The visible task does not identify one contraction mode or one distribution among muscles.

Activation can also change how an assembly responds to a perturbation. Agonist–antagonist coactivation may increase its apparent resistance to displacement, but that result is task- and model-dependent. Greater coactivation is not automatically greater stability, lower cost, or better organization. Surface electromyography adds another important distinction: it records electrical activity through a particular measurement process; it does not directly measure individual-muscle force, especially during dynamic tasks.[^06-02] Motion, activation, force, joint moment, stiffness, and task stability are related objects, not interchangeable observations.

This active layer changes the meaning of a load path. The candidate route available before contact is not simply filled with force when the object leaves the table. Muscle helps create the route by changing segment resultants, joint contact, tensile state, and response to disturbance. Several muscle-force combinations may remain compatible with the same external balance. An internal path is therefore a state-dependent model of cooperation, not a wire that becomes visible when the person exerts effort.

The relevant muscle description also depends on the chosen boundary. At the whole-person boundary, muscle forces are internal and do not appear in the external balance. Around a selected segment or joint, modeled muscle and tendon forces may cross the boundary and become explicit unknowns. At the tissue scale, architecture, length, velocity, activation, and passive state enter the calculation. Moving inward does not reveal one force at higher magnification. It creates a new model with new measurements and assumptions.

This is why a named muscle cannot be assigned one task-level meaning from anatomy alone. A large superficial muscle may contribute substantially to a resultant while smaller or deeper muscles alter local contact, segmental relation, or timing. The division can change as the object leaves the table, is redirected, and comes to rest. A serious account can estimate those contributions, but it must state the task, phase, geometry, measurement, and optimization or control assumptions that produced the estimate. The chapter's narrower point is already secure: active tissue changes both the force inventory and the mechanical state in which passive structures participate.

## Tensile tissues transmit while they deform

Tendon makes the first-order handoff especially clear. Active muscle produces or modulates force; tendon carries tension toward an attachment; the attachment distributes that interaction into bone; skeletal geometry and joint contact participate in resolving the task. This is a real and useful anatomical sequence. It also includes more mechanical behavior than a motor connected to a lossless cord.

Tendon deforms. Depending on anatomy and phase, that deformation can store and return energy, delay its transfer, amplify power, or attenuate the power delivered to active muscle during energy absorption. In experiments on landing turkeys, temporary tendon energy storage reduced muscle-fascicle lengthening velocity and power absorption during that particular high-rate task. A study of two equine tendons also found different interfascicular sliding in the tested energy-storing and positional tendons.[^06-03] These are bounded examples, not universal human rules, but they establish the relevant possibility: a tensile carrier can receive form and change the time course of demand while continuing to transmit substantial force.

Direction further changes the role. Ex vivo experiments on two porcine wrap-around tendons found region- and orientation-dependent behavior under longitudinal tension, transverse compression, and combined loading.[^06-04] A tendon may therefore be comparatively shape-preserving along a tensile direction while accommodating compression, shear, bending, or sliding elsewhere. In the hard/soft vocabulary motivated by traditional Chinese medicine and introduced in Chapter 3, the same tissue can perform a relatively hard role in one declared relation and a form-receiving soft role in another. Tissue name does not assign the role.

Ligaments share tensile and time-dependent features but enter the task differently. As Chapter 5 showed, their recruitment changes with configuration. A recruited ligament can guide or restrain one relative motion while remaining deformable and while other motion remains available. A capsule can contain and help guide a joint rather than acting as a simple bag or a fixed end stop. Tendon, ligament, and capsule can all carry meaningful demand without becoming permanently hard; slackness, tension, and deformation acquire meaning only with a task, direction, phase, and boundary.

The sequence from active tissue to tendon or restraint to attachment, bone, and contact should thus be retained as a highlighted path, not promoted into a universal conveyor. Several muscles and tendons can act at once. Ligament, fascia, pressure, and external support can form parallel branches. Force components can change direction, energy can be stored or returned, and contact can migrate. A handoff names a changing relation among carriers; it does not mean that one invariant quantity passes unchanged through each anatomical name.

An attachment makes this nonconveyor picture especially important. Tendon or ligament does not terminate at a dimensionless point. Its interaction is distributed through a graded region into bone and neighboring tissues. The local expression can include tension, compression, shear, bending, and contact, even when the task-level story begins with longitudinal tensile transfer. The attachment therefore changes how a relation is carried; it does not simply pass an unchanged scalar from one material to the next.

Reciprocity also matters. When a joint relation changes, it changes muscle–tendon length and the recruitment of passive restraints. When a tendon deforms, it changes the timing and velocity experienced by muscle fascicles. When an external support accepts more of the object, active and tensile demands can change before any anatomical structure has moved very far. The handoff is directional enough to trace and reciprocal enough that it cannot be treated as a one-way pipeline.

## Deformation and excursion are part of support

Support is sometimes pictured as the elimination of deformation. Living interfaces show why that is wrong. Articular cartilage is a fluid-saturated solid. Under specified tests, interstitial-fluid pressurization can carry a large share of contact demand and contribute to low friction while the solid matrix deforms. Direct measurements in human and bovine cartilage specimens under unconfined compression found high peak fluid-load-support fractions, with the result linked to the material's tension–compression nonlinearity.[^06-05] Those fractions belong to those specimens and protocols, not to every joint or phase. The general lesson is enough: deformation and fluid movement can be part of successful support.

Relative movement matters as well. A tendon can move within a sheath; neighboring muscles can move relative to one another; fascial planes can separate structures while permitting glide; a bursa can help two surfaces change relation. These interfaces are not empty spaces between the load-bearing parts. Their excursion is one of the mechanical jobs that lets support continue while shape changes.

Four descriptions should remain distinct. Gross task motion describes what the body or object does. *Excursion* or *glide* describes relative movement between named structures. *Deformation* describes change in a selected structure relative to a reference. Contact pressure, traction, stress, or strain describes a more local interaction or material state and requires its own measurement or model. One observation does not automatically supply the next.

This is where the hard/soft polarity becomes more precise. A form-receiving role is not a state of being unloaded. Cartilage can receive form while carrying contact demand. A tendon can deform while transmitting tension. A pressure-bounded compartment can change shape while supporting a larger relation. Conversely, a structure that appears soft by tissue class can become comparatively shape-preserving when its tension, pressure, geometry, or constraints make it so. The positive baseline is controlled accommodation: the larger task relation persists because local structures can deform and move where their functions require it.

That baseline does not imply that more deformation or more glide is always better. An interface needs the excursion appropriate to its task, geometry, and state. Too little or too much movement can matter under particular conditions, but the value cannot be inferred from magnitude alone. The first question is functional: what relative movement is required for the selected task, and what neighboring relation must remain organized while it occurs? Only then can a comparison ask whether contact, active control, or a material state altered that freedom.

The distinction also prepares a later negative case without importing it here. Chapter 13 will examine a form-receiving structure whose deformation or lost excursion is maintained by another boundary. That adverse pattern is intelligible only because ordinary form reception has first been presented positively. A yielding interface is not failed support. It becomes a candidate problem only when a specified relation maintains a consequential state, exceeds a capacity, or prevents a movement needed for the selected function.

## Pressure supports only through a boundary

Pressure has a legitimate structural role. In the force balance for a selected body, a pressure field contributes through traction on a declared boundary surface. For an idealized pressure field on a boundary portion, its resultant contribution can be written

\[
\mathbf F_p = \int_A -p\,\mathbf n\,dA,
\]

where \(A\) is the selected surface, \(p\) is pressure, and \(\mathbf n\) is the outward unit normal for the selected material boundary. The minus sign makes the pressure traction point inward under that convention. The expression is not a whole-body law. Its job is to prevent pressure from floating free of geometry. Uniform pressure over a complete closed surface produces no net translational resultant on the enclosed body because the surface normals cancel. A pressure difference across a deformable wall can nevertheless generate wall stress and alter or resist deformation. Structural consequence depends on pressure differences, boundary shape, wall behavior, active control, contacts, constraints, and phase.

Hydrostatic skeletons provide a clear biological existence proof. Muscles and connective tissues act around approximately volume-conserving contents so that internal pressure, boundary geometry, and active control can support shape, produce movement, and change stiffness without a rigid internal skeleton.[^06-06] This does not make a human abdomen equivalent to an invertebrate hydrostat. It establishes that pressure-mediated support is mechanically coherent when the boundary and mechanism are specified.

A small human experiment supplies a narrower example. In three participants lying prone, phrenic-nerve stimulation increased intra-abdominal pressure without concurrent abdominal- or back-extensor activity and increased the study's measured lumbar-stiffness response by 8–31 percent.[^06-07] The setup was invasive, the sample was tiny, and the endpoint was specific to the experiment. It was not a measure of posture, pain, health, voluntary breathing, or whole-body organization. The result shows that a pressure change can alter a bounded structural response; it does not make pressure the universal core force or breathing a treatment.

Cartilage fluid pressurization, hydrostatic skeletons, and intra-abdominal pressure are therefore three different systems. They operate at different scales and through different materials and boundaries. Pooling them would produce a grander story and a weaker model. Their shared lesson is exact: pressure can participate in support only through identified surfaces, geometry, material behavior, and phase.

This discipline also separates several quantities that ordinary language groups together. Cavity pressure is a field within a fluid or gas. Contact pressure describes the normal component of traction over an interface. Wall stress describes a state in the bounding material and is not numerically identical to either pressure without geometry and a model. A reported sense of internal effort is an experience, not a pressure measurement. The quantities can change together, but a technical argument must name the one actually observed or calculated.

The boundary can itself be active and deformable. Abdominal or thoracic walls, joint surfaces, and hydrostatic tissues do not merely receive a pressure supplied from elsewhere. Their geometry, tension, motion, and constraints participate in producing the pressure–volume relation and in determining where surface traction acts. This is why ordinary breathing can change geometry and pressure without proving that breath is the master organizer of support. Book IV can ask the stronger phase- and route-specific question; Book I needs only the bounded mechanics that make that question possible.

## Fascia belongs in the model at named scales

*Fascia* does not name one mechanically uniform object. Anatomical and clinical literatures use the term at different scales, and current proposals continue to distinguish named fasciae from broader descriptions of a fascial system.[^06-08] A useful account therefore begins with anatomy: an intermuscular septum, aponeurosis, retinaculum, sheath, compartment wall, or regional sheet such as thoracolumbar fascia. The proposed job then follows—connection, separation, containment, attachment, glide, sensory participation, or tensile transfer.

These jobs are not merely rhetorical. In eight unembalmed cadavers, investigators applied 10 N traction to named muscle attachments and measured tension and marker displacement in posterior and middle lumbar fasciae. The experiment demonstrated regional tensile transmission under its dissection and loading conditions.[^06-09] It did not measure living neural control, whole-body route dominance, clinical consequence, or a traditional channel. The bounded result remains valuable because it establishes more than visual continuity and less than a governing global network.

The scale transition can be organized as a continuity ladder:

1. named anatomical tissues are connected;
2. a local perturbation produces measurable tension or deformation elsewhere in a declared region;
3. the magnitude is consequential during a specified living task;
4. that relation changes a task-level or clinical endpoint after other carriers and control are considered; and
5. a body-wide network explains or predicts the result better than competing models.

Systematic reviews have found anatomical support for some proposed myofascial continuities and evidence of transfer between some adjacent structures, while also emphasizing heterogeneous methods, anatomical variation, and incomplete evidence for in vivo functional significance. A 2026 meta-analysis reported remote stiffness changes measured during local tissue lengthening or muscle contraction, but certainty, activity monitoring, and methods varied; remote stiffness did not identify the responsible carrier by itself.[^06-10] These findings place evidence on particular rungs. They neither erase long-range organization nor complete it.

This ladder is deliberately hospitable to the larger research program. A whole-body tension network remains possible. So do regional relations whose importance depends on task, state, or person. What does not survive is promotion by appearance: a continuous dissection line, coordinated motion, or remote stiffness change cannot by itself reveal the magnitude, carrier, direction, or consequence of force transfer across the entire body.

Fascia's separating and containing jobs are as important to this argument as connection. A septum or compartment wall can organize neighboring structures without serving as a long-distance cable. A retinaculum can constrain a tendon near a changing skeletal path. An aponeurosis can distribute muscle force across a broad attachment. A fascial plane can permit relative motion between layers. The same named tissue can therefore connect in one relation and preserve separation in another. Treating connection as fascia's only meaningful property would erase half of its mechanical contribution.

The task also helps distinguish coordination from material transfer. The hand, trunk, pelvis, and feet can participate in one organized event because neural control, skeletal linkage, external contacts, and several tissues couple them. That whole-task coordination does not require a large share of force to travel through one continuous fascial sheet. Conversely, a bounded fascial transfer may be mechanically consequential even if it does not govern the whole task. The network question becomes sharper when those possibilities are allowed to differ.

## The task begins before visible movement

When two transfers look the same at first contact, they need not begin from the same mechanical state. Recent contraction history can change passive muscle resistance and subsequent active force. Joint contact and ligament recruitment can differ. Local pressure, support, fatigue, and neural preparation can differ. The ending pose from a previous action can resemble a fresh starting pose while the internal conditions are not identical. Reviews of muscle history dependence and thixotropy support this narrower claim: prior movement and contraction can affect later mechanical response without establishing one body-wide value called tone.[^06-11]

Three levels should remain separate. A *local baseline state* belongs to a named tissue or small assembly and can include activation, passive tension, length history, force–displacement response, or local pressure. *Regional prestress* describes tension or compression already present in a declared regional model before another demand is added; regional preparation may also include coactivation, contact, or pressure. A *global set-point hypothesis* proposes coordinated whole-person state across regions. That last proposal is part of the research program, but it requires simultaneous multiscale variables, a formal relation among them, and predictions that a collection of local states does not already supply.

Prestress is a mechanics term, not a synonym for readiness, health, or tensegrity. Baseline tone is useful only after the text says whether it means neural activation, passive muscle resistance, connective-tissue tension, palpated resistance, pressure, or a model parameter. No evidence reviewed here shows that those quantities collapse into one scalar or that an evenly distributed value defines an ideal body.

The positive conclusion is stronger than a warning about words. Initial state is mechanically consequential. The next demand enters an assembly whose active and passive tensions, contacts, pressures, material histories, and support conditions already exist. A load path can therefore change even before the visible geometry changes. That insight prepares later chapters to discuss history, retained state, and capacity without pretending that posture alone contains the past.

Consider two otherwise matched holds of the same object. In one, the person has just shortened a relevant muscle group; in the other, the muscle has recently been lengthened or the object has already been held for some time. The external configuration can be nearly identical while passive resistance, active-force capacity, sensory state, or fatigue differs. The example does not predict which trial is superior. It shows why a photograph and object mass cannot fully specify the system that receives the next perturbation.

Initial state is also distributed rather than purely internal. A hand remaining on the table, a mobility aid, footwear–ground contact, or assistance from another person changes the support conditions before the object moves. These external relations can alter active preparation and candidate internal paths. A whole-system state description therefore needs contacts and environment as well as tissue variables; otherwise a proposed global tone can become a name for differences that actually entered through the boundary.

## From a tissue handoff to a network model

The complete object transfer now has a layered account. At contact, skin and other local interfaces conform while muscle positions the hand. During acceptance, active force and tendon tension change as the first table continues to share support. At departure, joint contacts migrate, restraints recruit, and object acceleration enters the external balance. During carrying, changing foot contacts, active force, tensile transfer, pressure-bearing boundaries, glide, and sensory control continue despite a relatively steady object. During placement, the second table gradually accepts support while muscle–tendon units brake, store, return, or dissipate energy. After release, pressure, active force, and material deformation need not return on identical timescales.

Within that event, the first-order soft-to-hard trace remains visible:

```text
active or form-receiving tissue accepts, generates, or redirects demand
    -> tendon, ligament, fascia, capsule, or pressure boundary transfers or constrains it
    -> attachment, contact, or interface changes its local expression
    -> skeletal geometry and external support participate in resolution
```

The trace is not the whole event. It sits inside parallel active structures, simultaneous contacts, pressure-mediated contributions, external supports, and feedback. Force and energy can branch or reverse direction. Different quantities appear at different boundaries. Mass and acceleration belong to force-and-moment accounting; inertia is never another anatomical route.

An integrated description still does not require one preferred mathematical model. The same bounded question can be represented in several ways:

| Model family | What it represents well | What it requires | When to stop using it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linked-segment or multibody | Task-level motions, contacts, resultants, and moments | Geometry, mass properties, constraints, contacts, and active-force assumptions | When the local tissue field is the question |
| Continuum or finite element | Stress, strain, contact, and deformation in a selected region | Geometry, constitutive laws, boundary and initial conditions, and validation | When inputs are unavailable or the selected scale is impractical |
| Muscle–tendon and control | State-dependent active force, redundancy, and timing | Muscle–tendon geometry, properties, activation, and a control model | When passive, pressure, or contact carriers remain essential and unspecified |
| Pressure-network | Cavity or fluid-saturated support | Pressure field, boundary geometry, wall behavior, and pressure differences | When no bounded pressure system has been defined |
| Broad tension-network | Named active, tensile, compressive, contact, and pressure relations | Carrier map, state variables, component and control rules, boundaries, and predictions | When it only relabels components or predicts nothing new |
| Formal tensegrity | A prestressed topology with discrete compression members held in a continuous or distributed tensile relation | Nodes, connectivity, member laws, prestress, constraints, equilibrium, and contact rules | When no stable mapping can be built or it adds no discriminating prediction |

Formal tensegrity is therefore narrower than the broad tension-network idea. A tensegrity model requires more than the visual resemblance of bones to struts and fascia to cables: it needs declared tensile and compressive members, a topology that keeps its discrete compression members within a continuous or distributed tensile relation, member behavior, boundaries, and a prestressed equilibrium from which static or dynamic response can be calculated.[^06-12] Distributed tension and compression in the body make tensegrity a legitimate representation to test; they do not select it in advance. The model may clarify how prestress changes stiffness, how a perturbation redistributes, or how shape persists. It does not prove that all anatomy forms one tensegrity, that one configuration is preferred, or that a medical effect follows.

A broader network can admit joint contact, active members, multiaxial tissues, pressure boundaries, changing topology, and external support. That flexibility better preserves the full research intuition. It also increases the obligation to name carriers and rules. The model survives when it predicts or clarifies a bounded relation better than linked-segment, continuum, muscle-control, or pressure accounts. If it can absorb every observation without risking a different prediction, it remains a metaphor rather than an explanatory model.

A discriminating comparison could begin with one phase of the transfer. The linked-segment model predicts aggregate joint resultants. A muscle–tendon model proposes how active and tensile contributors share them. A continuum model predicts a local deformation or stress field. A pressure model predicts the contribution of a bounded cavity or hydrated interface. A broad network model earns additional standing only if its cross-component state predicts a response those models miss or unifies their outputs without losing testability. A formal tensegrity model faces the still narrower test of whether an anatomically defensible prestressed topology produces a distinctive result.

The stop rule is practical. If the proposed network cannot be specified before the outcome, if a predicted carrier does not participate, if remote change is equally explained by active control or an external contact, or if a simpler model predicts the same endpoint with fewer assumptions, the network claim should be narrowed for that task. This is not a rejection of integration. It is how an integrated hypothesis becomes capable of accumulating evidence rather than merely accumulating components.

## A cooperative architecture

Skeletal geometry becomes usable living support through an organized exchange. Muscle generates and modulates force while changing the state of an assembly. Tendons transmit tension while storing or returning energy and while delaying or attenuating power transfer. Ligaments and capsules guide through changing recruitment. Cartilage and other interfaces support through deformation and excursion. Pressure shares structural work through identified fluid or gas domains and their boundary surfaces. Named fasciae connect, separate, contain, permit glide, and sometimes transmit measurable tension. Sensory control and prior state help determine what the system does next.

These relations establish more than a list of tissues and less than one proven body-wide network. Local continuity, regional transfer, task-level consequence, and whole-body model advantage remain separate levels of claim. The first-order movement from active or form-receiving tissue toward tensile and skeletal resolution is retained inside a branched, reciprocal, time-varying event. Hard and soft remain relative mechanical roles: shape preservation and form reception cooperate, and either can be performed by tissues that ordinary anatomy would classify as soft.

The chapter's integrated account is therefore deliberately plural. Linked-segment, continuum, muscle-control, pressure, broad tension-network, and formal tensegrity models answer different questions. A whole-system model must earn its place by defining variables, boundaries, carriers, and a result that its alternatives do not already explain. Nothing in this conclusion selects a preferred configuration, a breath-driven organizer, a clinical mechanism, or a method of practice.

The next question is anatomical. Where do large extensor muscles, posterior connective tissues, the spine, pelvis, shoulder girdle, and lower-limb structures form real regional continuities and task-participating relations? Chapter 7 maps the posterior chain at those bounded scales. Only after that anatomy is clear can Chapter 8 ask what posterior structures actually do in ordinary function.
