# Chapter 25 — Instability, repeated demand, and changing capacity

## The same event does not happen to the same ankle twice

Suppose two people describe a later episode in the same words: *the ankle gave way again*. The visible task might also look similar. Each person plants a foot, the ankle turns, the movement is interrupted, and weight shifts elsewhere. That resemblance does not establish the same material state, the same control problem, the same internal distribution, or even a new injury in both people.

One ankle might retain a measurable change in passive restraint after an earlier sprain. Another might not. One person might complete demanding tasks reliably while reporting uncertainty; another might report confidence but encounter a new perturbation beyond current capacity. Pain, swelling, attention, practice, conditioning, footwear, assistance, participation, and opportunity for exposure can all differ. A later event therefore begins from the state left by earlier demand, response, recovery, adaptation, learning, care, and behavior.

This opening is a reconstruction from several studies, not a report of two patients. No source followed one person continuously from a first sprain through passive testing, every task and recovery interval, perceived instability, proximal motion, internal tissue exposure, and a later event. The studies assembled here define different cohorts, ask different questions, and use different tasks. Their seams matter. A person called a *coper* in one study does not necessarily satisfy another study's definition, and a landing result cannot be transferred automatically to walking, cutting, or an unobserved recurrence.

The evidence is useful precisely because its measures do not always agree. Research criteria for chronic ankle instability were developed in response to substantial variation in participant selection and reporting.[^25-01] A later theoretical model separated the primary injury from pathomechanical, sensory-perceptual, motor-behavioral, personal, environmental, and outcome components.[^25-02] These frameworks do not identify one mechanism in an individual. They establish why the word *instability* should not be allowed to do the work of a complete history.

The chapter's claim is simpler and stronger than a list of possible factors:

> Repeated demand acts on a changing receiver. Each event can alter the material, sensory, motor, experiential, behavioral, and exposure state that receives the next demand, but the direction and consequence of that alteration must be established rather than assumed.

That claim permits decline, but it also permits recovery, increased capacity, useful protection, a new and unrelated event, or no important retained change. Recurrence is not a downward staircase. It is a sequence of state transitions whose branches have to be observed or tested.

The word *event* also needs a boundary. A recorded sprain, a reported episode of giving way, loss of balance during a laboratory task, and failure to complete a task are not interchangeable outcomes. Each has a different observation rule, timeframe, and relationship to exposure. Even two recorded sprains need not reproduce the same direction, contact, material disturbance, or recovery course. A credible chronology preserves the identity of each event instead of treating the shared label as proof of a shared internal mechanism.

This makes evidence state part of the physical account. A documented event is observed at the resolution of its record. A passive displacement, force-platform trace, angle, questionnaire score, or radiograph is measured under a stated method. A net joint moment or modeled internal force is calculated under assumptions. A transition assembled from observations months apart is reconstructed. A proposed carrier between ankle and hip is a hypothesis until an appropriate measurement or manipulation tests it. The grammar is not decorative caution. It prevents a plausible timeline from filling its own missing intervals.

## Three different objects inside one word

The first step is to separate three objects that are often compressed into *instability*.

**Passive restraint** concerns displacement, rotation, stiffness, or resistance under a declared passive test and direction. It may reflect articular geometry, ligament behavior, other tissues, and the apparatus used. **Task-level relation maintenance** concerns whether a named relation remains within, or returns to, a declared tolerance during a named demand. It may be described through motion, force, pressure, timing, perturbation, performance, and recovery. **Perceived instability** concerns a person's report of giving way, uncertainty, difficulty, or lack of confidence under stated conditions. Each object is meaningful. None measures the other two by implication.

A cross-sectional study of 364 young male prerecruitment infantry soldiers makes the combinatorial problem concrete. The investigators separated perceived instability, mechanically examined instability, recurrent sprains, and their combinations into eight subgroups; the prevalence and neuromuscular findings differed among those combinations.[^25-03] The study does not establish causal order, and its population cannot stand for everyone. Its structural lesson is durable: these states can occur together or apart.

The book's hard-and-soft vocabulary can describe the local division of mechanical labor without turning the distinction into a tissue taxonomy. The vocabulary is motivated by traditional Chinese medicine concepts of hard and soft, and of Yin and Yang, but is not definitionally identical to them. In a selected direction and phase, articular geometry, ligament, muscle-tendon stiffness, ground contact, footwear, or an external support can take relatively shape-preserving roles. Cartilage, synovial structures, muscle, tendon, skin, and other tissues can take relatively form-receiving roles by yielding, conforming, gliding, containing, or accommodating. A ligament can preserve a relation while deforming. A muscle can stiffen in one direction while yielding in another. Hard and soft name roles within a relation, not fixed lists of structures and not a verdict about whether the relation is healthy.

The comparison studies fill several cells that a one-variable account cannot represent. In an instrumented inversion test of sixteen participants with chronic ankle instability, sixteen copers, and sixteen controls, the chronic-instability group showed greater inversion than controls but did not differ significantly from copers; stiffness did not differ among the three groups.[^25-04] Under that test, passive inversion did not distinguish people classified with persistent difficulty from people classified as coping after injury.

A complementary result appeared in a comparison of thirty-seven chronic-instability participants, thirty copers, and twenty-six controls. Soleus spinal-reflex excitability differed among groups, while instrumented anterior-posterior and inversion-eversion laxity did not.[^25-05] The reflex measure is not the whole of proprioception, task control, or recurrence. The cross-sectional difference could precede injury, follow it, reflect participation, or arise through another shared cause. Still, the result prevents an absence of passive group difference from being treated as proof that every neural or task relation is the same.

Reports and selected performance measures also part company. In a comparison of twenty-four copers with twenty-four people classified with chronic ankle instability, self-reported outcomes distinguished the groups more strongly than selected force-platform stabilization measures, while sagittal talar position had poor discriminating utility.[^25-06] Those discrimination results are not diagnostic cutoffs for a reader to apply. They show that report, task performance, and one structural-position measure carried different information.

Static balance cannot absorb the category either. In a small study of fifteen healthy participants, fourteen people after a primary inversion injury, and twenty-three people with chronic ankle instability, functional ankle scores differed, but the selected static-balance measures did not; the balance measures also did not correlate with the functional scores.[^25-07] This does not mean balance never matters. It means that *instability is impaired balance* is not a sufficiently precise proposition.

The most demanding noncorrespondence comes from landing. Twenty-eight people with self-reported chronic ankle instability and twenty-eight matched controls completed barefoot forward and side jump landings. The chronic-instability group reported greater instability during both tasks and greater difficulty during the forward jump, but the study did not detect a group difference in the whole-curve hip, knee, or ankle kinematics.[^25-08] The null is specific to those tasks, that model, that sample, and its precision. It cannot prove equivalence. It does prevent the reported experience from being converted into an unobserved lower-limb kinematic deviation—and prevents a kinematic null from erasing the experience.

The practical consequence is not that measurement fails. It is that a measurement succeeds only at measuring its declared object. A passive test can be informative about passive behavior. A force-platform measure can be informative about the selected task. A report can describe an experience and its effect on participation. The error begins when one domain is silently promoted into the others.

Nor are the three domains independent in the ordinary sense. A changed passive relation can alter afferent information and the active response required by a task. Pain or uncertainty can alter attention, stiffness, excursion, and participation. A changed active strategy can alter joint contact and the demand placed on passive restraints. These are candidate interactions, not reasons to merge the variables. Keeping them separate is what allows an interaction to be measured: the account can ask whether a passive change preceded a task change, whether the task change corresponded with the person's experience, and whether any of those relations persisted after recovery.

The noncorresponding groups are therefore not inconvenient exceptions to a single hidden state. They are tests of the model's resolution. Copers with selected passive findings show that the finding is not sufficient for the selected poor outcome. People who report instability without a detected kinematic difference show that experience cannot be replaced by that motion model. A neural difference without a laxity difference shows that one null does not close every other domain. At the same time, none of these comparisons proves that the missing relation is absent under every task or measure. A null stops the arrow it tested; it does not erase the rest of the body.

This distinction also protects ordinary variability. A relation can vary from repetition to repetition while remaining within a task-sufficient range. A person can use more than one successful strategy. Variability becomes a candidate control problem only when the task, tolerance, direction, phase, and consequence are declared. Conversely, a repeatable pattern is not automatically capable merely because it is consistent. It can preserve one outcome while concentrating another exposure, using more effort, or reducing options. Those further propositions need their own measures.

## One task unfolds on several clocks

Task-level stability is a relation through time. A landing has events: approach, pre-contact preparation, initial contact, force development, joint motion, correction, and recovery. The useful question is not simply whether an angle or force was large. It is when the quantity changed, relative to which event, in what direction, for how long, and with what consequence for the declared task.

Different instruments observe different parts of that sequence. Motion capture estimates segment and joint kinematics. A force plate measures external contact. An inverse-dynamics calculation estimates net joint forces and moments under its assumptions. Surface electromyography records an electrical signal associated with selected superficial muscle activity; its onset is not muscle force or joint stability. A passive arthrometer imposes a different boundary altogether. A person report supplies experience and meaning over its stated timeframe. Aligning these records on one clock can test relations among them, but temporal order among signals does not by itself establish causal order.

A study of thirty-two people with chronic ankle instability during a single-leg drop landing illustrates why the whole trajectory can matter. Principal-component scores summarizing aspects of pre- and post-contact ankle-angle waveforms correlated with perceived-instability scores, whereas peak-angle summaries did not identify the same relations.[^25-09] The finding supports a richer time-resolved description under that task. A principal component is a mathematical summary of covariance in the sampled data, not an anatomical mode, a hidden force line, or a causal mechanism. The cross-sectional association does not show that changing the waveform would change future giving way.

This distinction also separates coordination from strength. Maximum voluntary force may matter, but it does not tell us whether force developed at the relevant phase, whether the contact was detected, whether a correction was appropriately scaled, or whether the relation recovered before the next demand. Conversely, a timing difference is not automatically discoordination. It becomes consequential only if the event definition, signal, repeatability, task effect, and alternative explanations support that interpretation.

The framework therefore asks for a task-specific relation rather than a visible shape. Stability does not mean minimal motion. A capable system can permit excursion, absorb variation, and recover. A stiff strategy can preserve one relation while increasing effort or changing another exposure; it can also be entirely appropriate. The task, tolerance, receiver, and timescale decide what must be measured.

Perturbation and recovery make the relational definition especially clear. A brief departure can be compatible with excellent control if the task permits it and the system returns without an adverse receiver exposure. A smaller departure can be consequential if it occurs at the wrong phase, removes a needed option, or persists into the next contact. The magnitude of one angle therefore cannot define stability without reference to timing and task. The same reasoning applies to muscle activity: earlier, later, larger, or smaller activation has no fixed value apart from the mechanical job it performs and the state in which it occurs.

Time-resolved evidence also resists a common retrospective shortcut. If a waveform differs years after an injury, the difference does not reveal its preinjury form. It can be retained impairment, learned protection, successful adaptation, selection into continued activity, or a consequence of pain and confidence. Establishing origin requires an earlier record, a manipulation, or another design capable of ordering those possibilities. A detailed waveform is a richer description of the present task, not a recovered history.

## Repetition changes the next starting state

The first event matters because it can change the state that meets the next demand. A sprain may alter passive restraint, swelling, pain, range, confidence, activity, care, and task choice in different combinations. Training and ordinary recovery may restore or improve capacity. A brace, changed footwear, a handrail, a teammate, or a different role may alter the boundary conditions. Reduced participation may lower event count without restoring the earlier capacity; greater participation may raise event count despite successful recovery.

Prospective studies provide temporal anchors without supplying a continuous mechanism. One cohort recruited eighty-two people after a first-time lateral ankle sprain. Seventy-one completed the six-month follow-up, and seventy completed the twelve-month follow-up used in the final analysis; twenty-eight were classified with chronic ankle instability and forty-two as copers. At two weeks, inability to complete two selected jump-and-landing tasks predicted the twelve-month research classification with 83 percent sensitivity and 55 percent specificity, correctly classifying 67.6 percent of cases. At six months, selected reach-task variables and self-reported function produced stronger in-cohort classification performance.[^25-10]

The temporal order is real: early and intermediate observations preceded a later classification. The mechanism remains incomplete. An inability to complete a task at two weeks can reflect pain, swelling, caution, strength, confidence, instruction, morphology, care, or another state. The six-month model is closer to the twelve-month outcome and includes self-report related to that outcome. These fitted results are not an individual prognosis, a clinical screen, or a reason to reproduce the tasks. They show that the observable state changes over the recovery year and that early information is imperfect.

A second longitudinal study recruited seventy controls and thirty people with an index sprain, followed them monthly for twelve months, retained ninety-six participants, and recorded ten sprains. Its multivariable model considered prior sprain status, age, body dimensions, perceived instability, laxity, balance, and inversion-eversion power; only recent index sprain and younger age were independent predictors in the reported model.[^25-11] With only ten events and many candidate variables, the model is exploratory and vulnerable to instability. It also lacked a task-exposure denominator. Its value here is not a prediction percentage. It shows how a mechanically interesting finding can belong in a history without independently determining a later event.

Exposure belongs in the denominator of recurrence. Time at risk, task class, frequency, intensity, surface, perturbation opportunity, support, assistance, participation, and changed role determine which demands a person actually encounters. Event count without opportunity can confuse lower exposure with greater capacity, or greater exposure with worse control. The record must also distinguish a continuation of an earlier route from a genuinely new acute event.

The coper literature keeps the recovery branch visible. In a cross-sectional comparison of twenty-five chronic-instability participants, twenty-five copers, and twenty-five healthy controls, the chronic-instability group showed more center-of-pressure sway and longer cognitive-task reaction times under the tested single-leg-standing conditions, while copers did not differ from healthy controls on the reported postural-control pattern.[^25-12] The authors' interpretation of successful compensation is plausible, but the study did not observe that strategy develop. A favorable coper outcome could reflect material recovery, a changed active strategy, learning, support, lower exposure, or several processes together. The evidence establishes capable performance in the selected comparison, not one optimal route.

The term *capacity* should therefore remain conditional. It means the current ability to tolerate and recover from a defined exposure, not a permanent scalar property of the ankle or person. A later demand can exceed capacity because the demand rose, capacity fell, recovery was incomplete, the environment changed, or the event was simply novel. The same later event can also occur after capacity improved if exposure rose further. Chronology prevents the outcome from being projected backward as proof of a declining system.

A complete recurrent account should be able to represent several histories without forcing them into one path. In a retained-restraint history, a passive change persists, corresponds with task difficulty, and precedes a later event. In a task-control history, the selected passive measures do not distinguish states, but event-aligned behavior does. In a perceptual or behavioral history, report and participation change while the selected passive and kinematic measures do not. In a successful-recovery history, relevant function returns under meaningful exposure. In an exposure-led history, the opportunity or circumstances for a new event explain more than a retained local state. In a mixed history, material behavior, pain, confidence, support, care, and environment change one another through time. Finally, in a no-added-value history, the routing reconstruction does not improve description, discrimination, or a useful research question for the endpoint under consideration.

These are not mutually exclusive diagnoses. They are competing and sometimes overlapping explanations of particular links. A person can have a well-supported local material consequence from the first injury, a successful task strategy under one demand, and a later event better explained by a new perturbation. The mechanical contribution can be substantial at one link and low priority at another. No universal ranking is required before the evidence is examined.

Recovery must be treated with the same care as recurrence. Resolution of pain does not establish restoration of the earlier passive or active state. A persistent finding does not establish continuing dysfunction. Return to participation can reflect regained capacity, altered strategy, support, tolerance of symptoms, or a changed role. Avoidance can reduce event opportunity while narrowing participation. To distinguish these outcomes, the account needs both a state measure and an exposure record, not an event count alone.

The exposure record need not pretend to be a single exact dose. It can state the task class, time at risk, frequency, duration, intensity, variability, surface, support, assistance, and perturbation opportunities at the resolution actually available. Unknowns should remain unknown. A coarse but honest denominator is more informative than a precise recurrence rate attached to an unmeasured opportunity structure.

## Protection comes before cost

After injury or uncertainty, a person may reduce excursion, place the foot differently, stiffen through active co-contraction, shift cadence, use an external support, avoid a task, or recruit other joints differently. The first question is what the change preserves: contact, balance, tissue tolerance, confidence, participation, or completion of the task. Only then should the account ask what else changed and whether any cost was measured.

A protective strategy can be useful and remain useful. It can be transient, become unnecessary, consume more effort, reduce available options, or redistribute demand. It can also be merely different. Calling every difference *compensation* can make harm look inevitable, while calling every successful task *recovery* can hide altered exposure or effort. The role, scale, task, and time have to be declared.

Several landing studies motivate a proximal question. In repeated jump landings, nineteen participants with unilateral chronic ankle instability showed less knee flexion at initial contact and reduced anteroposterior dynamic-stability performance on the involved side compared with nineteen controls.[^25-13] In a separate one-year comparison after first-time sprain, twenty-eight people classified with chronic ankle instability and forty-two copers differed in selected hip kinematics and energetics during a single-leg drop landing.[^25-14] These are positive proximal observations: group membership defined partly by ankle history corresponded with motion or calculated energetic differences at another joint during the same task.

They are not yet a kinetic-chain account. The studies do not establish that ankle state caused the proximal pattern, that the pattern increased internal tissue exposure, or that it led to later hip or knee injury. A changed angle is not a changed tissue load by implication.

A stop-jump study supplies the required control. Nineteen participants with chronic ankle instability had less knee flexion at the instant of peak calculated anterior tibial shear force than nineteen controls, but the groups did not differ in that peak calculated force or the other selected biomechanical variables.[^25-15] The force was calculated under model assumptions rather than measured in a tissue, and the study did not measure anterior-cruciate-ligament strain or later injury. Even so, the positive kinematic finding cannot be promoted into an observed proximal-load difference when the selected load comparison was null.

This scale discipline preserves a substantial local mechanical account. A documented injury can change passive behavior. A task can expose a relation that a static test does not. Repeated correction can change activation, motion, contact, work, or effort. External support can change the boundary. None of those observations must establish a proximal injury, systemic cost, or preferred whole-body organization in order to matter.

The hard-and-soft distinction helps organize this protection without deciding its consequence in advance. If passive geometry no longer preserves a relation under one direction of demand, active muscle and tendon can take more shape-preserving work. A shoe, brace, floor, hand support, or altered task can also share that work. Meanwhile, tissues that receive form continue to deform, slide, contain, and accommodate. The allocation can be effective, ineffective, costly, or neutral depending on the task and timescale. It cannot be inferred from a tissue name or from the mere presence of co-contraction.

A delayed cost claim requires an additional chain. First identify the protected function. Then measure the changed force, moment, pressure, contact, motion, work, activation, or exposure. Identify the receiver and establish that the changed quantity reaches it. Only then ask whether the receiver's response, capacity, recovery, or later outcome differs. Skipping from altered strategy to later symptom leaves the consequential middle unmeasured. The same discipline applies to energy: more electrical muscle activity is not automatically more mechanical work, metabolic cost, fatigue, or harm.

Protection can also improve the future state. Practice may make a correction faster or less costly. Conditioning can expand the range of tolerated demand. External support can change exposure long enough for another state to recover. A different participation choice can be a valued adaptation rather than evidence of failure. The framework loses explanatory value if every persistent difference is coded as compensation and every compensation as deterioration.

The adverse-pattern vocabulary remains useful if it does not become a stack of diagnoses. *Instability* identifies a failure to maintain or recover a declared relation. *Discoordination* identifies a timing or sequencing problem with a demonstrated task consequence. *Concentration* concerns a local distribution, not the mere presence of high force. *Interruption* and *substitution* concern how a named demand is carried. *Compensation* concerns a task-preserving change. Several patterns can coexist, but each must add a variable or test. If the same finding is merely relabeled six times, the framework has added no information.

## The axial transfer: lumbar motion and control

The ankle evidence cannot be transplanted into the spine. Ankle ligaments, inversion tests, landing metrics, giving-way definitions, and cohort thresholds are region-specific. What transfers is a set of questions: Which relation is supposed to be maintained? Under what task, direction, phase, and tolerance? What passive behavior was measured? What active motion or recovery was measured? What did the person report? Which endpoint is being explained? Which arrows are observed, calculated, reconstructed, or still hypothetical?

Lumbar research makes the need for those separations clear. In an observational study of 1,090 outpatients with low-back or leg pain, flexion-extension radiographs measured L4–L5 sagittal translation and angulation. Translation of at least three millimeters was associated with worse activity-limitation scores under the study's definitions, while angulation alone did not show the same relation; the combination corresponded with the lowest scores.[^25-16] The finding belongs to a symptomatic clinical sample and a selected radiographic method. It does not make the threshold universal or establish that translation caused symptoms.

A study of forty-nine people referred for flexion-extension imaging because lumbar instability was suspected evaluated clinical findings against radiographic criteria.[^25-17] That design can show correspondence with its selected comparator. The radiograph is not a ground truth for task-level relation maintenance, pain origin, or the person's experience. A result can therefore be accurate relative to a radiographic category while leaving the central functional question unresolved.

An experiment in healthy participants addresses active recovery more directly. Participants responded to unexpected support-surface perturbations with and without a rigid lumbar corset. Restricting lumbar motion increased center-of-pressure excursion, recovery time, and the number of adjustments, especially under larger perturbations.[^25-18] Under those experimental conditions, changing an available segment motion changed selected recovery measures. The result does not establish chronic pathology, a diagnosis, a treatment direction, or an ideal amount of lumbar movement. It also shows why stability cannot be defined as the least possible motion: available movement contributed to recovery.

The field does not supply one accepted construct that merges these objects. A systematic review of 118 studies found substantial variation in radiological definitions of sagittal-plane segmental instability in the degenerative lumbar spine.[^25-19] A 2026 systematic review of thirty-two studies likewise found varied clinical, radiological, and questionnaire definitions and no single criterion that completed the construct.[^25-20] Reviews can map heterogeneity; they cannot create a unified diagnostic category for this chapter.

The bounded axial result is therefore relational. Passive segment motion, active recovery under perturbation, radiographic category, pain, disability, and reported instability can correspond, but no one is the others in disguise. Imposed restriction can causally change a selected task without showing that a naturally occurring symptom history arose through the same mechanism. The ankle chapter contributes a state-transition grammar, not ankle certainty.

That grammar changes the spinal question in a useful way. Instead of asking whether a spine *is unstable*, the account asks what relation departs from what tolerance during which task; what passive, active, and experiential states were measured; what happened before and after the departure; and whether available motion, rather than its elimination, contributes to recovery. A radiographic relation can be important without identifying pain origin. A symptom can be important without revealing passive motion. A task experiment can identify one causal boundary condition without becoming a chronic-disease model.

The transfer also preserves a null outcome. If passive findings, active recovery, symptoms, and exposure do not correspond under appropriate measures, the ankle analogy should not be used to invent a hidden axial instability. The account can remain unresolved or assign lower priority to the proposed route. That stopping rule protects the broader spinal research program by making its future claims answerable rather than inevitable.

## A state-transition account of recurrence

Recurrent instability is not looseness repeated. It is a later demand meeting a system whose passive restraint, pain, swelling, sensory information, timing, active strategy, confidence, support, participation, recovery, and exposure may have changed since the earlier event. Some of those variables may correspond closely. Others may diverge. The history can end in persistent difficulty, capable coping, useful protection, greater capacity, a new acute event, or an unresolved state.

The multistudy ankle reconstruction supports several local links and stops at others. Passive inversion can differ from controls without distinguishing copers from chronic-instability participants. A selected reflex measure can differ while passive laxity does not. Reports can distinguish groups when selected static balance, talar position, or whole-curve landing kinematics do not. Time-resolved motion can carry information that a peak misses. Early and intermediate observations can precede a later research classification without revealing one mechanism or predicting an individual. Proximal motion can differ while a selected calculated proximal load does not.

Those are not failures of the mechanical lens. They are the lens working at the resolution the evidence permits. It separates a local mechanical contribution from a whole history, requires exposure opportunity before interpreting recurrence, treats protection as protection before asking about cost, and lets a proposed route stop when its carrier or receiver is missing.

The spinal transfer reaches the same conclusion by a different path. Passive motion, active task recovery, radiographic relation, and symptoms have to remain distinct. A bounded experiment can establish that restricting available motion changes recovery under one perturbation, while leaving chronic origin, diagnosis, and care entirely open.

The chapter therefore adds time to the book's account of routing. A task does not act on an abstract ankle, joint, or spine. It acts on a present state with a history. The next chapter keeps that temporal insight but removes the convenience of a clear first event. In a mixed chronic presentation, the harder question is whether a plausible history of compensation and changing capacity adds explanatory value—or merely completes a story that the observations cannot support.

Gribble et al., “Selection Criteria for Patients with Chronic Ankle Instability in Controlled Research: A Position Statement of the International Ankle Consortium,” *Journal of Athletic Training* 49, no. 1 (2014): 121–127, [doi:10.4085/1062-6050-49.1.14](https://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-49.1.14). The statement standardizes research selection and reporting; it is not a diagnostic rule, causal mechanism, or individual prognosis.

[^25-02]: Jay Hertel and Revay O. Corbett, “An Updated Model of Chronic Ankle Instability,” *Journal of Athletic Training* 54, no. 6 (2019): 572–588, [doi:10.4085/1062-6050-344-18](https://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-344-18). The authors describe an explicitly theoretical model spanning injury, impairments, context, interactions, and outcomes. The model does not establish that every component or arrow applies to every person.

[^25-03]: Nili Steinberg et al., “Chronic Ankle Instability and Neuromuscular Performance in Prerecruitment Infantry Soldiers,” *Journal of Athletic Training* 59, no. 1 (2024): 73–80, [doi:10.4085/1062-6050-0564.22](https://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-0564.22). This cross-sectional study assessed 364 male soldiers aged eighteen to twenty-one and separated combinations of perceived, mechanically examined, and recurrent-sprain states. It does not establish chronology or general population prevalence.

[^25-04]: Cathleen N. Brown, Adam B. Rosen, and Jupil Ko, “Ankle Ligament Laxity and Stiffness in Chronic Ankle Instability,” *Foot & Ankle International* 36, no. 5 (2015): 565–572, [doi:10.1177/1071100714561057](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100714561057). Sixteen chronic-instability participants, sixteen copers, and sixteen controls completed instrumented inversion stress testing. The positive inversion comparison and the coper and stiffness nulls are specific to that test and sample.

[^25-05]: Samantha Bowker et al., “Neural Excitability and Joint Laxity in Chronic Ankle Instability, Coper, and Control Groups,” *Journal of Athletic Training* 51, no. 4 (2016): 336–343, [doi:10.4085/1062-6050-51.5.05](https://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-51.5.05). The case-control comparison included thirty-seven chronic-instability participants, thirty copers, and twenty-six controls. A soleus H-reflex result does not measure complete proprioception, historical cause, or future recurrence.

[^25-06]: Erik A. Wikstrom et al., “Discriminating between Copers and People with Chronic Ankle Instability,” *Journal of Athletic Training* 47, no. 2 (2012): 136–142, [doi:10.4085/1062-6050-47.2.136](https://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-47.2.136). Twenty-four copers and twenty-four chronic-instability participants completed report, radiographic talar-position, and hop-stabilization measures. Case-control discrimination and fitted cutoffs are not prospective clinical prediction.

[^25-07]: J. S. de Vries et al., “Difference in Balance Measures between Patients with Chronic Ankle Instability and Patients after an Acute Ankle Inversion Trauma,” *Knee Surgery, Sports Traumatology, Arthroscopy* 18, no. 5 (2010): 601–606, [doi:10.1007/s00167-010-1097-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00167-010-1097-1). The study compared fifteen healthy participants, fourteen participants after an acute inversion injury, and twenty-three participants with chronic ankle instability. Its null is limited to the selected static-balance measures and sample.

[^25-08]: Roel De Ridder et al., “Lower Limb Landing Biomechanics in Subjects with Chronic Ankle Instability,” *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* 47, no. 6 (2015): 1225–1231, [doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000000525](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000525). Twenty-eight self-reported chronic-instability participants and twenty-eight matched controls completed barefoot forward and side jump landings. No detected whole-curve kinematic difference is not proof of equivalence or absence of unmeasured internal differences.

[^25-09]: Kentaro Watanabe et al., “The Relationship between Joint Kinematic Patterns during Single-Leg Drop Landing and Perceived Instability in Individuals with Chronic Ankle Instability,” *Clinical Biomechanics* 114 (2024): 106237, [doi:10.1016/j.clinbiomech.2024.106237](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinbiomech.2024.106237). Thirty-two participants completed the landing study. Principal-component waveform associations are not anatomical modes, causal mechanisms, intervention targets, or recurrence predictions.

[^25-10]: Cailbhe Doherty et al., “Recovery from a First-Time Lateral Ankle Sprain and the Predictors of Chronic Ankle Instability: A Prospective Cohort Analysis,” *American Journal of Sports Medicine* 44, no. 4 (2016): 995–1003, [doi:10.1177/0363546516628870](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546516628870). Eighty-two first-sprain participants were recruited; seventy entered the final twelve-month analysis. The in-cohort models establish time order for selected observations, not a continuous mechanism, reader screen, or individual prognosis.

[^25-11]: Fereshteh Pourkazemi et al., “Predictors of Recurrent Sprains after an Index Lateral Ankle Sprain: A Longitudinal Study,” *Physiotherapy* 104, no. 4 (2018): 430–437, [doi:10.1016/j.physio.2017.10.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physio.2017.10.004). The study recruited one hundred participants, retained ninety-six for twelve months, and recorded ten sprains. The low event count and absent task-exposure denominator limit model stability and practical inference.

[^25-12]: Somayeh Mohamadi et al., “Attentional Demands of Postural Control in Chronic Ankle Instability, Copers and Healthy Controls: A Controlled Cross-Sectional Study,” *Gait & Posture* 79 (2020): 183–188, [doi:10.1016/j.gaitpost.2020.03.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gaitpost.2020.03.007). Seventy-five participants were divided equally among chronic-instability, coper, and healthy-control groups. The cross-sectional study did not observe a compensatory strategy developing through time.

[^25-13]: Phillip A. Gribble and Richard H. Robinson, “Alterations in Knee Kinematics and Dynamic Stability Associated with Chronic Ankle Instability,” *Journal of Athletic Training* 44, no. 4 (2009): 350–355, [doi:10.4085/1062-6050-44.4.350](https://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-44.4.350). Nineteen chronic-instability participants and nineteen controls completed repeated jump landings. The study did not measure proximal tissue exposure, historical cause, or future injury.

[^25-14]: Cailbhe Doherty et al., “Single-Leg Drop Landing Movement Strategies in Participants with Chronic Ankle Instability Compared with Lateral Ankle Sprain ‘Copers,’” *Knee Surgery, Sports Traumatology, Arthroscopy* 24, no. 4 (2016): 1049–1059, [doi:10.1007/s00167-015-3852-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00167-015-3852-9). Twenty-eight chronic-instability participants and forty-two copers were evaluated one year after a first-time sprain. The comparison does not establish harmful compensation, causal direction, or future proximal injury.

[^25-15]: Masafumi Terada, Brian Pietrosimone, and Phillip A. Gribble, “Individuals with Chronic Ankle Instability Exhibit Altered Landing Knee Kinematics: Potential Link with the Mechanism of Loading for the Anterior Cruciate Ligament,” *Clinical Biomechanics* 29, no. 10 (2014): 1125–1130, [doi:10.1016/j.clinbiomech.2014.09.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinbiomech.2014.09.014). Nineteen chronic-instability participants and nineteen controls completed a vertical stop jump. Calculated anterior tibial shear is not measured ligament strain or future injury risk.

[^25-16]: Tetsuhiro Iguchi et al., “Lumbar Instability and Clinical Symptoms: Which Is the More Critical Factor for Symptoms: Sagittal Translation or Segment Angulation?,” *Journal of Spinal Disorders & Techniques* 17, no. 4 (2004): 284–290, [doi:10.1097/01.bsd.0000102473.95064.9d](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.bsd.0000102473.95064.9d). The observational flexion-extension study involved 1,090 symptomatic outpatients. Its thresholds and associations are study-specific and do not establish causal origin.

[^25-17]: Julie M. Fritz, Sara R. Piva, and John D. Childs, “Accuracy of the Clinical Examination to Predict Radiographic Instability of the Lumbar Spine,” *European Spine Journal* 14, no. 8 (2005): 743–750, [doi:10.1007/s00586-004-0803-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00586-004-0803-4). Forty-nine symptomatic referred patients underwent clinical and radiographic examination. The radiographic criteria were not a direct ground truth for task-level control, pain origin, or treatment response.

[^25-18]: Nicola W. Mok and Paul W. Hodges, “Movement of the Lumbar Spine Is Critical for Maintenance of Postural Recovery following Support Surface Perturbation,” *Experimental Brain Research* 231, no. 3 (2013): 305–313, [doi:10.1007/s00221-013-3692-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-013-3692-0). Healthy participants completed unexpected perturbations with and without a rigid lumbar corset. The acute imposed constraint does not establish chronic pathology, diagnosis, preferred alignment, or treatment.

[^25-19]: Signe F. Elmose et al., “Radiological Definitions of Sagittal Plane Segmental Instability in the Degenerative Lumbar Spine: A Systematic Review,” *Global Spine Journal* 13, no. 2 (2023): 523–533, [doi:10.1177/21925682221099854](https://doi.org/10.1177/21925682221099854). The review included 118 studies, many in surgical degenerative populations. It maps radiographic-definition heterogeneity rather than defining functional instability or symptom origin.

[^25-20]: Nikita Gonugunta et al., “Definitions and Diagnostic Criteria of Lumbar Spine Instability: A Systematic Review,” *The Spine Journal* (online ahead of print, June 19, 2026), [doi:10.1016/j.spinee.2026.06.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.spinee.2026.06.002). The review included thirty-two studies using varied clinical, radiological, and questionnaire criteria. It does not validate a combined diagnostic framework for this book.
