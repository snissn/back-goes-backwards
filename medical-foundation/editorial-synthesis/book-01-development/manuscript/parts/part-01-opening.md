# Part I — Mechanical foundations of living structure

Calling the body mechanical can sound either trivial or reductive. It is trivial if the word means only that bodies have mass and occupy space. It is reductive if a living person is treated as a passive machine whose behavior follows from a few levers and material constants. Part I develops the useful position between those extremes.

Living structures carry force, sustain pressure, deform, move, maintain contact, and recover. They also activate, sense, regulate, grow, remodel, fatigue, heal, and change their behavior with context. The mechanical description is therefore indispensable but incomplete. Its job is to make material relationships inspectable without pretending that those relationships exhaust biology or experience.

The four chapters move from broad fact to working model. Chapter 1 establishes mechanics as one dimension of embodied life and places it inside a whole-person account. Chapter 2 opens the word *load* into the quantities and timescales it can conceal. Chapter 3 introduces hard and soft as changing mechanical roles: comparative shape preservation and form reception rather than permanent tissue classes. Chapter 4 places those roles inside three-dimensional configuration and develops a bounded meaning of load path.

One ordinary object transfer accompanies the progression. At first it shows only that material demand must be managed. It then acquires forces, moments, contacts, boundaries, rates, and phases. Different structures take on different jobs, and the available paths change with geometry, activation, support, and time. The example prescribes no technique. It teaches a method: declare the system before explaining what happened inside it.

By the end of Part I, a mechanical question should be more precise and less certain than it first appeared. That is progress. The reader will be able to distinguish external demand from internal distribution, force from stress, deformation from damage, visible posture from configuration, and a candidate path from a proven route. Part II can then ask which anatomical systems perform the work those distinctions describe.
