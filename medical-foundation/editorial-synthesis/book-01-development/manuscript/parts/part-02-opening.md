# Part II — The architecture of support

A body does not support itself through one material or one kind of force. Bones preserve useful geometry while deforming. Joints redirect demand while moving. Muscle acts through changing length, velocity, leverage, and activation. Tendons and ligaments transmit, store, guide, and restrain. Cartilage receives contact through a fluid–solid structure. Fasciae and sliding interfaces connect, separate, contain, and permit relative movement. Cavities and pressure can contribute to support. Sensation and control continually alter the distribution.

Part II turns Part I's abstract roles into this cooperative anatomy. Chapter 5 begins with bones and joints because their geometry provides the clearest shape-preserving routes. It also shows why the skeleton cannot be understood as a rigid frame acting alone. Chapter 6 adds the active, tensile, compliant, gliding, pressure-bearing, and distributed systems that make skeletal paths usable and adaptable. It tests broad network and tensegrity representations without asking a model to prove a medical effect.

Chapters 7–9 then take up the posterior body. Chapter 7 maps what the phrase *posterior chain* can legitimately include and where continuity remains anatomical, inferred, variable, or unmeasured. Chapter 8 follows posterior participation through ordinary functions such as walking, lifting, propulsion, braking, balance, and recovery. Chapter 9 returns those functions to whole-body variation across development, training, pregnancy, pain, illness, disability, assistance, and aging.

The sequence matters. Posterior anatomy appears only after the reader has seen that support is shared. This prevents a large and consequential system from becoming either invisible or total. The questions are affirmative but bounded: What posterior structures exist? What do they contribute in a declared task? With what other systems do they cooperate? What changes with phase, capacity, and context? The stronger claim that posterior participation belongs to a preferred body-wide organization remains a later hypothesis rather than an answer built into the anatomy.
