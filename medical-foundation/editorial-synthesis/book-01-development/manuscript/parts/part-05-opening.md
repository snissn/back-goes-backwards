# Part V — Worked mechanical narratives

A framework can remain persuasive as long as its examples are short. The difficult test is a complete narrative, where a visible finding does not identify an internal field, chronology is incomplete, several structures share demand, symptoms fluctuate, and an alternative explanation can enter at every arrow.

Part V uses four narratives to make those difficulties visible. They are not templates for self-assessment or disguised treatment cases. Each declares what kind of example it is, limits its system and time, identifies measured and inferred quantities, and states what would strengthen, weaken, or leave unresolved the proposed account.

Chapter 23 follows a geometric relation toward localized strain. It compares spine and limb-joint contexts so that one anatomy does not become a universal model. Chapter 24 examines the thoracic outlet as a constraint problem. The central mechanism is not the mere appearance of less space, but the contact, pressure, tension, excursion, wall behavior, or other load relation that maintains deformation in a compliant structure. Chapter 25 studies instability and repeated demand while capacity changes. Chapter 26 ends with a hypothetical mixed chronic presentation in which compensation may preserve function, a mechanical history may contribute, and no single story can legitimately absorb sleep, work, symptoms, behavior, biology, and time.

Every chapter follows the same basic chain: observation; task and environment; configuration and available paths; external demand and internal distribution; local exposure; tissue or system response; finding, symptom, or function; alternatives; and discriminating observations. The repeated structure permits comparison. The outcomes should not converge merely because the same vocabulary is used.

A strong narrative can end with support for one bounded arrow, a mixed account, a negative result, or an unresolved inference. What it cannot do is jump from posture to diagnosis, from symptom modulation to origin, from plausible mechanics to treatment, or from one favorable case to the preferred-configuration hypothesis.
