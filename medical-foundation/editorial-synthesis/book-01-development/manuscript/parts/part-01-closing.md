## Part I synthesis — From embodiment to paths

Part I began with a fact: life takes place through material structures under demand. It ends with a disciplined way to describe that fact.

The word *load* now separates into forces, moments, pressures, stresses, strains, deformations, rates, repetitions, work, power, and exposure over time. Each quantity belongs to a declared system, scale, frame, reference, task, and phase. None can be inferred in full from appearance alone. A whole-body motion, a joint resultant, a tissue field, and a cellular response can describe the same event at different levels without becoming interchangeable.

The hard/soft polarity adds a relational question. Which structures or relations comparatively preserve shape, transmit demand, constrain motion, or resolve gross geometry? Which must remain able to receive form, yield, glide, contain, expand, or change local shape? A structure can answer both questions in different directions or phases. The vocabulary is useful only when it sharpens an observable division of labor rather than recoloring anatomy after the result is known.

Configuration then gathers geometry, contact, activation, pressure, material state, and available motion into a changing whole. A load path is one model of how a defined demand is partitioned within that configuration. The path can branch, recruit, disappear, reverse, or remain indeterminate. Similar external success can conceal different internal solutions.

These distinctions do not yet tell us what the body's structures actually do. They establish the questions anatomy must answer. Part II begins with the skeleton and joints, then restores active, tensile, compliant, sliding, pressure-bearing, and control systems around them. Only after that cooperation is visible can the posterior chain be examined as a real anatomical and functional system rather than assumed as a line on a diagram.
