## Editorial decision

Chapter 19 has a strong affirmative center. It does not need to retreat to the weak proposition that “many factors matter,” and it must not advance the opposite simplification that repetition becomes degeneration. The supportable bridge is:

> Once a local mechanical exposure has been defined, a living tissue can detect and respond to it. The response depends on the receiver, the exposure's magnitude, mode, direction, rate, spatial distribution, sequence, repetition, duration, rest, and prior history, and the tissue's current structural and biological state. Bone, tendon, and cartilage demonstrate maintenance, adaptation, transient response, recovery, remodeling, reduced tolerance, and injury branches under different conditions. The resulting biological change can alter material behavior and therefore the mechanics of later tasks. None of those links makes a tissue response, structural finding, symptom, diagnosis, or outcome interchangeable with the next.

The chapter's principal contribution is this reciprocal, tissue-specific bridge:

```text
declared task and environment
    -> internal distribution
    -> named local exposure and history
    -> receiver state
    -> measured tissue response / no detected response
    -> changed or unchanged later material behavior and capacity
    -> later distribution and exposure

separate downstream obligations:
    response -> finding -> function or symptom -> diagnosis -> outcome
```

This is an affirmative mechanobiology argument, not a disclaimer. It establishes that mechanics is biologically consequential within measured scopes and that a material receiver is not passive. It also establishes why “load” cannot be one body-wide scalar. What it does not establish is that a reconstructed whole-body route produced the local exposure, that a detected response is adverse, or that an adverse response explains a person's symptoms or diagnosis.

## Exact causal grammar and stopping rules

The evidence packet should govern each future paragraph with one principal arrow. The minimum nodes are:

```text
task or environment
    -> regional force, moment, contact, or motion
    -> local tissue stress, strain, pressure, fluid movement, or deformation
    -> mechanosensing or material perturbation
    -> cellular, matrix, vascular, inflammatory, remodeling, repair, or failure response
    -> structural or functional finding
    -> function or symptom
    -> diagnosis
    -> outcome
```

Every arrow requires its own evidence state. A source may support several arrows only if it measured each of them. The following substitutions are prohibited:

- body weight, ground reaction force, joint moment, or exercise label **for** local tissue exposure;
- one instant of stress or strain **for** cumulative tissue dose;
- mechanotransduction **for** an adverse response;
- biosynthetic or signaling change **for** adaptation, damage, or disease;
- material change **for** a symptomatic finding;
- imaging finding **for** symptom or diagnosis;
- association **for** chronology or causal priority; and
- a receiver-specific result **for** a general rule about all living tissue.

Where a source stops, the chapter stops. If a cartilage explant experiment measures proteoglycan synthesis but not pain, it belongs at response. If a human MRI study measures cartilage deformation but not cell behavior, it belongs at exposure or material state. If a tendon intervention measures stiffness but not symptoms, it belongs at changed material behavior. No rhetorical bridge may join the missing nodes.

## One task, three receiver-specific questions

The opening comparison should be explicitly synthetic. It may use a controlled lower-limb task or class of tasks to ask what reaches bone, tendon, and cartilage, but it must not imply that one published experiment measured all three tissues simultaneously.

### Bone: site-, mode-, and person-specific strain

Milgrom and colleagues implanted two strain-gauged staples along the medial tibia in four physically active adult men and measured axial strain at the mid-diaphysis and distal third during walking, running, drop jumps, and serial jumps. Tensile and compressive strain differed by activity, site, and participant.[^milgrom2022] The study is unusually direct but very small, samples only two surface locations and one axial gauge orientation at each, and cannot reconstruct a complete three-dimensional strain field.

**Permitted use:** an external task label and ground reaction do not specify even one bone's local exposure. Location, direction, sign, rate, and individual solution matter.

**Not permitted:** ranking the tested activities by universal bone benefit or harm, extending surface axial strain to all tibial material, or treating the sample as population norms.

### Tendon: longitudinal loading produces heterogeneous three-dimensional deformation

Obst and colleagues used freehand three-dimensional ultrasound in eight participants at rest and during a 70% maximal voluntary isometric plantar-flexion contraction. The free Achilles tendon lengthened while its cross-sectional dimensions changed differently along two transverse axes; transverse strain and rotation varied regionally and peaked in the tendon midportion.[^obst2014] This is a local-exposure study, not a tissue-response or injury study.

**Permitted use:** tendon exposure cannot be reduced to one longitudinal force or mean strain. Three-dimensional geometry and regional deformation can matter even in a controlled isometric task.

**Not permitted:** inferring injury risk, adaptation, or a preferred rotational direction from the observed deformation.

### Cartilage: contact produces intratissue strain rather than one compressive scalar

Chan and colleagues synchronized MRI with cyclic loading equivalent to one-half body weight at the foot in healthy volunteers. Tibiofemoral cartilage showed rigid-body sliding plus complex regional and subject-specific intratissue strains, with dominant strains approaching 12% in shear under the tested geometry.[^chan2016] Eckstein and colleagues separately found 2.4–8.6% patellar-cartilage volume deformation after 50 knee bends in seven volunteers, no further group-level deformation after 100 bends, and recovery taking longer than 90 minutes after 100 bends.[^eckstein1999]

**Permitted use:** contact, fluid redistribution, shear, depth, time, and recovery enter cartilage exposure. A gross compression label is insufficient.

**Not permitted:** calling ordinary measured deformation damage, treating one-half body weight as the matrix stress, or generalizing seven-person recovery data to every joint and tissue state.

## Dose and recovery are not scalar modifiers

### Bone: changing rest can change response while magnitude does not

Srinivasan and colleagues compared low-magnitude loading with and without ten-second rests between cycles in avian ulna and murine tibia models. Rest insertion markedly increased periosteal labeling or bone-formation rate even though waveform magnitude was held constant within the relevant comparisons; in the mouse experiment the rest-inserted regimen used fewer cycles.[^srinivasan2002] Endocortical measures did not change under the tested regimens.

This is an especially clean demonstration that dose includes sequence and rest, and that response is site-specific. It does not prescribe a human exercise interval or prove that more recovery is always better.

### Tendon: acute synthesis, long-term material change, and recovery occupy different clocks

Miller and colleagues studied healthy young men for up to 72 hours after one hour of one-legged kicking at 67% of maximum workload. Patellar-tendon collagen fractional synthesis increased after exercise, peaked at approximately 24 hours, and remained elevated at the last sampled time, while histology did not show muscle damage under the tested task.[^miller2005] Tendon injury was not a measured outcome. The experiment supports a tissue response to exercise and a time course; it did not directly measure local tendon strain during the task and did not establish net matrix accumulation, improved function, or a clinical effect.

Arampatzis and colleagues then supply a longer material-response example. Eleven young men trained each leg for 14 weeks at different estimated Achilles-tendon strain magnitudes with otherwise similar frequency and exercise-volume targets. Stiffness and elastic modulus increased only in the higher-strain leg; tendon cross-sectional area did not significantly change in either leg. Comparison with an earlier, slower-frequency protocol suggested that strain duration also affected the response.[^arampatzis2010] The study is small and sex- and age-specific, and strain was estimated rather than measured throughout the tendon.

Nuri and colleagues later reported in a randomized crossover experiment that Achilles-tendon creep recovery after a conditioning protocol was complete within approximately two hours under the tested conditions.[^nuri2018] This time constant concerns mechanical deformation recovery, not collagen turnover or readiness for another person or task.

Together these studies prevent three collapses:

```text
acute synthesis != net structural adaptation
mechanical recovery != biological repair
elapsed rest != restored capacity
```

### Cartilage: rate, frequency, waveform, fluid movement, and recovery change the question

Sah and colleagues exposed calf cartilage explants to dynamic compression across amplitudes, waveforms, and frequencies and measured biosynthetic activity.[^sah1989] Kim and colleagues further examined small-amplitude dynamic compression across a broad frequency range and related the spatial response to candidate physical stimuli including fluid flow, hydrostatic pressure, streaming potential, and cell deformation.[^kim1994] These ex-vivo studies show that “compression” is not a complete biological input.

Kurz and colleagues applied single 50% compressions to cartilage explants at different strain rates. Under their model, the lowest tested rate produced no measured cellular or matrix effect despite substantial peak stress, whereas faster rates reduced biosynthesis, cell viability, compressive and shear stiffness, and the remaining cells' anabolic response to later low-amplitude stimulation.[^kurz2001] This is a controlled adverse branch. It is not a human joint threshold.

Eckstein's in-vivo deformation and recovery study adds the missing ordinary-use clock: repeated knee bends produced measurable fluid-associated deformation, a plateau within the tested repetitions, and a recovery period longer than the exercise itself.[^eckstein1999] No source in this set supports merging the explant injury thresholds with the in-vivo volunteer deformation values.

### Dose vocabulary for the draft

Every developed example should state, or explicitly mark unknown:

- receiver, region, and state;
- loading mode and direction;
- magnitude and spatial distribution;
- rate and waveform;
- repetition, duration, and sequence;
- variability and intervening rest;
- prior exposure and current capacity;
- recovery process and recovery measure;
- observation interval; and
- response measure.

The phrase *cumulative load* is acceptable only as shorthand after these dimensions have been opened. It must not imply that disparate loads add linearly or share a common unit.

## Mechanotransduction is a bridge, not a verdict

Jaalouk and Lammerding define mechanotransduction broadly as the translation of mechanical forces or deformations into biochemical signals, with feedback that can alter cellular and extracellular structure.[^jaalouk2009] For Chapter 19, the term should name a family of processes rather than a black-box arrow.

The minimum analytical sequence is:

```text
specified mechanical condition
    -> specified sensing or material event
    -> specified signaling, synthesis, degradation, proliferation, death, or remodeling measure
    -> specified structural or functional change, if measured
```

The sequence admits at least these branches:

1. ordinary maintenance;
2. useful adaptation under a declared outcome;
3. no detected material or biological change;
4. transient response followed by measured recovery;
5. fatigue or reduced tolerance;
6. protective or sensitizing response;
7. remodeling or repair;
8. injury or failure; and
9. a biological-first or common-cause state that modifies the mechanical response.

These are not stages of one universal progression. The same tissue can exhibit simultaneous synthesis and degradation, local injury and neighboring repair, or a measured molecular response without a detectable macroscopic change. *Adaptation* should mean a state change, not improvement. *Remodeling* should mean organized turnover or structural change, not degeneration. *Inflammatory response* should name measured mediators or cells, not a synonym for pain.

## Receiver file: bone

### Supported positive branch — formation and material response

The rest-insertion animal experiments establish that a low-magnitude stimulus can become osteogenic when its temporal pattern changes.[^srinivasan2002] A human unilateral intervention offers a distinct, bounded bridge. Twenty healthy postmenopausal women completed three months of daily one-legged jumps, using the other leg as a within-person control. The exercised tibia showed a change in impact-microindentation-based bone material strength index, while high-resolution peripheral quantitative CT did not show corresponding changes in the selected density or microarchitectural measures.[^sundh2018]

The human result should be described as a change in one material proxy under a small, nonrandomized leg-choice intervention, not proof of whole-bone strength, fracture prevention, or a generic “bone gets stronger under impact” rule.

### Supported fatigue/remodeling branch — local damage can recruit a targeted response

In a rat ulna fatigue-loading model, Kennedy and colleagues localized apoptotic osteocytes near microdamage and pro-osteoclastogenic signals in neighboring non-apoptotic osteocytes before osteoclast appearance.[^kennedy2012] A later experiment used a pan-caspase inhibitor and found that blocking osteocyte apoptosis also blocked the tested fatigue-associated RANKL and VEGF changes and OPG reduction, supporting a causal role for apoptosis in that signaling sequence under the animal model.[^kennedy2014]

This is a strong mechanistic example of a localized fatigue exposure becoming a spatially organized remodeling signal. It should not be called degeneration, generalized inflammation, pain, or disease.

Waldorff and colleagues add a recovery complication. After fatigue damage was induced in rat tibiae, weight-bearing or intermittent weight-bearing groups showed intracortical resorption and declining microdamage over time, whereas hindlimb suspension did not show the same repair response.[^waldorff2010] The study means that removal of ordinary mechanical input can alter repair in this model; it does not authorize a stress-fracture treatment rule.

### State and human alternative branch

The bone response is not mechanically isolated. In a population-based cross-sectional sample of 375 women and 325 men aged 21–97 years, Melton and colleagues found no close or consistent correspondence between age-related patterns in habitual-load proxies and estimated bone-strength indices.[^melton2006] The design cannot determine individual chronology, but it directly challenges reduced loading as a sufficient primary explanation for age-related bone loss.

A 25-week randomized trial in 98 women aged 75–85 with low bone mass found small, site-specific cortical-density changes after resistance or agility training, with no significant between-group differences in many other DXA or pQCT outcomes.[^liuambrose2004] That positive/null pattern is more useful to Chapter 19 than a generic “exercise builds bone” sentence.

## Receiver file: tendon

### Supported ordinary and adaptive branches

The in-vivo Achilles study establishes that a tendon deforms in three dimensions and nonuniformly under an ordinary contraction.[^obst2014] Miller's human biopsy study establishes an acute tendon collagen-synthesis response after strenuous exercise; the reported absence of visible histological damage applies to the sampled muscle, not to a tendon-injury assessment.[^miller2005] The Arampatzis intervention establishes that a longer exposure history can alter stiffness and elastic modulus without a detectable cross-sectional-area change. Estimated strain magnitude differed within that intervention, while strain duration entered through the authors' comparison with an earlier protocol.[^arampatzis2010]

These sources support the progression from exposure to response to changed material behavior, but they do not form one continuous experiment. The local exposure in Miller was not measured with Obst's method; the acute synthesis response was not shown to cause Arampatzis's material change; and none of the three studies establishes a symptom or diagnosis.

### Supported adverse branch — repeated activity can change tendon structure and material behavior in an animal model

Soslowsky and colleagues compared 36 rats exposed to a downhill-running overuse regimen for 4, 8, or 16 weeks with ten cage-activity controls. Exercised supraspinatus tendons showed increased cellularity, reduced collagen organization, increased cross-sectional area, and lower modulus and maximum stress at failure under the model.[^soslowsky2000] The study supplies a genuine adverse response branch rather than an inference from repetition alone.

Its limits are central: the quadruped supraspinatus model, downhill-running task, local exposure, recovery history, and tissue state are not a human occupational or athletic dose. The experiment supports “this regimen altered these tendons,” not “overuse causes human tendinopathy” as a universal clinical chain.

### Finding and symptom remain distinct

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies included 810 Achilles and 1,156 patellar tendons from asymptomatic athletes. Baseline sonographic abnormalities were associated with later pain, but positive predictive value was approximately 27% for each tendon group under the included studies.[^cushman2025] The correct lesson is neither that structure is irrelevant nor that an abnormal scan is a latent diagnosis. A finding can alter risk while remaining a poor individual proxy for future symptoms.

## Receiver file: articular cartilage

### Ordinary deformation and fluid-dependent recovery

The Chan and Eckstein human studies establish that ordinary loading produces regional intratissue deformation and a measurable recovery history rather than an unchanging solid response.[^chan2016][^eckstein1999] Cartilage must therefore be represented as a hydrated, time-dependent receiver. These exposure studies do not show damage.

### Biosynthetic and mechanotransductive branches

The Sah calf-cartilage explant study shows that dynamic-compression amplitude, frequency, and waveform affect biosynthetic response.[^sah1989] Kim and colleagues found frequency- and region-dependent biosynthesis and compared those response patterns with predicted fluid-flow, pressure, streaming-potential, and cell-deformation fields.[^kim1994] Together the studies provide a foundational response family while also demonstrating why a single stress scalar is inadequate; Kim's comparisons do not establish which candidate physical mechanism caused the measured response.

Roos and Dahlberg randomized 45 people who had undergone partial medial meniscus resection three to five years earlier to supervised exercise or nonintervention for four months; 30 completed baseline and follow-up dGEMRIC. The exercise group showed a between-group change in the imaging-derived T1(Gd) measure interpreted as compatible with improved glycosaminoglycan content.[^roos2005] The trial supports an adult human cartilage response under a selected at-risk population, but dGEMRIC is an indirect imaging measure, attrition was substantial, local cartilage exposure was not measured, and the study does not identify a universal protective dose.

### Biology-first and interaction branch

Chowdhury and colleagues exposed IL-1β-stimulated primary bovine articular chondrocytes in agarose constructs to 15% dynamic compression at 1 Hz for 48 hours. Under that cell-culture system, compression suppressed measured nitric oxide and PGE2 synthesis and altered proteoglycan synthesis and proliferation through partially distinct pathways.[^chowdhury2001] This is valuable precisely because the biological state is not downstream of the mechanical exposure in the experiment. Cytokine condition and compression interact.

The result supports a biology–mechanics interaction at cell-culture scale. It does not establish an anti-inflammatory exercise effect, disease modification, symptom reduction, or treatment.

### Adverse and rate-sensitive branch

Kurz and colleagues' cartilage-explant work supplies a controlled injurious branch: response differed strongly by strain rate even at the same final compression, and faster protocols altered viability, biosynthesis, and material properties.[^kurz2001] In Szczodry and colleagues' controlled-impact experiments on bovine osteochondral cores, thinner cartilage cores fractured at lower impact energies; among cores that remained intact, chondrocyte death increased with impact energy and between the one- and four-day measurements.[^szczodry2009] The studies support rate-, energy-, structural-thickness-, and time-dependent injury in ex-vivo tissue. Neither supplies a human everyday-use threshold or a direct osteoarthritis trajectory.

### Tissue state and deformation are associated

Cotofana and colleagues compared loaded and unloaded knee MRI in 11 healthy women and 19 women with radiographic tibiofemoral osteoarthritis. Cartilage deformation tended to be greater in the osteoarthritis group under a 50%-body-weight loading condition.[^cotofana2011] This cross-sectional comparison supports an association between measured osteoarthritic state and deformation tendency under the tested load. It does not establish that the tissue state caused the difference, that mechanics initiated the osteoarthritis, or, despite the article's concluding language, that progression was mechanically driven.

## Response fork and branch tests

The chapter's central model should be a fork, not a ladder:

```text
named local exposure + declared receiver state + recovery context
    |
    +-> ordinary maintenance
    +-> useful adaptation under a named function
    +-> no detected response in the selected measure or interval
    +-> transient response with partial or complete measured recovery
    +-> remodeling or repair
    +-> fatigue or reduced tolerance
    +-> protective change or sensitization
    +-> injury or failure
    +-> response dominated by a biological-first or common-cause state
```

Each branch requires four tests:

1. **Object test:** What changed—molecule, cell, matrix, material property, geometry, imaging signal, behavior, symptom, or outcome?
2. **Direction test:** Is the change beneficial, adverse, neutral, mixed, or not classifiable under the measured outcome?
3. **Time test:** Was it transient, recovering, persistent, progressive, or sampled only once?
4. **Alternative test:** Could prior state, measurement error, common task, biological cause, behavior, or regression to the mean explain the observation?

*No detected response* is measure- and interval-specific, not evidence that nothing happened. *Useful adaptation* requires a declared function and cannot be inferred from any increase. *Adverse* requires a material, functional, symptomatic, or clinical criterion, not merely the presence of signaling.

## Reciprocal biology–mechanics feedback

The sources support a bounded reciprocal model:

```text
local exposure history
    -> tissue response
    -> material or structural state changes / remains similar
    -> local deformation, force sharing, contact, or capacity changes / remains similar
    -> later task distribution

reverse and coequal entries:
    biological state -> material behavior -> exposure
    task selection or pain-related behavior -> exposure
    common age, endocrine, inflammatory, genetic, or environmental factor -> both
```

Examples usable in the model:

- higher-strain Achilles training was followed by greater stiffness and modulus, which changes the force–deformation relation for a later task;[^arampatzis2010]
- the rat supraspinatus overuse regimen was followed by lower modulus and failure stress, which changes later material response;[^soslowsky2000]
- a human bone intervention changed one material proxy without detectable selected density or architecture changes, showing that material dimensions need not move together;[^sundh2018]
- osteoarthritic and healthy cartilage states differed in their deformation tendency under the tested load, without establishing which state began the history;[^cotofana2011] and
- an inflammatory cell-culture condition changed the response to compression, making biology a co-input rather than a downstream footnote.[^chowdhury2001]

None proves a self-amplifying clinical loop. A feedback arrow requires dated measurements of both states and their intermediate mechanics. The eventual figure should include stabilizing, amplifying, recovery, reverse, common-cause, and null paths.

## Exposure, response, finding, symptom, diagnosis, and outcome are not synonyms

### Evidence of non-equivalence

Brinjikji and colleagues synthesized 33 studies including 3,110 asymptomatic people and found that several spinal imaging features commonly labeled degenerative were prevalent without pain and increased with age.[^brinjikji2015] Culvenor and colleagues similarly synthesized knee MRI findings in asymptomatic, uninjured adults and found substantial prevalence of cartilage defects, meniscal tears, bone-marrow lesions, and osteophytes, with feature-specific age variation.[^culvenor2019] The prospective tendon-imaging review shows the complementary point: an asymptomatic structural abnormality can be associated with later pain while retaining low positive predictive value for an individual.[^cushman2025]

These findings support a disciplined separation, not nihilism about structure:

| Proposed arrow | Evidence required before the draft crosses it | Counterexample that must remain available |
|---|---|---|
| Exposure -> tissue response | named local exposure, receiver state, temporal order, response measure, comparator or valid baseline | exposure within capacity or no detected response |
| Response -> structural finding | validated tissue-to-finding relation and timing | molecular response without imaging or material change |
| Finding -> function or symptom | population- and condition-specific association plus alternatives | asymptomatic finding |
| Symptom + examination -> diagnosis | accepted diagnostic criteria, differential reasoning, and relevant tests | symptom generated by a different tissue or whole-person process |
| Diagnosis + context -> outcome | longitudinal, treatment, prognostic, and contextual evidence | stable diagnosis with variable outcome or outcome dominated by other factors |

The chapter should also state the reverse possibilities: symptoms can alter task selection and loading; biological disease can alter tissue state before a mechanical observation; and a finding may be incidental context.

## Negative, null, and alternative evidence program

The following are not end-of-chapter disclaimers. They belong beside the positive evidence:

### Ordinary, non-adverse, or unresolved responses

- ordinary cartilage deformation followed by recovery;
- tendon collagen synthesis after exercise without a measured tendon-injury outcome, which therefore cannot by itself populate either an injury or a tolerance branch;
- low-magnitude bone loading with minimal response under one temporal protocol;
- many human intervention measures remaining unchanged despite a declared exercise program.

### Biology-first or mechanically modified rather than mechanically initiated

- IL-1β state interacting with cartilage compression in cell culture;
- osteoarthritic cartilage showing different deformation under load without establishing initiation;
- age-related bone changes failing to track habitual-load proxies closely;
- endocrine, metabolic, medication, genetic, inflammatory, developmental, nutritional, sleep, and vascular state as named nodes when relevant.

### Finding without symptom and symptom without selected finding

- asymptomatic spinal and knee imaging findings;
- asymptomatic tendon abnormality with low individual positive predictive value;
- pain, disability, and outcome requiring condition-specific evidence beyond structural response.

### Reverse causality and common cause

- tissue state changes stiffness or deformation and therefore later exposure;
- symptom or threat changes movement and task selection;
- one age, disease, treatment, or environment changes both tissue state and mechanics;
- a modeled exposure and a finding can share a task or anatomy without one causing the other.

## Evidence gaps before publication

### Foundational and technical

- Confirm tissue-mechanics terminology and matrix descriptions with bone, tendon, and cartilage specialists.
- Specify the exposure quantity, frame, spatial resolution, constitutive assumptions, and measurement error for every figure.
- Decide whether *mechanical homeostasis* remains organizing vocabulary or becomes a formal model; no body-wide set point is established here.
- Distinguish material fatigue, cellular response, sensitization, damage, injury, repair, and degeneration with tissue-specific definitions.

### Multiscale bridge

- No source in this packet measures one declared whole-body task, complete internal distribution, local exposure, cell response, structural change, symptom, and outcome in one longitudinal history.
- The opening bone–tendon–cartilage example is therefore a pedagogical juxtaposition and must be labeled as such.
- Model-derived local exposure requires sensitivity and validation; task labels and external force measurements cannot fill that gap.

### Human translation

- Most causal mechanotransduction and injury evidence is animal, explant, or cell-culture evidence.
- Human intervention studies are small, receiver-specific, and commonly use estimated exposure or imaging/material proxies.
- Sex, age, health state, anatomical site, prior training, medication, endocrine state, and recovery context limit transportability.
- No source here establishes a preferred whole-body route, posterior loading rule, condition diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment.

### Clinical bridge

- Any response-to-symptom sentence requires condition-, population-, timeframe-, and outcome-specific evidence.
- Imaging prevalence in asymptomatic people does not establish that findings are clinically meaningless; comparative and longitudinal evidence remains necessary.
- Pain, function, diagnosis, disability, and valued outcome must remain distinct endpoints.
- Medical-coauthor review is required for every disease-, symptom-, diagnostic-, prognostic-, or treatment-adjacent sentence.

## Controlled drafting handoff

Independent source and salvage review of this packet is complete. The Chapter 19 first draft is ready to begin if it:

- starts from a Chapter 18 local-exposure record rather than from posture, task, or a disease label;
- presents bone, tendon, and cartilage as different receivers under a synthetic comparison, not one measured shared dose;
- states a positive mechanobiology thesis early;
- gives dose and recovery multiple dimensions and receiver-specific clocks;
- uses mechanotransduction to open branches rather than prove harm;
- includes maintenance, adaptation, no detected response, recovery, fatigue/remodeling, injury, and biology-first branches;
- distinguishes acute response from net material adaptation;
- uses adverse evidence only where damage or material change was actually measured;
- shows that response can change later mechanics without assigning an initiating cause;
- keeps exposure, response, finding, symptom, diagnosis, and outcome on separate evidence rows;
- includes asymptomatic and positive-predictive-value evidence without implying that structure is irrelevant;
- hands vessels, nerves, lymphatics, airways, glide interfaces, and cavities to Chapter 20;
- contains no exercise prescription, tissue threshold, disease attribution, preferred route, or treatment inference; and
- preserves the larger biomechanical-medicine research program as staged obligations rather than deleting or prematurely publishing it.

No authorial decision blocks the draft. New reader prose that materially changes a packet claim will require renewed source verification. Visual construction, tissue-specialist review, and medical/causal review remain publication gates.
