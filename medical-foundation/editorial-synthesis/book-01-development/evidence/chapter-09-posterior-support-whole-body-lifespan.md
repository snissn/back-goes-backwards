# Chapter 9 evidence packet — posterior support across the whole body and lifespan

**Unit:** `BGB-U-0009`

**Status:** controlled evidence and model packet for first-draft use; not reader prose

**Primary controls:** [Chapter 9 brief](../chapter-briefs/BGB-U-0009-posterior-support-whole-body-lifespan.md), [Chapter 8 evidence packet](chapter-08-physics-ordinary-function-posterior-support.md), `BGB-M-0003`, `BGB-M-0004`, `BGB-M-0005`, `BGB-Q-0006`, `BGB-Q-0009`, and `BGB-Q-0010`

## Editorial decision

Chapter 9 should preserve the archive's full whole-body and lifespan ambition. It should not reduce the posterior thesis to a short warning that “all systems matter,” and it should not restore the older claim that posterior engagement is already known to protect every body across every life stage. The defensible synthesis is stronger and more useful:

> Posterior structures form a substantial anatomical and mechanical system whose contribution changes with the task, phase, body, state, history, environment, and available support. Anterior–posterior distribution is therefore a useful conditional lens inside whole-body load sharing. The project further expects posterior participation to be necessary but insufficient within the preferred organization developed in Book II; that expectation is stated directly here but does not carry Book I.

The chapter closes the positive architecture of Part II. It shows a body whose support is acquired, altered, assisted, and reorganized over time. It also gives Part III a clean baseline: adverse load management can be discussed only after viable variation, useful compensation, and altered boundaries have been recognized.

## Three posterior propositions with independent burdens

| Level | Proposition | Present posture | What can support it | What cannot support it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — system existence | Posterior structures form a major layered anatomical and mechanical system | FND/SYN; owned by Book I | documented anatomy plus task- and phase-specific measured or modeled contribution from Chapters 7–8 | muscle size alone, one fascial line, evolutionary narrative, or the preferred-configuration hypothesis |
| 2 — conditional lens | Anterior–posterior distribution can organize useful questions about whole-body load sharing | SYN; owned by Book I | comparisons in which the lens changes a prediction, measurement, or interpretation while other dimensions remain visible | calling every posterior contribution beneficial, every anterior contribution substitutive, or the lens universally sufficient |
| 3 — preferred organization | Posterior participation is expected to be necessary but insufficient within the preferred all-Yang organization | HYP; named but non-load-bearing in Book I | Book II definitions, scale choice, derivation, efficiency objective, countercases, and discriminating measurements | Levels 1–2, posterior anatomy, ordinary extension, or a task in which posterior work is large |

“Necessary” at Level 3 still needs a formal object and scale. It may refer to a whole-person organization while permitting a selected posterior structure to be quiet, lengthening, internally loaded, or mechanically secondary during a particular phase. Book II must decide what counts as participation, what state is preferred, and what observation would refute necessity. Chapter 9 preserves the author's expectation without supplying those missing definitions by implication.

## The system boundary must include the life around the body

The compact callback to the recurring object transfer should begin with the Chapter 8 posterior overlay and then widen the selected system. Add anterior and lateral contributors, deep segmental control, skeletal contacts, pressure-bearing boundaries, sensory information, the object, the floor, any furniture or rail, and any person or device providing assistance. The widening is not rhetorical completeness. Each added element can cross the system boundary with force, moment, information, constraint, or energy and can change the internal solution.

Walking simulations illustrate why contribution is phase- and model-specific: gluteal, hamstring, plantar-flexor, vasti, and skeletal-alignment relations perform different support and progression jobs at different speeds.[^liu2008] Perturbation experiments likewise show several direction-dependent recovery strategies rather than one posterior response.[^karabin2024] An active exoskeleton study altered modeled spinal loading and lumbar motion by redistributing device support between lumbar and hip joints, showing that an external carrier can change a task's mechanical solution without becoming part of the person's anatomy.[^brouwer2024] These studies do not establish one whole-body partition, but together they justify a model in which contribution depends on phase, endpoint, boundary, and available support.

### Whole-body load-sharing matrix

The draft should use a matrix like the following, populated with a few concrete rows rather than every possible structure:

| Task phase | Selected output | Posterior participation | Cooperating relations | Boundary or state that can change the result | Evidence state |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carry at steady speed | object support and trunk moment balance | active or passive posterior trunk, hip, and lower-limb contribution | anterior pressure boundary, lateral balance, skeletal contact, grip, ground reaction | object position, hand choice, speed, fatigue, belt or device, external help | measured external contacts; internal sharing modeled or inferred |
| Place object | deceleration, lowering, and final contact | negative work, restraint, or changing leverage in selected posterior structures | knee extensors, upper limb, foot placement, target support, sensory guidance | target height, permission to lean, assistance, pain, range, confidence | mixed measurement and model |
| Recover from perturbation | prevent a fall or regain a viable base | hip, ankle, trunk, or stepping contribution depending on direction and timing | lateral ankle action, foot placement, arms, vision, vestibular and somatosensory input, rail or helper | perturbation direction, predictability, footwear, surface, support availability | observed kinematics and kinetics under a defined protocol |
| Walk with an unloading device | progression while unloading a selected region | posterior work can be reduced, displaced, or retained in a different phase | device contact, upper limb, contralateral limb, trunk, ground, user control | device design, fit, goal, comfort, speed, allowed weight bearing | device-specific measurement; no universal ranking |

The matrix fails if it merely lists systems. It succeeds only when a changed boundary or contributor changes the force balance, modeled internal partition, energetic cost, stability endpoint, or interpretation.

## Region, domain, role, and sign are not synonyms

The archive often placed posterior, exterior, hard, and Yang in one sentence because it was reaching toward a coherent whole-body relation. Chapter 9 should preserve that research direction while keeping four objects distinct:

| Column | Question answered | Example | Not equivalent to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Region | Where is the structure relative to a declared anatomical frame? | posterior thigh, anterior abdominal wall, lateral hip, deep spinal region | mechanical role or direction of force |
| Relative domain | What lies outside or inside a selected boundary? | skin relative to viscera; a cavity wall relative to contents | posterior/anterior |
| Mechanical role | Is the selected relation relatively shape-preserving/path-establishing or form-receiving/shape-conforming in this direction, task, state, and timescale? | a pressurized abdominal boundary can preserve shape; an active posterior muscle can yield | fixed tissue class, location, strength, or virtue |
| Proposed sign or traditional relation | Which Yin/Yang tendency or later coordinate family is being proposed? | a future Yang assignment under a defined axial, rotational, or lateral mode | validated mechanical variable or anatomical region |

Hard and soft remain relational roles motivated by traditional Chinese medicine's hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts. A posterior muscle can perform a form-receiving role while actively lengthening. An anterior or lateral pressure-supported assembly can perform a shape-preserving role. A tendon can transmit substantial tension and still deform. A bone can cease to be the dominant path when its contact is removed. These counterexamples do not weaken the hard/soft model; they prevent it from collapsing into a tissue or region taxonomy.

The later hypothesis may find systematic relations among the columns. If so, the relation will be more meaningful because it was not built into the definitions.

## Development creates changing options, not one completed template

The support system available at a given moment is historically formed. Geometry, mass distribution, muscle architecture, tendon behavior, contact experience, sensory calibration, confidence, task opportunity, and learned coordination change together without following one deterministic sequence.

Hinnekens and colleagues followed eighteen infants between birth and walking onset and found that trial-to-trial organization of leg-muscle activity changed over development: early variability could be generated from relatively few locomotor primitives, while later activation became more regular as the identified primitive structure changed.[^hinnekens2023] This is a small longitudinal EMG and modeling study, not a universal developmental law, but it directly opposes the picture of one adult coordination pattern simply becoming stronger.

Naturalistic observation supplies a complementary scale. Adolph and colleagues recorded spontaneous locomotion in twelve- to nineteen-month-old infants and found large volumes of short, variable bouts rather than only periodic straight walking; walking experience was distributed across thousands of steps and repeated falls in the recorded hour.[^adolph2012] The study describes learning opportunities in its sample. It does not turn variability, falling, or a milestone date into a health score.

For Chapter 9, the transferable conclusions are limited but important:

- motor organization and experience change before and after walking onset;
- variability can be part of exploration rather than evidence of failure;
- a visible task can emerge from changing neural, musculoskeletal, environmental, and experiential constraints; and
- developmental routes should not be scored against a posterior-engagement ideal without age-, task-, population-, and measurement-specific evidence.

The archive's stage-wise sequence should remain, but stages serve as questions: what body, task, environment, and strategy are available now? They are not instructions about when one posterior pattern must appear.

## Training changes specific capabilities and strategies

“Training the posterior chain” is too coarse to name the state change. Training can alter force, power, endurance, tissue dimensions, stiffness, timing, skill, confidence, perception, or task strategy, and changes can transfer unevenly across outcomes.

In a six-week randomized comparison involving thirty older adults, Pagan and colleagues reported that conventional high-intensity resistance training produced larger gains in the trained strength tests, while loaded functional practice produced larger changes in gait speed and timed-up-and-go; some isometric and muscle-size measures improved in both groups.[^pagan2024] The sample and duration were small, and the study does not establish an optimal program. It provides a useful existence proof that “capacity improved” must be decomposed by task and measure.

Chapter 9 should therefore distinguish at least:

1. the trained exposure;
2. the measured adaptation;
3. transfer to an untrained task;
4. persistence after training;
5. the strategy used to produce the outcome; and
6. the person's goal and available alternatives.

The posterior system can become more capable without becoming the only changed system. A task can improve without a measured increase in posterior force. A stronger posterior contribution can coexist with higher cost or poorer transfer. These are research outcomes, not reasons to suppress the chapter's positive claim that posterior structures are trainable and consequential.

## Pregnancy changes demand while preserving multiple viable strategies

Pregnancy is not a short-lived deviation from a canonical adult model. It changes the system's mass distribution, anthropometry, tissue state, fatigue and recovery context, and sometimes the supports and strategies a person chooses. The mechanical effects are neither uniform nor readable from appearance alone.

Foti and colleagues compared three-dimensional gait in fifteen women late in pregnancy and one year postpartum. Overall gait kinematics changed less than a stereotype of “waddling” would predict, even as body mass and trunk mass distribution changed; hip and ankle kinetic measures increased, and individual pelvic-tilt changes varied widely.[^foti2000] In a separate repeated-measures treadmill study of fifteen pregnant participants, Flores and colleagues found gestation-related changes in center-of-mass-based walking-balance measures and showed that the anthropometric method used to locate trunk center of mass materially affected the result.[^flores2018]

These studies support three narrow claims:

- a changing body can preserve a familiar external task through changed internal or kinetic demands;
- individual trajectories can differ even within a small, defined population; and
- measurement models must change when anthropometry changes.

They do not establish a universal pregnancy posture, posterior deficit, fall mechanism, treatment, or corrective strategy. Chapter 9 can say that posterior participation is reorganized only when it names the measured task and keeps anterior, lateral, pressure, sensory, and environmental relations visible.

## Pain, injury, illness, and connective-tissue variation change interpretation

### Pain can change strategy without identifying origin

Pain can change movement, but changed movement does not reveal pain's origin. In an experimental crossover study of thirty healthy adults, Devecchi and colleagues coupled lumbar electrical stimulation to flexion or extension during repeated box lifting. Participants reduced motion in the pain-provoking direction, and some changes persisted after the stimulation ended.[^devecchi2025] Because pain was experimentally imposed and direction-linked, the study supports a behavioral adaptation to that protocol. It does not show that a similar movement in a person with chronic pain identifies the initiating tissue, mechanical history, or preferred correction.

### Injury changes the starting state and available alternatives

Injury can alter material properties, sensation, confidence, active control, support, and exposure. Chapter 9 should treat those as candidate variables, not infer that a prior load path caused the injury or that one reorganization will reverse it. The viable mechanical question is narrower: given the present tissue state, task, supports, and goals, what is the current strategy preserving, and what demands does it redistribute? Any named injury requires its own evidence before the draft asserts the direction or magnitude of change.

### Illness must be specified rather than treated as one mechanical category

Illness may change energy availability, recovery, circulation, sensation, control, task goals, and the value of assistance, but no single direction of change follows from the word. This list is a modeling inventory rather than a universal causal sequence. A reader-facing example must name the condition, relevant state variable, task, timescale, treatment context, and alternatives before claiming a mechanical effect. The immediate conclusion is only that illness can change the system being modeled and therefore cannot be represented as a generic departure from an otherwise canonical body.

### Connective-tissue variation changes assumptions without determining strategy

Connective-tissue variation also changes what can be assumed about restraint and stiffness. In a cross-sectional comparison of twenty-nine people classified with joint hypermobility syndrome and thirty controls, Alsiri and colleagues reported differences in walking speed, stance and double-support duration, joint moments, and power, while the observed gait was described as relatively stiff rather than visibly hypermobile.[^alsiri2020] Symptoms, selection, and the cross-sectional design prevent causal attribution. The useful lesson is that a label about passive range does not uniquely determine the strategy used during a task.

Compensation should begin as a neutral description of a change that preserves something valuable: task completion, comfort, tissue protection, balance, participation, or energy. Whether it is costly, temporary, stable, necessary, or harmful is a second question requiring chronology and an outcome.

## Disability and assistance change the mechanical system

The World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health treats functioning and disability as interactions among health conditions and contextual factors and explicitly includes environmental factors, activity, and participation.[^whoicf] Chapter 9 need not adopt the ICF as a mechanical model. It should adopt the discipline that a person's current environment and support are part of the situation being described.

An assistive device is not merely a correction applied to an otherwise complete free body. It adds contacts, constraints, mass, geometry, sensory information, and control requirements. In a within-subject comparison involving twenty healthy participants asked to walk while unloading one ankle and foot, Saffuri and colleagues found different kinematic, force, metabolic, comfort, and stability measures across forearm crutches, a knee crutch, and an unloading orthosis.[^saffuri2024] The study does not represent disabled users, long-term use, clinical effectiveness, or every device. It demonstrates that support design changes the mechanical solution and its tradeoffs.

The chapter should therefore describe an assisted strategy as a complete viable system:

```text
person + device + fit + ground + task + environment + control history
```

Removing the device from the analysis does not reveal the “true” body. It changes the boundary and may remove the very route that makes the task possible. Lived-experience and accessibility review remain publication gates because laboratory mechanics cannot determine a person's priorities, comfort, participation, or preferred strategy.

## Aging changes trajectories and margins, not human worth or one chain

Population studies support average changes in selected measures, but they also show that different quantities and people follow different paths. White and colleagues followed 2,364 initially well-functioning adults aged seventy to seventy-nine and identified three gait-speed trajectories over eight years, including markedly different rates of decline.[^white2013] Hicks and colleagues used InCHIANTI data from 934 adults aged at least sixty-five and found that baseline lower-extremity strength and power related to later mobility outcomes, but a single measurement and change over time did not become interchangeable predictors.[^hicks2012] A later InCHIANTI analysis made the measurement problem more specific: leg power discriminated later loss of activities of daily living better than knee strength in men, while neither measure showed the same advantage in women.[^travaglini2025]

These studies justify neither fatalism nor a posterior-chain score. They support a conditional concept of reserve:

> For a defined task, time window, and current state, reserve is the margin between the demand being met and the capability available through the whole person–environment system.

Reserve is not one scalar already measured by gait speed, strength, power, or age. Its components can include force and power, endurance, balance recovery, sensory confidence, pacing, task knowledge, recovery rate, environmental access, assistance, and alternative strategies. A handrail can increase usable reserve for one transition without changing muscle capacity. Familiarity can reduce a task's effective demand. Illness or poor sleep can narrow the margin temporarily. Training can expand one component while leaving another unchanged.

The draft should make at least three illustrative trajectories visible: declining, stable, and improving or reconfigured. These are editorial possibilities, not categories supplied by the cited cohort study. It should also show that the same measured task can occupy different fractions of available capacity. A slower task is not automatically inefficient; it may be an effective way to preserve reserve.

## Candidate observations are questions, not tests

Chapter 9 can preserve the archive's desire for recognizable signs by turning each sign into a measurement program. Four questions must remain distinct:

| Question | Candidate observation | Minimum added work | What the observation cannot say alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Participation | EMG, modeled muscle force, joint moment sensitivity, tendon strain, pressure, contact, device load | define task, phase, system, signal validity, and alternatives | capacity, correctness, or causality |
| Capacity | maximal or repeated task output, force, power, endurance, range under demand | repeatability, population, safety, task specificity, and strategy | reserve in daily life or tissue health |
| Compensation | changed timing, contact, support, asymmetry, bracing, route, pace, or assistance | chronology, preserved goal, prior state, alternatives, and cost | dysfunction or hidden injury |
| Reserve | response to graded demand, dual task, perturbation, fatigue, recovery, or loss of support | define margin, stopping rule, timescale, and whole-system contributors | a universal health score or age-equivalent |

Every candidate should carry a provenance state: observed, measured, calculated, modeled, inferred, hypothesized, or embodied. Reliability, validity, responsiveness, reference population, measurement error, and clinically meaningful change are later obligations. An asymmetry can be stable and useful. A visible brace can be purposeful. Fatigue can be perceived without measured performance loss, and performance can decline without identifying a tissue. A change after assistance can show that the boundary mattered without diagnosing why.

## Counterexamples and stop rules

| Tempting inference | Counterexample or alternative | Required drafting move |
|---|---|---|
| Posterior structures are major, so more posterior participation is always better | a task can use efficient skeletal alignment, anterior or lateral support, pressure, or an external carrier; added activation can increase cost | state the outcome and compare routes rather than rank activation |
| Posterior means hard | an active posterior muscle can yield; an anterior pressure boundary can preserve shape | assign role by task, direction, state, scale, and time |
| A noncanonical strategy is compensation, therefore it is defective | assistance, pacing, asymmetry, or altered contact may be the stable solution that preserves participation | name what is preserved and which alternative is actually available |
| Development should converge on one adult pattern | early coordination and natural practice are variable, and anatomy and opportunity change | use developmental questions, not a deficit ladder |
| Training proves the preferred route | changes are task- and measure-specific and can transfer unevenly | report trained exposure and measured outcome separately |
| Pregnancy exposes a posterior deficit | external kinematics can be preserved while kinetics and anthropometric assumptions change | describe the measured change without posture verdict |
| Painful movement reveals the cause | pain itself can alter strategy and learning | separate current adaptation from initiating mechanism |
| A mobility marker is a reserve score | prediction and discrimination depend on outcome, population, threshold, and other systems | keep markers plural and validation unfinished |
| Level 1 or 2 proves Level 3 | a major system can be optional for a selected task or inefficiently recruited | show prohibited inference in the proposition figure |

The Chapter 9 account should be weakened or revised when the anterior–posterior lens does not change a prediction; when a nonposterior, environmental, sensory, or task-specific model explains the selected endpoint more simply; when a proposed marker lacks repeatability or adds no information; or when viable strategies contradict a single reference trajectory. None of those outcomes automatically refutes the broader Level 3 hypothesis, but Book II must explain rather than absorb them.

## First-draft authorization

A controlled Chapter 9 draft is authorized if it:

- embeds posterior contribution in anterior, lateral, deep, skeletal, pressure, sensory, environmental, and assistive cooperation without neutralizing its substantial role;
- gives region, relative domain, mechanical role, and proposed sign separate definitions and examples;
- treats development, training, pregnancy, pain or injury, illness, connective-tissue variation, disability and assistance, aging, and reserve as distinct contexts;
- represents multiple viable trajectories rather than one canonical adult peak and decline;
- defines compensation first by what it preserves and reserve as a conditional whole-system margin;
- converts candidate signs into participation, capacity, compensation, or reserve questions rather than a checklist;
- states Levels 1–3 directly, including the author's expectation of necessary-but-insufficient posterior participation at Level 3;
- makes Level 3 visibly nonderived and non-load-bearing for Book I; and
- ends Part II with the positive baseline Part III needs.

The draft is not authorized to prescribe a developmental milestone, pregnancy posture, training program, assistive device, aging strategy, or posterior engagement cue; score a person against an unassisted symmetrical body; diagnose cause from pain or movement; equate posterior with hard or Yang; use assistance as evidence of deficit; or claim that posterior organization prevents disease, injury, or decline.
