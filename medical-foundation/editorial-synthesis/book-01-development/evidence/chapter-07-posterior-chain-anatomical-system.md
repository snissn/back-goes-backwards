## Editorial decision

Chapter 7 can state a positive anatomical conclusion without making the later preferred-configuration hypothesis carry Book I:

> The posterior body contains an extensive, layered, branching anatomical system of muscles, tendons, aponeuroses, fasciae, ligaments, bones, joints, interfaces, and control relations extending from ground contact toward the cranium. Direct attachments and several regional continuities are documented, and bounded mechanical effects have been measured across some regional connections. These facts make posterior anatomy a serious system for mechanical analysis. They do not establish one uninterrupted cable, one conserved force traveling foot to skull, posterior dominance in every task, a clinical outcome, or the preferred organization investigated in Book II.

This preserves the archive's central observation: the posterior chain is not merely a gym slogan or an arbitrary list. The calf–Achilles complex, hamstrings and gluteals, spinal extensors and segmental muscles, thoracolumbar fascial and aponeurotic structures, latissimus dorsi, trapezius, and scapular relations form a substantial anatomical program. The chapter's discipline is to identify **what kind of relation** joins each region and to stop at the strongest relation actually demonstrated.

## Chapter theorem and boundary

The chapter must keep four propositions separate:

1. **Anatomical components exist.** Named posterior muscles, tendons, ligaments, fasciae, bones, joints, and interfaces can be described with standard anatomical terminology.[^fipat]
2. **Regional continuities exist.** Some adjacent structures attach directly, share a connective interface, blend through an aponeurosis or retinaculum, or meet through a skeletal junction.
3. **Functional coordination and regional transfer can occur.** Distant structures may participate during one task, and some experiments measure effects across a declared regional connection.
4. **One consequential whole-posterior route remains a hypothesis.** A mechanically significant transfer from foot to cranium requires a named carrier, quantity, direction, task, state, and measurement. It cannot be inferred from a continuous drawing.

Chapter 7 establishes the first two, permits bounded examples of the third so the distinction is concrete, and preserves the fourth as a research question. Chapter 8 owns task-specific magnitude and role. Book II owns the proposed preferred posterior organization. Book III owns the fuller hard/soft and Yin/Yang correspondence. Books IV and V own breath-driver and practice claims.

## The nested-scope model

Every use of *posterior chain* in the draft must carry one of three scopes. The scopes are nested for editorial clarity; they are not three claims that nature contains three sharply separated chains.

| Scope | Inclusion rule | Typical contents | Legitimate chapter job | Exclusion or stop rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Narrow posterior chain** | Selected muscles situated primarily posteriorly in the declared anatomical frame, plus their direct tendons or aponeuroses | gastrocnemius–soleus–Achilles complex; hamstrings and proximal/distal tendons; gluteus maximus; erector-spinae and multifidus groups; latissimus dorsi; trapezius and selected posterior scapular muscles | Establish a recognizable muscular and myotendinous map | Does not imply direct serial continuity among all listed muscles or simultaneous recruitment |
| **Broad posterior chain** | Narrow chain plus a named anatomical attachment, connective continuity, shared retinaculum, aponeurosis, fascial layer, ligament, or sliding interface relevant to the region | plantar aponeurosis relation at the calcaneus; sacrotuberous ligament; erector-spinae aponeurosis; thoracolumbar fascial layers; nuchal ligament; vertebral aponeurosis; capsules and retinacula where specified | Show how regions are joined, partitioned, guided, or allowed to slide | A shared layer, bone, or interface is not automatically a direct tensile cable or consequential transfer route |
| **Whole-posterior support system** | Broad chain plus every skeletal, articular, pressure, sensory, control, external-contact, anterior, lateral, or deep relation required to close a declared mechanical question | foot and ground contact; calcaneus and other bones; ankle, hip, sacroiliac, vertebral, scapulothoracic, shoulder, and craniovertebral relations; abdominal and serratus-anterior partners; pressure and neural control | Provide the complete system boundary for later task mechanics | Inclusion does not reclassify all partners as posterior or prove that the posterior subset dominates the system |

The term *posterior* uses standard anatomical orientation at the regional scale unless a figure explicitly introduces a local frame. A muscle that wraps laterally, crosses compartments, or changes orientation is not forced into a binary identity. A structure may enter the whole-support scope because a question cannot be closed without it, even if the structure is anterior or lateral.

## Relation grammar: anatomy is not one kind of connection

The draft should classify every cross-structure statement before describing it smoothly.

| Relation | Required observation | Example | Inference still unavailable |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Named component and attachment** | Standard identification and origin/insertion or attachment description | gastrocnemius and soleus contributing to the Achilles tendon | Consequential transfer beyond that bounded muscle–tendon relation |
| **Direct tissue continuity** | Continuous fibers or a direct attachment demonstrated by dissection, histology, or imaging | superficial sacrotuberous-ligament fibers continuing with long-head biceps-femoris tendon in the studied specimens | Uniformity across people, loading magnitude, or task importance |
| **Connective continuity or shared interface** | Blend, aponeurosis, retinaculum, periosteal relation, fascial layer, or sliding plane | Achilles paratenon–calcaneal periosteum–plantar-fascia relation | One unbroken fiber population or lossless transmission |
| **Shared skeletal junction** | Structures attach to or act through the same bone or joint complex | Achilles tendon and plantar fascia meeting the calcaneal system | Direct soft-tissue continuity or identical loading direction |
| **Coordinated task participation** | Several regions change activation, motion, stiffness, or timing during the same declared task | calf, hip, trunk, and shoulder relations during carrying | That their changes are caused by direct tensile transmission |
| **Measured regional transfer** | A perturbation at one named structure produces a quantified mechanical effect at another under controlled conditions | 10-N traction on latissimus or transversus-abdominis attachments producing measured tension and marker displacement in lumbar fasciae in cadavers | Ordinary in-vivo magnitude, unique carrier, whole-chain propagation, or clinical consequence |
| **Inferred long-range or whole-system transfer** | A model links named carriers, boundary conditions, quantities, and measurements across several regions | candidate foot-to-cranium force, stress, strain, or stiffness route | Acceptance until the model discriminates itself from neural control, skeletal coupling, pressure, external contact, and correlated movement |

*Force line* may remain project-native vocabulary, but it must be marked as an external line of action, inferred internal multicarrier relation, or embodied continuity. A visually or experientially continuous line is hypothesis-generating unless its carrier, quantity, scale, state, and measurement are specified.

## Controlling “large” and “mechanically substantial”

The archive's observation that the posterior system contains some of the body's largest muscles is recoverable, but only after *large* is given a metric.

| Metric | What it can support | What it cannot support by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Muscle volume or mass | Amount of contractile material and an important correlate of strength | Physiological cross-sectional area, moment arm, activation, realized force, endurance, or task priority |
| Physiological cross-sectional area | Architectural force-generating capacity under the model's assumptions | In-vivo force, moment, work, or dominance in an actual task |
| Fiber or fascicle length | Candidate excursion and shortening range | Force capacity or whole-system transfer |
| Tendon dimensions | A substantial tensile structure and candidate load capacity | Ordinary strain, safety factor, energy return, or a continuation beyond its attachment |
| Attachment area or anatomical span | Broad regional access and plausible coupling | Large transmitted force or preference |
| Net joint moment or modeled muscle contribution | A task- and phase-bounded mechanical role | Anatomical primacy outside that task or a unique internal solution |

Riem and colleagues segmented 70 muscles and 13 bones in whole-body MRI scans from 102 active healthy adults aged 19–50. Their dataset supports volume-based comparisons and shows that posterior groups such as the gluteals, erector spinae, soleus, latissimus dorsi, and trapezius occupy substantial measurable volume.[^riem2026] Ward and colleagues dissected 27 lower-limb muscles from 21 limbs and showed why architecture changes the ranking: soleus and gluteus medius had large physiological cross-sectional areas, while the nonposterior vastus lateralis was also among the strongest by that proxy.[^ward2009] O'Brien's Achilles review describes the tendon as the conjoined gastrocnemius–soleus tendon, sometimes receiving plantaris contribution, and conventionally the body's largest and strongest tendon.[^obrien2005]

The authorized synthesis is therefore:

> The posterior system includes several of the body's largest or most architecturally substantial muscle–tendon units under stated measures. Large nonposterior muscles also exist, and no size measure establishes a universal mechanical priority.

Latissimus dorsi and trapezius may be called **broad**, **extensive**, or **region-spanning** on anatomical grounds. They should not be placed in an absolute whole-body size ranking unless the draft names the dataset, metric, and comparator.

### Distal junction: direct Achilles continuity, qualified plantar relation

The calf–Achilles relation is the chapter's clearest direct myotendinous example. Gastrocnemius and soleus contribute to the Achilles tendon; plantaris may contribute. The tendon inserts into the calcaneal system.[^obrien2005] That is enough to establish a substantial bounded chain.

The transition from Achilles tendon to plantar fascia is more instructive because it resists a simple drawing. Singh and colleagues studied 19 cadaveric feet with imaging, serial sectioning, plastination, morphometry, and selected histology. They found morphometric correlations and a superficial paratenon–periosteum–plantar-fascia relation, but did not confirm complete fiber continuity by x-ray or histology.[^singh2021] The calcaneus is therefore not merely an interruption to be erased: it is a skeletal junction through which attachments, trabecular architecture, periosteal tissues, and contact geometry may relate. The morphology makes a mechanical question plausible; it does not answer the magnitude, direction, task, or clinical meaning.

This junction should be the first visual counterexample to a literal cable. It simultaneously preserves the foot-to-cranium ambition and teaches that a real pathway may change carrier and form.

### Pelvic junction: branching attachments rather than a passive bridge

The hamstrings do not begin as one uniform proximal cord. In a 2024 anatomical, histological, and ultrasound study of 97 hemipelves from 66 cryopreserved specimens, Miguel-Pérez and colleagues described retinacular tissue around the proximal hamstring tendons, layered sacrotuberous-ligament relations, a superficial sacrotuberous layer continuing caudally with the long-head biceps-femoris tendon, and connections involving semimembranosus and adductor magnus.[^miguelperez2024] These observations justify a real regional connective map while also showing several layers, neighboring structures, and different insertions.

Gluteus maximus is large by volume and spans broad pelvic and femoral relations; the hamstrings include biarticular and monoarticular relations with different architecture and excursion. The pelvis also connects the lower limbs and trunk through bone, hip and sacroiliac joints, ligaments, deep hip muscles, lateral muscles, abdominal relations, and neural control. Chapter 7 should therefore present it as a **junction**: multiple carriers may converge, branch, slide, or change direction. Chapter 8 later asks what those relations do during gait, lifting, sitting, carrying, and balance.

### Axial system: long spans, segmental spans, aponeuroses, and sliding planes

The term *spinal extensors* hides important differences. Erector-spinae subdivisions span regions and attach substantially through aponeurotic structures. Multifidus and other transversospinal muscles have shorter and overlapping fascicular relations. A chemical-dissection study of eight cadavers reported four lumbar-multifidus layers, multipennate architecture, and inter-fascicle attachments rather than one separable cord.[^lonnemann2008] A 2018 study of ten fresh cadavers described the erector-spinae aponeurosis and thoracolumbar fascia as structures that circumscribe subcompartments and sliding areas between the fascia and paraspinal muscles, between erector spinae and multifidus, and between longissimus and iliocostalis.[^creze2018]

These studies support the chapter's layered anatomy. They do not show that each structure always produces extension, remains active during every upright task, or carries one continuous cranio-caudal force. *Extensor* remains a conventional anatomical and kinematic label; it is not a preferred-direction conclusion.

### Thoracolumbar and scapular junction: a regional transfer problem with nonposterior partners

The thoracolumbar fascia is not one sheet detached from the rest of the trunk. Willard and colleagues describe a girdling arrangement of fascial and aponeurotic layers, with latissimus-dorsi and serratus-posterior-inferior aponeuroses contributing to the superficial posterior layer, a deeper paraspinal retinacular sheath, and relations with abdominal aponeuroses at the lateral raphe.[^willard2012] This anatomy makes the thoracolumbar region an especially strong example of why *posterior system* cannot mean posterior structures acting alone.

The region also supplies bounded transfer evidence. Barker and colleagues applied 10 N of traction to named muscle attachments in eight unembalmed cadavers and measured tension and marker movement in posterior and middle lumbar fasciae. Latissimus dorsi and transversus abdominis produced the largest affected regions in the posterior layer under that setup, with gluteus maximus and oblique effects more locally distributed.[^barker2004] The experiment demonstrates that named regional fascial tissues can transmit tension under controlled cadaveric loading. It does not estimate ordinary living forces, active sharing, adaptation, clinical consequence, or a whole-posterior route.

Carvalhais and colleagues tested 37 living participants and found that passive or active latissimus-dorsi tensioning changed a contralateral hip resting-position endpoint; active tensioning also changed passive hip stiffness.[^carvalhais2013] This is a useful regional in-vivo effect, not a direct measurement of fascial force. The endpoint can reflect several interacting carriers, joint mechanics, and control relations. A 2025 follow-up in 48 participants found different distal hip responses between chronic-low-back-pain and control groups and described the measured hip-stiffness change as small and below the standard error of measurement, reinforcing that effect size, population, and endpoint matter.[^procopio2025]

At the scapula, trapezius is extensive but does not stabilize or rotate the scapula in isolation. Serratus anterior is a principal synergistic partner, and the scapula's relation to the thorax depends on several muscles, the clavicle, shoulder complex, thoracic geometry, contact, and control.[^camargo2019] A figure that shows trapezius and rhomboids but omits serratus anterior would turn a posterior atlas into an incomplete mechanism.

### Neck and cranium: plausible continuation with visible variation

The archive's foot-to-cranium ambition should not be reduced to “calf to low back.” Posterior fascial, ligamentous, and muscular relations do continue through thoracic and cervical regions. A dissection study of 40 cadavers described a vertebral aponeurosis deep to latissimus dorsi that attached to thoracic spinous processes and rib angles, related inferiorly to thoracolumbar structures, and blended superiorly with deep cervical fascia.[^loukas2008] Willard and colleagues likewise describe posterior fascial and aponeurotic continuity toward the cranial base.[^willard2012]

The endpoint is variable. De La Paz and colleagues dissected 20 cadavers from New Zealand and Thailand and found direct cranial attachment of upper trapezius in six; most attached proximally only to the nuchal ligament in their sample.[^delapaz2023] This does not negate a posterior neck–cranial system. It prevents a textbook line from being drawn as uniform anatomy in every person. The atlas should show nuchal ligament, deep cervical fascia, selected intrinsic posterior neck muscles, entheses, and craniovertebral joints rather than making trapezius alone complete the route.

## Current state of continuity and transfer evidence

Two systematic reviews from 2016 establish the core distinction. Wilke and colleagues reviewed 62 human dissection studies and reported strong morphological support for all examined transitions in the proposed superficial back line, back functional line, and front functional line, while naming functional relevance as the urgent next question.[^wilke2016] Krause and colleagues reviewed nine human dissection and in-vivo studies and found evidence that tension can transfer across some adjacent myofascial connections, while emphasizing heterogeneity, anatomical variation, and limited evidence for functional significance.[^krause2016]

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis brings the remote-stiffness literature current through its search. Across 15 healthy-adult studies, Kretschmer and Wilke reported nonlocal stiffness increases during local lengthening or contraction within proposed chains. They rated certainty very low for lengthening and moderate for contraction; methods and protocols were heterogeneous, reporting bias was suspected, and only eight studies monitored unwanted muscle activity with electromyography.[^kretschmer2026] Remote stiffness is a legitimate measurement endpoint. It does not by itself identify the carrier, distinguish passive transfer from active control, quantify force through a whole route, or establish health or treatment effects.

The evidence therefore supports this exact hierarchy:

```text
documented component and attachment
    -> documented adjacent interface or regional continuity
    -> coordinated change during a declared task
    -> measured bounded effect across a named regional relation
    -> mechanically consequential whole-posterior transfer for a named task and carrier
```

The hierarchy is not automatically cumulative. Coordinated change may occur without direct connective transfer, and a measured regional effect may use a carrier that differs from the drawn chain. Embodied continuity (`EMB`) belongs beside the ladder as a provenance label that can motivate a hypothesis; it is not a substitute rung for `MEAS`.

## Continuity-evidence ladder for Chapter 7

| Level | Required statement | Minimum evidence | Authorized language | Prohibited shortcut |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Named structures | the structures exist and have specified attachments | current anatomical terminology and regional anatomy | “includes,” “attaches,” “arises,” “inserts” | “therefore carries force to” |
| 2. Adjacent relation | structures share a direct attachment, interface, aponeurosis, joint, fascial relation, or skeletal junction | dissection, histology, or validated imaging | “is continuous with,” “blends with,” “shares an interface,” with exact relation | “one cable” or “uninterrupted force path” |
| 3. Coordinated participation | several regions change during one task or perturbation | motion, activation, stiffness, timing, or control measurements | “participated,” “changed concurrently,” “was associated under this task” | “force traveled through the line” |
| 4. Bounded regional transfer | a mechanical effect is measured across a named connection | controlled perturbation, force, deformation, stiffness, or validated model with alternatives | “transmitted tension under this setup,” “changed the specified endpoint” | ordinary whole-body magnitude or clinical importance |
| 5. Whole-posterior transfer | consequential foot-to-cranium transfer is demonstrated for a named quantity, carrier, direction, task, and state | simultaneous multiregional measurements and a model that distinguishes competing carriers | future claim matched to the actual measurement | inference from adjacency, remote range of motion, felt continuity, or an uninterrupted illustration |

## Counterexamples, nulls, and falsifiers

| Tempting inference | Counterexample or alternative | Drafting consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Achilles and plantar fascia form one unbroken cable | morphometric and superficial periosteal relations were present, but complete fiber continuity was not confirmed in the studied feet | show the calcaneus as a carrier-changing junction, not a gap to hide |
| Every posterior structure has the same attachment pattern | upper-trapezius cranial attachment varied markedly in a 20-cadaver sample | include variation and avoid one canonical endpoint |
| Large muscles prove posterior primacy | vastus lateralis is also exceptionally large by lower-limb architectural measures; task contribution depends on geometry and state | state the metric and preserve nonposterior comparators |
| The thoracolumbar fascia is a posterior-only sheet | abdominal aponeuroses, transversus abdominis, obliques, quadratus-lumborum relations, and paraspinal sheaths participate | show the region as a girdling junction with anterior/lateral partners |
| Posterior scapular muscles explain scapular support alone | serratus anterior and clavicular/shoulder relations are essential partners | include the partner even in a posterior-focused atlas |
| Simultaneous distant motion proves fascial force transfer | neural control, linked-segment dynamics, external contact, and pressure can coordinate regions | label coordinated participation separately from transfer |
| Cadaver traction proves ordinary living magnitude | dissection, imposed 10-N traction, absent active control, and altered boundaries constrain the result | report setup and do not export magnitude |
| A remote stiffness change isolates one chain | active contraction, measurement location, connective routes, joint mechanics, and unwanted activity can contribute | state endpoint and uncertainty; do not call stiffness a transported force |
| A continuous atlas is more explanatory than regional anatomy | the grouping may add no prediction beyond named regional components | retain a null model in which “posterior chain” is pedagogically useful but mechanically no better than regional linked-system analysis |

The whole-posterior hypothesis is weakened when a proposed transition lacks a reproducible anatomical relation; when a predicted regional effect is absent under a task that should reveal it; when magnitude becomes negligible after active control and external contacts are accounted for; or when a simpler linked-segment, regional-fascia, pressure, or control model explains the observations equally well. The anatomical system claim survives such a result if its nested scopes remain accurate and useful; the stronger transfer claim must narrow.
