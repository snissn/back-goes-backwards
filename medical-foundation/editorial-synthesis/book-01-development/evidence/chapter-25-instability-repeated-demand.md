## Editorial decision

Chapter 25 can recover the archive's recurrent-instability program through one positive claim:

> A later demand acts on the state left by earlier demand, response, recovery, adaptation, learning, care, and exposure. Recurrent events therefore should not be represented as identical forces repeatedly applied to an unchanged body.

That claim is stronger than “many factors matter.” It gives time a specific mechanical job. Passive restraint, joint relation, swelling, pain, sensory information, activation, task strategy, confidence, assistance, participation, conditioning, and exposure can each change between events. Those changes can alter the next task without moving in one direction. A first event may leave lower capacity, successful adaptation, useful protection, an unresolved state, or no important retained change.

Recurrent lateral ankle instability is the lead because the literature permits three commonly fused objects to be compared directly:

1. passive or material restraint under a declared test;
2. relation maintenance and recovery during a declared task; and
3. the person's report of giving way, difficulty, confidence, pain, or function.

Those objects sometimes correspond and sometimes do not. Prospective first-sprain cohorts also provide an imperfect bridge from an earlier state to a later outcome. The chapter should use that combination to build a source-grounded multistudy reconstruction. It must not imply that one published cohort supplied a complete continuous record, that the research label identifies one mechanism, or that a measured difference predicts an individual outcome.

Lumbar segmental control remains the required axial transfer. The transfer asks whether the same distinctions survive when passive motion, active control, symptom report, and radiographic relation are measured differently and when “instability” lacks a single accepted definition. It does not make an ankle mechanism into a spinal mechanism.

The chapter's state-transition record is:

```text
state before task n
    + task, environment, support, and exposure n
    -> task behavior and internal distribution n
    -> success, perturbation, giving way, injury, or no event
    -> material, sensory, motor, experiential, and behavioral response
    -> recovery, adaptation, retained change, or unresolved state
    -> state before task n+1
```

Every arrow remains evidence-state labeled. A documented first sprain is not continuous observation of the later route. A cross-sectional group difference is not a preinjury cause. A predictor is not a mechanism or prognosis. A proximal kinematic difference is not a tissue-load measurement. A response to support or rehabilitation is not proof of historical origin.

## Operational distinctions

### Instability is not one object

The International Ankle Consortium created research-selection criteria because participant definitions had varied enough to impede comparison across studies.[^gribble2014] Hertel and Corbett's later model organizes primary injury, pathomechanical, sensory-perceptual, motor-behavioral, personal, environmental, interaction, and outcome components, while explicitly describing the model as theoretical.[^hertel2019] These sources establish a heterogeneous research domain. They do not prove that every listed component occurs in every person or that the model's arrows are causal.

Chapter 25 should use the following narrower terms:

| Object | Working definition | Evidence form | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material or passive restraint state | displacement, rotation, stiffness, or resisting relation under a declared passive test and direction | instrumented arthrometry, imaging, stress test, or other specified measure | task success, perceived giving way, recurrence, pain source, or tissue-load history |
| Task-level relation maintenance | whether a named contact or segment relation remains or returns within a declared tolerance during a named demand | kinematics, kinetics, force, pressure, timing, perturbation, performance, and recovery record | passive ligament state, subjective meaning, historical origin, or diagnosis |
| Perceived instability | reported giving way, uncertainty, difficulty, loss of confidence, or instability under stated conditions | person report with instrument, timeframe, task, and scoring context | ligament behavior, joint motion, motor-control mechanism, or recurrence by itself |
| Recurrent sprain | a later injury event under a declared event definition, observation window, and exposure context | prospective or retrospective event record | that the event reproduced the first mechanism or arose from persistent instability |
| Pain-related or threat-related protection | a changed task, motion, activation, support, or exposure after pain, fear, expectation, or prior experience | dated report, observation, experiment, or longitudinal record | whether the original injury was mechanical, whether protection is harmful, or whether it should be removed |
| Capable variability | different strategies that preserve the task relation and acceptable exposure | repeated task observation with success and exposure criteria | equal energetic cost or equal local distribution |

The chapter may use *chronic ankle instability* when reporting a source's cohort. It should not let the label substitute for that source's eligibility criteria, injury history, report, task, measure, or timeframe.

### Mechanical, functional, and perceived remain source-dependent labels

“Mechanical instability” can refer to passive laxity, arthrokinematic change, altered restraint, or a clinical category. “Functional instability” has been used for giving way, disability, sensorimotor deficit, or task failure. Because those usages vary, reader prose should usually name the measured object instead of importing a label.

Steinberg and colleagues illustrate the value of this discipline. They assessed 364 prerecruitment male infantry soldiers and separated perceived instability, mechanically examined instability, recurrent sprains, and their combinations into eight subgroups. The prevalence and neuromuscular findings differed across combinations.[^steinberg2024] This cross-sectional study does not establish that one impairment caused another. Its important contribution is structural: the combinations exist and should remain representable.

### Hard and soft are roles inside a declared relation

The archive's hard/soft distinction is motivated by TCM hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts without being definitionally identical to them. In this chapter, a shape-preserving role maintains a boundary, span, contact, alignment, or relation against a selected demand. A form-receiving role yields, conforms, accommodates, contains, slides, cushions, or permits transfer under that demand.

These are relational roles, not permanent tissue classes. Bone and articular geometry may take a prominent shape-preserving role for one direction. A ligament may preserve a relation while deforming. Muscle and tendon may stiffen, transmit, yield, or decelerate. Cartilage receives and redistributes contact while bearing load. Ground, shoe, brace, and handrail can alter which structures preserve or receive form. Chapter 25 can use this vocabulary to ask where relation-preserving demand is resolved. It cannot infer a preferred posterior organization, a fixed hard/soft taxonomy, or a TCM channel mapping.

## The lead is a multistudy reconstruction, not a hidden case report

No source in this packet measures, in one person and continuously, the first injury, passive restraint, sensory state, task behavior, internal exposure, perceived instability, proximal redistribution, every recovery interval, total task exposure, and later recurrence. The opening must disclose that it combines bounded observations from several designs.

The reconstruction compares four evidence families:

1. **cohort-definition evidence** establishes what a study meant by first sprain, coper, perceived instability, recurrent sprain, or CAI;
2. **correspondence evidence** asks whether passive, task, and experiential measures occur together;
3. **prospective evidence** asks whether selected earlier measures relate to a later defined outcome; and
4. **task evidence** asks how contact, motion, force, moment, activation, and recovery differ under one laboratory demand.

The reader should see the seams. A “coper” in one study is not guaranteed to match a coper in another. A landing task is not walking. A passive inversion measure is not an anterior translation measure. A twelve-month group classification is not an injury event. Each result keeps its population, task, comparison, and uncertainty.

## Passive restraint, task behavior, and experience do not collapse

### Passive difference can coexist with capable function

Brown, Rosen, and Ko compared sixteen recreationally active participants with CAI, sixteen copers, and sixteen controls using an instrumented inversion stress test. The CAI group had greater inversion than controls but did not differ significantly from copers; stiffness did not differ among groups.[^brown2015] The positive and null results belong together. Under that test, passive inversion separated CAI from controls but not CAI from people who had recovered enough to be classified as copers.

The result does not prove that laxity is irrelevant or that copers and CAI have identical ligament states. It does establish a real noncorresponding cell:

```text
selected passive inversion finding
    + capable self-reported outcome in coper group
    -> passive finding is not sufficient to define the later state
```

### Task or neural difference can occur without measured laxity difference

Bowker and colleagues compared thirty-seven participants with CAI, thirty copers, and twenty-six controls. Soleus spinal-reflex excitability differed, while instrumented anterior-posterior and inversion-eversion laxity did not differ among groups.[^bowker2016] This is a complementary noncorrespondence: the selected neural measure differed without the selected passive measure doing so.

The study was cross-sectional. It cannot show whether the reflex difference preceded the first injury, followed it, reflects participation or training, causes giving way, or predicts recurrence. It also cannot turn one H-reflex measure into “proprioception.” Its legitimate job is to prevent the absence of passive group difference from becoming evidence that every task-control process is the same.

### Perceptual outcomes can distinguish groups when a selected mechanical measure does not

Wikstrom and colleagues compared twenty-four copers with twenty-four people classified with CAI. Self-reported outcomes had the strongest ability to distinguish the groups, selected force-plate stabilization indices had lower discriminating accuracy, and sagittal talar position had poor utility.[^wikstrom2012] The paper's cutoff calculations are not reader-facing diagnostic rules. The important result is that a person's report, a selected sensorimotor task, and one structural-position measure performed different jobs.

De Vries and colleagues provide an additional null. Fifteen healthy participants, fourteen people after a primary inversion injury, and twenty-three people with CAI differed on functional ankle scores but not on the selected static-balance measures; balance measures did not correlate with the functional scores.[^devries2010] This does not discredit balance as a domain. It shows that a generic claim such as “instability is impaired balance” is too coarse.

### Reported instability can remain when whole-curve landing kinematics do not differ

De Ridder and colleagues compared twenty-eight participants with self-reported CAI and twenty-eight matched controls during barefoot forward and side jump landings. Whole-curve hip, knee, and ankle kinematics did not differ significantly, while the CAI group reported greater instability during both tasks and greater difficulty during the forward jump.[^deridder2015] This is one of the chapter's principal nulls.

The null is not universal. It is specific to the selected tasks, kinematic model, sample, and statistical power. Yet the result makes a firm editorial demand: reader prose cannot convert perceived instability into an unobserved lower-limb kinematic deviation, and it cannot dismiss the reported experience because that deviation was not detected.

## Time-resolved behavior can add information without becoming a mechanism

A peak angle compresses a waveform into one value. That can be useful, but it can miss when and how a relation changes. Watanabe and colleagues studied thirty-two people with CAI during a single-leg drop landing. Principal-component scores representing aspects of pre- and post-contact ankle-angle waveforms correlated with perceived-instability score, whereas the study's peak-angle summaries did not identify the same relations.[^watanabe2024] This supports the chapter's preference for an event-aligned time record over one static or peak descriptor.

The result remains cross-sectional and within one selected cohort. Principal components are mathematical summaries of observed covariance, not anatomical modes or causes. A correlation between waveform pattern and a report does not establish that changing the waveform would change giving way, prevent injury, or restore a preferred route.

The draft may therefore make the following bounded contrast:

| Compression | What it preserves | What it may lose | Permitted conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static posture or passive test | selected relation under selected conditions | task transition, perturbation, timing, adaptation | one state measurement |
| Peak angle or force | one extreme value and phase if reported | trajectory, order, duration, covariance | selected maximum, not whole control strategy |
| Whole waveform | time-varying selected quantity | unmeasured internal variables and causal order | richer task description |
| Principal component | dominant covariance pattern in the sampled data | direct physical interpretation and individual mechanism | compact association, not a force line or causal mode |

## A first event can alter the later state without fixing its direction

### The prospective bridge is real and incomplete

Doherty and colleagues recruited eighty-two people after a first-time lateral ankle sprain. Seventy-one completed the six-month follow-up, and seventy completed the twelve-month follow-up and entered the final prospective analysis; twenty-eight were classified with CAI and forty-two as copers. At two weeks, inability to complete two jump/landing tasks predicted the twelve-month CAI classification with 83% sensitivity and 55% specificity and correctly classified 67.6% of cases. At six months, a set of reach-task variables and self-reported function correctly classified 84.8% with 75% sensitivity and 91% specificity.[^doherty2016]

This study supports temporal order: selected early and intermediate observations preceded the later classification. It does not establish one causal pathway. Task noncompletion can reflect pain, swelling, caution, instruction, strength, confidence, care, morphology, or other variables. The six-month model is nearer the twelve-month outcome and partly includes a self-reported variable related to the later classification. Model performance in the sampled cohort is not an individual prognosis or an authorization to reproduce its tasks as a screen.

The chapter should use the study as a diagram of changing observability:

```text
documented first sprain
    -> two-week ability or inability under selected tasks
    -> six-month task and self-report state
    -> twelve-month research classification

observed time order: yes
complete mechanism: no
continuous exposure and recovery record: no
individual prediction or care rule: no
```

### Recurrence is not selected by one variable

Pourkazemi and colleagues followed seventy controls and thirty participants with an index sprain monthly for twelve months; ninety-six completed follow-up and ten sustained a sprain. A multivariable model included prior sprain status, age, body dimensions, perceived instability, laxity, balance, and inversion/eversion power, but only recent index sprain and younger age were independent predictors in the reported model.[^pourkazemi2018] The small number of events relative to candidate predictors makes the result exploratory and potentially unstable. The study did not supply a task-exposure denominator, so it cannot distinguish recurrent opportunity from altered capacity.

Its value is not the reported classification percentage. The value is that recurrence can reflect a combination of prior event, age, exposure opportunity, perception, passive state, performance, and other unmeasured circumstances. A mechanically interesting variable can belong in the history without independently determining the later event.

### Exposure is part of the denominator

Neither a symptom-free interval nor a lack of recurrence automatically means the same capacity under the same demand. A person may return to demanding activity, reduce exposure, use external support, change sport or work, avoid a movement, gain conditioning, or simply encounter fewer perturbations. Hertel and Corbett explicitly include the possibility of an asymptomatic outcome produced by reduced activity rather than full recovery.[^hertel2019]

Chapter 25 should therefore record at least:

- task class and environment;
- frequency, duration, intensity, variability, and perturbation opportunities;
- participation and assistance;
- time at risk;
- any changed care, support, equipment, surface, or role; and
- whether exposure was observed, reported, estimated, or unknown.

This is not an exposure score. It is the minimum needed to avoid comparing event counts without opportunity.

## Coping and compensation begin as successful outcomes

The coper group is analytically important because it shows that prior injury need not determine one future state. Copers are not one known mechanism and should not be romanticized as mechanically optimal. They are source-defined participants who, despite a sprain history, met a selected favorable outcome definition.

Mohamadi and colleagues compared twenty-five people in each of CAI, coper, and healthy-control groups during single-leg standing with visual and cognitive conditions. The CAI group showed greater center-of-pressure sway and longer cognitive-task reaction times, while copers did not differ from healthy controls on the reported postural-control pattern.[^mohamadi2020] The study's interpretation of “successful compensatory” control is plausible, but the cross-sectional design did not observe the compensation develop.

The reader-facing lesson is narrower:

> A changed state after injury can resolve into capable task performance. When a source labels that outcome compensation, the chapter should still ask what changed, what was preserved, and whether any cost was actually measured.

The hard/soft vocabulary can help describe the local task without deciding its value. Passive restraints, articular geometry, active muscle-tendon stiffness, ground contact, and external support may share shape-preserving work. Compliant tissues, changing contacts, and controlled motion receive and accommodate the demand. A coper outcome could reflect recovery of material behavior, an alternate active strategy, changed exposure, external support, learning, or several of these. The source set does not select one universal route.

## Proximal change is a test, not an automatic chain

### Positive proximal observations

Gribble and Robinson compared nineteen participants with unilateral CAI and nineteen controls during repeated jump landings. The CAI group showed less knee flexion at initial contact and reduced anteroposterior dynamic stability on the involved side.[^gribble2009] Doherty and colleagues compared twenty-eight people classified with CAI and forty-two copers one year after first-time lateral ankle sprain; the CAI group used greater hip flexion during descent and differed in selected hip energetics during a single-leg drop landing.[^dohertylanding2016]

These are legitimate proximal observations. They show that a local injury-history group can differ at another joint during the same task. They do not show that ankle dysfunction *caused* the proximal pattern, that the proximal pattern was harmful, or that internal tissue exposure rose.

### Motion without selected load difference

Terada, Pietrosimone, and Gribble compared nineteen people with CAI and nineteen controls during a vertical stop jump. The CAI group had less knee flexion at the instant of peak calculated anterior tibial shear force, but peak anterior tibial shear force and the other reported biomechanical variables did not differ between groups.[^terada2014] This is the chapter's required scale-control result:

```text
proximal kinematic difference
    does not automatically imply
group difference in the selected calculated proximal load
```

The study did not measure ACL strain or future knee injury. Its modeled or calculated shear quantity carries assumptions. Still, the positive kinematic result cannot be promoted into an observed tissue-load or injury-risk result when the selected force comparison was null.

### A declared carrier is required at every scale

To advance from ankle to knee, hip, contralateral limb, or trunk, the chapter must name:

1. the shared task and event phase;
2. the changed local contact, force, moment, work, motion, or activation;
3. the proximal quantity measured under the same task;
4. the hypothesized carrier between scales;
5. chronology or manipulation capable of testing direction;
6. a receiver-specific exposure if harm is proposed; and
7. a result that would stop the chain.

The route stops when only group membership, visible motion, or a generic “kinetic chain” remains. It can restart in later research if an intermediate is measured.

## Repetition is a branching state transition

The state-transition model should never be drawn as a downward staircase. Each event opens several branches:

| Between-event process | Possible next state | Required observation | Common overclaim to prevent |
|---|---|---|---|
| material healing or retained laxity | restored, altered, or unresolved passive restraint | direction-specific repeated measure | every sprain permanently loosens the ankle |
| pain, swelling, or tissue response | resolved, fluctuating, or retained receiver state | dated symptom/finding/response record | symptom proves instability or damage accumulation |
| task practice and conditioning | improved, alternate, or unchanged task control | repeated matched task and exposure | different strategy is automatically compensation or correction |
| confidence, fear, and expectation | changed participation, attention, support, or strategy | report plus behavior and timeframe | psychological state is noise or the sole cause |
| care, brace, footwear, or environment | altered boundary condition and opportunity | dated intervention/support and task comparison | response reveals original cause |
| exposure change | greater, lower, or different event opportunity | task denominator and time at risk | recurrence count equals declining capacity |
| new acute event | new material and experiential state | event record and chronology | every later event is the old mechanism recurring |
| ordinary development or health change | different capacity and strategy | relevant biological/context record | all change came from the ankle history |

A later event can be mechanically continuous with the earlier state, partly continuous, or mechanistically new. The chapter earns the word *recurrent* by preserving event identity and chronology, not by declaring that the same internal route recurred.

## Competing histories and principal nulls

The lead reconstruction must compare at least these histories:

1. **retained passive-restraint path:** passive state differs, corresponds with selected task difficulty, and precedes recurrence;
2. **task-control path:** passive state does not distinguish groups, but time-resolved task behavior does;
3. **perceptual or behavioral path:** report and participation differ while the selected passive and kinematic measures do not;
4. **successful recovery path:** prior injury is followed by capable function under relevant exposure;
5. **exposure-led path:** later events track opportunity, new acute circumstance, or task change better than retained local state;
6. **mixed path:** material, control, pain, confidence, environment, and care change one another over time; and
7. **no-added-value path:** the proposed routing record does not discriminate the selected outcome beyond the existing account.

The principal nulls are not generic caveats. They are observed or explicitly testable results:

- passive inversion differed from controls but not copers in Brown's study;
- laxity did not differ while a selected reflex measure did in Bowker's study;
- talar position had poor discrimination in Wikstrom's comparison;
- static balance did not distinguish groups or correlate with functional scores in de Vries's sample;
- whole-curve landing kinematics did not differ despite greater reported instability in De Ridder's sample;
- a proximal knee-angle difference did not correspond to a group difference in peak calculated anterior tibial shear force in Terada's study; and
- prospective models left substantial uncertainty, used selected outcomes, and did not establish unique causal routes.

The draft must not convert “not statistically significant” into proof of equality. It should report that the selected study did not detect a difference, preserve uncertainty and precision where material, and ask whether the null stops the proposed arrow.

## Axial transfer — lumbar segmental control

### What transfers

The ankle reconstruction supplies questions rather than thresholds:

- What relation is proposed to be maintained?
- Under what task, direction, phase, and tolerance?
- What passive behavior was measured?
- What active motion or recovery was measured?
- What did the person report?
- Which finding or symptom is the endpoint?
- What changed over time?
- What exposure and recovery occurred between observations?
- Which arrows are measured, calculated, inferred, or hypothesized?
- What result would let the account stop?

### What does not transfer

Ankle ligaments, inversion tests, giving-way definitions, balance tasks, landing metrics, research cutoffs, cohort labels, and recurrence rates do not become lumbar variables. A lumbar “segment” is embedded in a multisegment trunk, rib cage, pelvis, muscles, fascia, abdominal pressure system, neural control system, and environment. Passive translation or angulation, movement timing, bracing, symptom report, and function are different objects.

### Passive motion, active recovery, and symptom correspondence remain separate

Iguchi and colleagues measured L4–L5 sagittal translation and angulation on flexion-extension radiographs in 1,090 outpatients with low-back or leg pain. Translation of at least three millimeters was associated with worse activity-limitation scores, while angulation alone did not show the same relation; combined translation and angulation corresponded with the lowest scores in their comparisons.[^iguchi2004] This is an observational symptomatic clinical sample. The thresholds were study definitions, not universal truths, and the design does not establish that radiographic motion caused symptoms.

Fritz, Piva, and Childs studied forty-nine people referred for flexion-extension imaging because lumbar instability was suspected. Their clinical variables were evaluated against radiographic criteria rather than against a direct ground truth of task-level control or symptom origin.[^fritz2005] The study can show correspondence with that selected comparator. It cannot merge clinical examination, radiographic motion, and functional instability.

Mok and Hodges provide a different kind of evidence. Healthy participants recovered from unexpected support-surface perturbations with and without a rigid lumbar corset. Restricting lumbar motion increased center-of-pressure excursion, recovery time, and number of adjustments, particularly under larger perturbations.[^mok2013] This experiment supports a causal arrow under its conditions: an imposed boundary condition that reduced lumbar motion changed postural recovery. It does not establish a spinal disease, instability diagnosis, chronic bracing mechanism, or treatment inference. It also warns against defining stability as minimal movement; available lumbar movement contributed to recovery.

Current reviews find substantial variation in lumbar-instability definitions and diagnostic criteria.[^elmose2023] A 2026 review similarly concluded that no single clinical test, imaging threshold, or questionnaire supplies a complete definition.[^gonugunta2026] Reviews organize the disagreement; they do not validate a combined diagnostic framework for this book.

The bounded transfer is therefore:

```text
selected passive segment relation
    != task-level postural recovery
    != person's symptom or instability report
    != cause of pain or disability

but

changing an available segment motion can alter task recovery
under experimentally declared conditions
```

That result preserves the archive's axial-control interest without importing ankle certainty or treating a straighter or less mobile spine as inherently more stable.

## Evidence-state chain and stopping rules

For every worked branch, the draft should complete only the links the source set can support:

```text
event or prior state
    -> passive/material state
    -> task-specific behavior
    -> local internal distribution
    -> receiver exposure
    -> material, sensory, motor, or experiential response
    -> finding, symptom, function, participation, or later event
```

Most studies in this packet begin partway through that chain. The following rules prevent a persuasive chronology from filling the gaps:

- **Observed event:** establish who recorded the event, its definition, task, timing, and exposure window.
- **Measured state:** name the instrument, quantity, direction, site, reference, uncertainty, and time since the event.
- **Calculated quantity:** disclose inputs and assumptions; a net moment or shear estimate is not a tissue measurement.
- **Modeled route:** state boundary, validation domain, parameter sensitivity, and nonunique alternatives.
- **Reconstructed transition:** disclose that separate observations have been joined and identify missing intervals.
- **Hypothesized arrow:** name a disfavoring result before using the arrow to motivate research.
- **Clinical endpoint:** keep pain, report, function, diagnosis, recurrence, participation, and valued outcome distinct.

The account stops when:

- the proposed states do not correspond under the declared measure;
- chronology is absent and competing direction is equally plausible;
- a local difference has no identified carrier to the next scale;
- the selected internal exposure or receiver response does not differ;
- recurrence is better represented by changed exposure or a new event;
- the proposed record does not add discrimination, communication value, or a useful research question; or
- another medical, biological, contextual, or personal concern has priority for the declared endpoint.

A stop is not an assertion that mechanics is unreal. It is the supported boundary of the current account.

## Measurement and disfavoring program

| Question | Minimum record | Evidence that supports the interpretation | Result that lowers its priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Did passive restraint change? | direction, load/test condition, displacement/rotation/stiffness, time, reliability | repeated or comparative material behavior under matched conditions | no detected change, poor reliability, or same finding in capable copers |
| Was the task relation unstable? | task, event, tolerance, departure, recovery, repetition | relation repeatedly leaves or fails to return under relevant demand | relation remains controlled or alternate strategy succeeds without selected cost |
| Was there discoordination? | event marker, participant signals, timing definition, repeatability, consequence | timing relation predicts task departure or measured exposure | timing varies without task consequence or signal is unreliable |
| Did reported instability correspond? | exact report, timeframe, task, state measure | within-person or prospective relation under stated conditions | report persists while proposed state is absent, or state exists without report |
| Did an earlier event change later state? | dated injury, intermediate states, exposure, recovery, later task/outcome | repeated measurement establishes temporal order and plausible intermediates | state recovers, adaptation dominates, missing interval is decisive, or new event explains change |
| Did compensation preserve a function? | changed strategy, protected task/outcome, comparator | success improves or is maintained under changed demand | strategy changes without improving declared task or was not needed |
| Did protection impose a local cost? | local force, moment, pressure, work, fatigue, receiver exposure, time | selected cost rises and corresponds prospectively with response | cost does not rise, is transient, or remains within capacity |
| Did the route propagate proximally? | same task; local and proximal quantities; carrier; chronology | local manipulation or temporal change predicts proximal quantity | proximal change absent, nonspecific, precedes local state, or selected load remains unchanged |
| Did the route explain recurrence? | event denominator, exposure, capacity, chronology, alternatives | prospective calibrated model with external validation and useful consequence | model does not generalize, exposure dominates, or intervention on mediator does not change outcome |
| Does the lumbar transfer hold? | region-specific passive, active, task, symptom, and time variables | experimentally or prospectively supported lumbar relation | ankle analogy is doing the work or definitions remain noncorresponding |

No item in this table is a reader self-test, clinical examination sequence, return-to-activity screen, or diagnostic battery.

## Evidence gaps before publication

### Cohort and construct gaps

- Confirm every ankle cohort's inclusion, exclusion, event, coper, recurrence, and timeframe definitions from full text rather than relying on abstract shorthand.
- Review the use of *material laxity*, *task-level instability*, *perceived instability*, *giving way*, and *recurrence* with foot-and-ankle clinicians and measurement specialists.
- Avoid transporting findings from young active research samples, male military cohorts, or referred spine populations to the general public without explicit limits.
- Preserve disability, assistance, pain, confidence, and altered participation as meaningful states rather than treating departure from unassisted athletic performance as failure.

### Temporal and causal gaps

- No source continuously observes the complete injury-to-recovery-to-recurrence path.
- Prospective cohorts measure selected snapshots and incompletely ascertain exposure, care, behavior, and intervening events.
- Cross-sectional CAI/coper differences can be causes, consequences, adaptations, selection effects, or common-cause results.
- Prediction models require external validation, calibration, decision-consequence analysis, and adequate event counts before any practical use.
- Intervention-response evidence would establish modifiability under tested conditions, not historical origin, unless a mediator and causal assumptions were separately supported.

### Mechanical and multiscale gaps

- Surface motion, center of pressure, net joint moment, EMG, and passive arthrometry measure different objects.
- No reviewed human study measures a complete local ankle internal load path and then a complete proximal receiver exposure in one recurrent history.
- The proximal literature contains both positive and null findings and substantial task dependence.
- Whole-body, contralateral, energetic, and systemic consequences remain research candidates rather than Chapter 25 conclusions.
- Skeletal-primary routing, preferred posterior support, all-Yang organization, signed dimensional modes, force-line mappings, pressure/breath drivers, bandhas, and torus dynamics remain later-book programs.

### Lumbar-transfer gaps

- Lumbar instability lacks one accepted construct or ground-truth measurement across clinical, radiographic, functional, and experiential domains.
- Passive translation, angulation, task motion, postural recovery, bracing, pain, and disability require separate source families.
- The corset experiment supports a bounded active-recovery relation in healthy people; it cannot establish chronic spinal instability or a therapeutic direction.
- The axial program remains important, but its formal mechanical model and preferred-configuration relations belong to Book II development.

### Publication and practice gaps

- No diagnostic rule, self-test, prognosis, recurrence threshold, brace selection, exercise, return-to-activity criterion, or treatment follows from this packet.
- Foot-and-ankle, spine, biomechanics, motor-control, pain-science, causal-method, rehabilitation, medical-coauthor, ethics, disability-language, and patient-centered review remain open.
- Source refresh, conflict-of-interest review, bibliography normalization, figure rights, caption, accessibility, and final-art gates remain open.

Independent source and salvage review has confirmed this packet's exact source claims and archive coverage. The Chapter 25 reader draft is authorized and must:

- open with superficially similar later events arising from measurably different states;
