# Chapter 5 evidence packet — skeleton, joints, and shape-preserving paths

**Unit:** `BGB-U-0005` — *Skeleton, Joints, and Shape-Preserving Paths*

**Status:** source-grounded development packet as of 2026-07-11; sufficient for a controlled first draft; anatomy, biomechanics, medical, and figure review remain publication gates; not reader prose and not an evidence-status upgrade

**Companion controls:** [Chapter 5 brief](../chapter-briefs/BGB-U-0005-skeleton-joints-shape-preserving-paths.md), `BGB-M-0001`, `BGB-M-0002`, `BGB-M-0004`, `BGB-M-0005`, `BGB-X-0001`, `BGB-Q-0009`, and `BGB-Q-0010`

## Editorial decision

Chapter 5 can be drafted around a bounded anatomical proposition:

> Bones, articular surfaces, and recruited restraints provide the body's clearest relatively shape-preserving architecture, but a usable skeletal route exists only through changing contact, active force, tensile support, pressure, and tissue cooperation.

This proposition keeps the archive's central insight—that skeletal geometry matters to how gross demand is organized—without treating bone as rigid, a joint as a fixed hinge, visible alignment as a verdict, or the skeleton as an autonomous chain. The chapter may use **skeletal-primary routing** as a declared first-order modeling assumption for gross gravitational and external structural demand. It may not present that assumption as an empirical conclusion established by Book I, and it may not use it to derive Book II's preferred configuration.

The external source base supports several narrower claims: bone deforms under ordinary activity; cortical bone behavior depends on loading direction and mode; joint loads and moments change with task; articular contact locations move with configuration; different ligament bundles change length differently through motion; and support conditions can materially alter measured joint forces. Those findings make the chapter's model plausible and testable. They do not establish a unique whole-body route, one optimal alignment, a universal posterior solution, or a medical consequence.

## Quantity and inference controls

Chapter 5 inherits Chapter 2's quantity stack and must not compress it into “load through the bones.” Each anatomical claim should identify at least the relevant level:

```text
external demand and contact
    -> segment force and moment balance
    -> joint resultant and articular contact state
    -> candidate component load share
    -> local stress, strain, pressure, or deformation
    -> tissue response, if separately supported
```

The first three levels can often be described or estimated without resolving the last three. A visible joint relation may change an external moment arm; that change may alter the active force needed by a selected model; those forces may change a joint resultant; and a contact model may then estimate distribution across an interface. None of those steps licenses direct inference from appearance to local tissue stress.

The chapter should use **route** for a time-bounded representation of carriers and interfaces, not for a substance moving intact through the body. At every boundary, force and moment balance apply to a newly selected system. Components may branch, cancel, reverse, or be supported by muscle, ligament, pressure, inertia, or an external surface.

## Source-grounded mechanical kernels

### Bone deforms in ordinary use

Lanyon and colleagues attached a strain-gauge rosette to the midshaft of one living person's tibia and recorded surface deformation during activities. The study is a direct existence proof that a load-bearing human bone is not rigid in use. It is not a population estimate and samples one surface region rather than the full three-dimensional strain field.[^lanyon1975]

**Permitted use:** bone can preserve task-relevant gross geometry while undergoing small, measurable deformation.

**Prohibited inference:** a strain measured at the tibial surface specifies the full internal load path, an ideal strain, tissue health, or a preferred posture.

### Cortical bone is direction- and mode-dependent

Reilly and Burstein tested compact bone in tension, compression, torsion, and off-axis loading and evaluated a transversely isotropic representation. The study supports direction- and load-mode dependence in compact bone rather than an isotropic rigid-strut picture.[^reilly1975] Mirzaali and colleagues later tested hydrated human cortical specimens under several load modes and related properties to composition, porosity, age, and sex in an elderly donor sample.[^mirzaali2016]

**Permitted use:** material response must be attached to tissue, scale, direction, mode, state, and experiment. A simple beam or rigid-segment model is a deliberate reduction.

**Prohibited inference:** the whole human body is orthotropic, three anatomical directions are therefore established, or material symmetry selects the later Yang directions. Orthotropy remains an optional constitutive approximation at a named tissue scale, not the chapter's organizing premise.

### Bone geometry can change over time

The Penn State Young Women's Health Study related longitudinal changes in femoral geometry and estimated strength to lean mass, sports participation, and sex-steroid measures over ten years.[^petit2004] The design supports a relationship among use, development, biology, and structural geometry. It does not isolate mechanical loading as a sole cause or prove that any observed geometry is optimal.

Laboratory fatigue work on human cortical bone shows that repeated cyclic loading can accumulate microdamage and change compliance under the tested conditions.[^zioupos1996] This is useful for explaining capacity and history, but it must not be converted into the archive's claim that ordinary postural asymmetry silently causes degeneration. Dose, stress state, recovery, tissue condition, and the gap between an in-vitro specimen and living repair remain decisive.

### Joint contact moves with configuration

Li and colleagues combined MRI-based knee models with dual-orthogonal fluoroscopy in five subjects during a quasi-static lunge. Tibiofemoral contact points moved in three dimensions through flexion, with different patterns on the medial and lateral plateaus.[^li2005] The finding directly defeats a fixed-pin or single-contact-point picture.

**Permitted use:** a skeletal route crosses a changing and distributed articular interface; configuration can alter which parts of an interface are engaged.

**Prohibited inference:** the measured contact point is the full contact patch, pressure field, stress field, or disease mechanism. The sample and task are bounded, and contact kinematics do not establish one healthy path.

### Joint force and moment components change with task

Kutzner and colleagues measured tibial-component contact forces and moments in five people with instrumented knee implants during daily activities. Resultant force magnitudes, shear components, and moments differed across walking, stair use, standing, sitting, and knee bending.[^kutzner2010] These data demonstrate that a joint cannot be assigned one generic “load” independent of task and phase.

Instrumented hip and knee implants in twelve older participants also showed that water depth, movement velocity, and added drag altered measured joint forces during aquatic and land activities.[^kutzner2017] Buoyancy reduced forces in some comparisons, while faster motion or added resistance could increase them. The study supplies an especially useful counterexample: a similar visible limb relation can coexist with different external support, active demand, and joint force.

**Limit:** these are selected post-arthroplasty cohorts. The chapter may use them to demonstrate task and support dependence, not to announce normal reference loads for every reader.

### Ligament recruitment is regional and configuration-dependent

Park and colleagues reconstructed collateral-ligament bundle lengths in five healthy knees during flexion. Different bundles lengthened, shortened, or changed little; the ligaments did not behave as uniform ropes.[^park2005] This supports a configuration-dependent recruitment picture but does not measure force directly.

A robotic cadaver study of a simulated pivot shift found that the relative force contributions of the ACL and anterolateral capsular regions changed with flexion angle.[^bell2018] This is a bounded force example under a specific provocative simulation, not evidence about quiet standing, ordinary carrying, or every ligament.

Together these studies permit a careful statement: ligamentous restraints can guide an articulation because different fibers and structures become mechanically relevant under different geometries and demands. They do not justify the archive's simpler assertion that ligaments are passive stabilizers forced to rescue every misaligned joint.

## Skeletal-primary routing as a controlled reduction

The author's first-order intuition is that gross structural demand should, to a useful approximation, be organized principally through the skeleton. Chapter 5 should state this openly and modestly. The model is useful because mineralized bones and articular geometry preserve gross relations more clearly than many neighboring tissues over ordinary task phases. It provides a tractable starting graph for gravitational and externally applied demand.

The reduction is not anatomical exclusion. A skeletal node or edge has no functioning route unless the model includes the contacts and forces that make it available. Muscle produces and redirects force. Tendon transmits it. Ligaments and capsules constrain motion. Cartilage and fluid share contact demand. Pressure can support shape. Fascia and sliding interfaces couple or separate regions. Ground, furniture, water, tools, and other people can alter external support. Chapter 6 develops those systems; Chapter 5 keeps them visible as unresolved terms.

### Minimum model record

For every linked skeletal trace, record:

1. the object, person, segment, joint, or contact system being isolated;
2. task phase and time scale;
3. global and local frames;
4. external contacts and support conditions;
5. segment masses, accelerations, and whether the treatment is static, quasi-static, or dynamic;
6. modeled active forces and restraints;
7. articular contact representation;
8. observed, measured, calculated, modeled, hypothesized, or embodied status of each quantity; and
9. at least one alternative route or force-sharing solution.

### Model survival tests

The skeletal-primary assumption remains productive in a selected example only when:

- the declared skeletal graph helps close force and moment balance;
- its nodes and contacts correspond to anatomical interfaces rather than a decorative line;
- changes in configuration yield testable changes in moment, contact, or candidate load sharing;
- omitted active, tensile, pressure, inertial, and support terms are named and bounded; and
- at least one observation could distinguish the proposed route from an alternative graph.

It should be weakened or abandoned for that example when the active or external-support terms dominate the selected outcome, balance cannot be closed, the inferred path is nonunique without additional measurement, or the graph adds no explanatory or predictive value beyond a conventional free-body diagram.

## Worked example program

### Primary trace — the recurring object transfer

Use `BGB-X-0001` from support through acceptance, departure, transport, and placement. The same event must be redrawn at more than one boundary:

1. **Object boundary:** gravity and support/contact forces; before departure, the original surface carries most of the object's weight.
2. **Person-plus-object boundary:** ground, support surface if retained, gravity, and acceleration; internal joint forces disappear from the external balance.
3. **Selected segment boundary:** neighboring joint resultants, muscle/tendon forces if modeled, gravity, inertia, and external contact.
4. **Articular boundary:** distributed contact and restraint rather than a frictionless pin.

The trace should make one conservative point: once the object leaves its support, gross demand must be reconciled across the person's contacts and linked segments. The skeleton provides an intelligible first graph, but the same external success may be achieved by different configurations and force-sharing strategies.

**Required variants:**

- two visibly different but viable transfer strategies, neither labeled correct;
- one support-modified variant, such as retaining contact with a table or receiving help; and
- one matched-looking configuration with a different acceleration or external support, demonstrating that appearance does not specify internal exposure.

### Joint-contact inset

Use the knee as a bounded illustration, not as the whole-body template. Draw a qualitative contact-location sequence through flexion based on Li et al. The figure must say **contact location**, not pressure or stress. It should show that medial and lateral contacts need not translate identically and that a route across an articulation changes with configuration.

### Ligament-guidance inset

Represent anterior, middle, and posterior collateral bundles through a few flexion states. Label the displayed variable as modeled bundle length. Add the sentence “length change is not force measurement.” If a force panel is used, keep the cadaveric pivot-shift experiment separate so the two evidence types are not fused.

### Stability comparison

Compare the same assembly under two tasks or perturbations. One configuration may preserve the selected relation under slow support but not a rapid perturbation; another may move more while recovering the task. Stability should be scored by declared task success, excursion, recovery, contact, and active response—not by stillness or symmetry.

## Alternatives and counterexamples

| Tempting inference | Counterexample or alternative | Drafting consequence |
|---|---|---|
| The same posture produces the same joint load | External support, acceleration, muscle co-contraction, and task can differ; aquatic and land comparisons alter measured joint forces | Never infer a joint force from appearance alone |
| The skeleton is a stack carrying gravity downward | Active force, tensile restraint, pressure, inertia, and external contacts create branches and moments | Draw a graph or free body, not one vertical arrow |
| Bone's hard role means rigidity | Living tibia deforms; cortical response is direction-, mode-, and state-dependent | Use *relatively shape-preserving* and name the scale/time |
| Joint position fixes contact mechanics | Contact locations migrate through motion, and distribution remains unresolved without further measurement/modeling | Separate position, contact location, pressure, stress, and response |
| Ligaments are passive end stops | Bundle lengths and force contributions change with configuration; muscle and capsule share stability | Use *recruited restraint* or *guidance* under stated conditions |
| Symmetry or neutral alignment is mechanically optimal | Multiple strategies can satisfy the same task, and internal states are underdetermined | Treat alignment descriptively; Book II owns preference |
| More stiffness means more stability | Excessive constraint may impair a needed freedom; movement can be part of recovery | Operationalize the task and perturbation |
| Repetition necessarily produces damage | Fatigue depends on stress state, cycles, tissue state, repair, and recovery | Keep fatigue evidence bounded; do not diagnose ordinary use |

## Falsifiers and stop rules

The chapter's model is weakened for a selected system when:

- skeletal geometry does not improve prediction of contact, moment, or task behavior beyond a simpler model;
- an asserted route cannot satisfy the declared force and moment balance;
- materially different internal solutions remain compatible with every available observation and are not disclosed;
- the proposed shape-preserving carrier does not preserve the named relation at the selected scale;
- a support, active-force, or pressure term omitted by the skeletal reduction changes the conclusion;
- a ligament-guidance statement relies only on visual position without recruitment, length, force, or constraint evidence;
- a contact-location observation is used as if it were pressure or stress; or
- “alignment” supplies the conclusion without an explicit frame, mechanical link, endpoint, and comparator.

## Draft and publication gates

### First-draft gate

- Keep the full claim chain visible wherever appearance or geometry approaches exposure.
- State skeletal-primary routing as an authorial first-order assumption at first use.
- Use at least two viable configurations and one support-modified variant.
- Recall Chapter 3's hard role in one sentence; do not re-teach the full polarity.
- Keep active, tensile, compliant, sliding, pressure, and external-support terms visible as unresolved contributors.
- Include one in-vivo bone-deformation example, one moving-contact example, and one ligament-recruitment example with their design limits.
- Do not infer health, pain, damage, treatment, or preferred configuration.

### Publication gate

- A musculoskeletal anatomist reviews articular and ligament descriptions.
- A biomechanics reviewer checks all free bodies, moment statements, contact terminology, and material-property scope.
- A medical reviewer confirms that examples remain descriptive rather than diagnostic or prescriptive.
- Figure review verifies that every visual distinguishes observed, measured, calculated, modeled, and hypothetical elements.
- Citation review checks the primary paper rather than relying only on abstracts for quantitative statements.
