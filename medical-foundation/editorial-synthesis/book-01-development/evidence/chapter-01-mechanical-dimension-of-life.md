# Chapter 1 evidence pack — the mechanical dimension of life

**Unit:** `BGB-U-0001`

**Status:** Source-grounded first-draft authorization; focused foundation pack rather than a systematic review

**Search date:** 2026-07-11

## Editorial decision

Chapter 1 may state directly that the human body is a living biomechanical system. The statement means that embodied biological activity always occurs through material structures under forces, moments, pressures, contacts, constraints, motion, and deformation. It does not mean that the body is a passive machine, that mechanics has causal priority in every health question, or that a visible configuration determines a local tissue state.

The chapter's strongest defensible opening is therefore:

> The human body is a materially embodied, actively regulated biomechanical system. Its tissues and structures continuously receive, generate, distribute, transform, transmit, oppose, and balance mechanical demand while storing, returning, or dissipating mechanical energy and managing pressure, contact, motion, and deformation over time.

This wording preserves the author's intended foundation without using *adapt* as the governing verb. Response, regulation, maintenance, accommodation, remodeling, injury, and recovery remain different possible processes that later chapters must name specifically.

The chapter can also establish three consequences of that starting point:

1. mechanical state and biological state can alter one another;
2. task-, segment-, interface-, tissue-, and cellular-scale descriptions are related but non-equivalent; and
3. the explanatory importance of mechanics must be assessed within biological, psychological, social, developmental, and environmental context.

The broader possibility that mechanical history sometimes matters upstream of a visible injury, symptom, or rehabilitation episode may be named as the book's motivating inquiry. It is not established by the foundational statement and cannot carry a disease claim in Chapter 1.

## First-draft disposition

The source set and limits below authorize a controlled reader-facing draft covering:

- material embodiment and ordinary mechanical conditions across biological scales;
- reciprocal relations between mechanical surroundings and living biological state;
- one bounded example of a mechanical input changing cellular or matrix behavior;
- one bounded example of an inflammatory exposure changing measured tissue mechanics;
- scale transitions as modeling and measurement problems rather than automatic causal bridges;
- a whole-person and environmental placement of mechanical reasoning; and
- a negative branch in which mechanical history is present but has little explanatory priority for the selected outcome.

The pack does **not** authorize:

- a claim that mechanical organization is the root of health or disease;
- one ideal posture, alignment, load path, posterior route, or efficiency state;
- a direct line from posture to inflammation, fatigue, circulation, immunity, autonomic regulation, pain, or chronic illness;
- generic claims that loading is beneficial, harmful, or adaptive;
- treating cell-culture or explant findings as whole-person clinical outcomes;
- treating the WHO ICF framework as evidence for a mechanical cause; or
- any diagnostic, preventive, corrective, or treatment recommendation.

## 1. Material embodiment without machine reduction

The opening claim is broad because it is prior to any particular disease or movement theory. Living tissues are not exempt from mechanics: internal cellular processes and external interactions can both generate stresses, and tissue behavior is organized across molecular, cellular, tissue, organ, and whole-person scales. Bonfanti and colleagues review living-tissue fracture as a multiscale process driven by stresses that may arise within tissue or be externally applied. Their subject is fracture, not general health, but the review supports the narrower premise that living material is mechanically active and exposed ([Bonfanti et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tcb.2022.01.005)).

Humphrey, Dufresne, and Schwartz describe soft connective tissue at steady state as dynamic. Resident cells sense extracellular-matrix mechanics and participate in matrix deposition, rearrangement, removal, organization, and prestress. Their review supports active mechanical reciprocity and homeostatic regulation at cell–matrix and tissue scales; it does not establish a single body-wide set point or prove the project's later preferred configuration ([Humphrey et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrm3896)).

The reader-facing distinction should be explicit:

- **Mechanical dimension:** material relations are continuously present and can be described.
- **Mechanical explanation:** a defined mechanical relation helps account for a selected state or outcome.
- **Mechanical priority:** that relation explains more than plausible alternatives for the selected question.

Only the first is established by embodiment alone.

## 2. Bidirectional mechanics–biology examples

### Mechanical surroundings changing biological behavior

Use one primary example in the chapter body and keep the second in a note or short comparison.

1. In an in-vitro study, rabbit aortic medial cells grown on elastin membranes were exposed to repeated elongation and relaxation. Under that protocol, cyclic stretching was associated with higher synthesis rates of collagen, hyaluronate, and chondroitin 6-sulfate than agitated or stationary preparations ([Leung, Glagov, and Mathews, 1976](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/128820/)). This supports a bounded statement: a specified mechanical history altered specified matrix-related cellular outputs in that preparation.
2. Discher, Janmey, and Wang synthesized quantitative work in which substrate elasticity influenced behaviors of several anchorage-dependent cell types. The source is useful for showing that “the environment” includes mechanical properties, but its cell and gel systems do not specify what a whole organ or person will do ([Discher, Janmey, and Wang, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16293750/)).

Neither source should be paraphrased as “load makes tissue healthy” or “cells adapt to load.” Direction, magnitude, rate, duration, material state, cell type, and measured response matter.

### Biological state changing mechanical behavior

Thompson and colleagues exposed bovine cartilage explants to interleukin-1 beta in vitro and reported matrix catabolism together with changes in measured stiffness and stress-relaxation behavior. Lithium chloride was the intervention under study, but Chapter 1 needs only the control relation: an inflammatory signaling environment and associated matrix change altered the explants' measured mechanical properties ([Thompson et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/jor.22913)).

The permissible inference is that the same externally described task or imposed deformation can meet a mechanically different tissue when biological state has changed. The experiment does not establish a human inflammatory condition, a clinical threshold, a symptom mechanism, or a treatment inference.

### Reciprocal rather than symmetric

The two directions need not be equally strong, equally fast, or mediated by the same variables. Feedback can be delayed, state-dependent, stabilizing, destabilizing, or negligible for the selected outcome. The chapter should use *reciprocal* to mean that influence is possible in both directions, not that every mechanical input receives a matching biological response.

## 3. Scale and boundary discipline

Guo and colleagues review multiscale models linking nonlinear, viscoelastic, anisotropic tissue behavior with cell-level mechanobiology. Their review is appropriate support for a methodological claim: tissue-scale behavior can emerge from hierarchical organization, while cell-scale signaling is a different explanatory level that requires an explicit bridge ([Guo et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0085025)). Bonfanti et al. give a second bounded example in which tissue fracture couples molecular adhesion, cellular behavior, and tissue-scale stress.

Chapter 1 should not teach the equations reserved for Chapter 2. It should show what is missing at each transition:

```text
task and environment
    -> body configuration and external contacts
    -> segment forces and moments
    -> joint, interface, or pressure distribution
    -> local stress, strain, pressure, or excursion
    -> tissue- and state-specific biological response
    -> possible function, finding, symptom, or outcome
```

Each arrow requires additional geometry, boundary conditions, active forces, material behavior, measurement, model assumptions, or biological evidence. The diagram is an audit path, not a prediction that every task travels to a health outcome.

## 4. Whole-person placement

The World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (`ICF`) treats functioning and disability across body structure and function, activity, participation, and environmental context. The WHO beginner's guide describes an integrated biopsychosocial model and places health conditions in interaction with personal and environmental factors ([WHO ICF](https://www.who.int/classifications/international-classification-of-functioning-disability-and-health); [WHO beginner's guide](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/classification/icf/icfbeginnersguide.pdf)).

Chapter 1 may use this framework to keep mechanical reasoning at the body, task, environment, and participation levels at once. It should not turn the framework into a ritual list or imply that it settles etiology. The following are candidate pathways and questions to test, not causal mappings supplied by the ICF:

| Context | Candidate node or relation it may alter |
|---|---|
| Development, aging, genetics, inflammation, healing, medication, sleep, and nutrition | Material state, active capacity, sensitivity, recovery, and reserve |
| Expectation, attention, fear, confidence, and prior experience | Task choice, motor strategy, perceived demand, symptom report, and participation |
| Work organization, caregiving, sport, transport, resources, and social conditions | Exposure history, rest, available assistance, and consequences of task failure |
| Built environment, terrain, tools, assistive technology, and accessibility | External demand, contacts, feasible movement options, and performance |
| Diagnosis and care history | Interpretation, behavior, treatment exposure, and current state |

The list is not a claim that each factor is relevant in every case or that any listed pathway has been established. Each belongs in a reader account only where separate evidence or observation supports its effect on a named node, arrow, comparison, or outcome.

## 5. Negative and low-priority branches

Chapter 1 must include at least one explicit result in which tracing mechanics does not become the main explanation. A suitable hypothetical comparison is a person carrying the same object on two days while an acute systemic illness substantially changes fatigue and performance. Mechanical demand remains present, and altered strength or behavior may change its distribution, but the selected question may be explained chiefly by the illness. In another question—how the object was supported—the mechanical account may still be primary. Priority therefore belongs to a question and outcome, not to the body in the abstract.

The chapter should also preserve reverse direction. Pain, inflammation, fatigue, fear, or disease can change movement and loading. A mechanically coherent pattern observed afterward may be a consequence, accommodation, or modifier rather than the initiating cause.

These branches do not weaken the project's mechanical inquiry. They define a framework capable of returning a low-priority or no-added-value result.

## 6. Example and figure decisions

### Recurring object transfer

Use the ordinary object-transfer task as the opening and closing scene. Chapter 1 may name gravity, support, contact, movement, active regulation, sensation, expectation, prior fatigue, and environment without calculating them. The teaching question is not the correct way to move the object. It is which descriptions would be needed to decide what mattered to a selected later outcome.

### Short supporting examples

- Use cell–matrix reciprocity as the principal bounded experimental example.
- Use the cartilage-explant study as the biological-state-to-mechanics counterdirection.
- Mention lung expansion or pulsatile vessels only as ordinary pressure-and-deformation examples, without adding quantitative, disease, or systemic-effect claims unless separately sourced.
- Use fracture only to contrast a discrete mechanical event with a cumulative or uncertain history; do not imply that all chronic presentations are smaller versions of fracture.

### Text-native figure

Reuse the nested scale logic of `BGB-FIG-01-002`, but add a Chapter 1 context layer rather than a new smooth causal arrow. The draft figure should show:

- mechanical state and biological state connected by two separately qualified arrows;
- task, segment, interface, tissue, and cellular scales as non-equivalent levels;
- whole-person and environmental factors entering named nodes; and
- a branch in which the selected mechanical history adds little explanatory value.

The caption must say that the figure organizes questions and does not establish a mechanical cause or one preferred route.
