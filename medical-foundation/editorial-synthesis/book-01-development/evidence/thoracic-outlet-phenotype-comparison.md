# Thoracic-outlet phenotype comparison: objective arterial versus thrombotic venous

**Control unit:** `BGB-U-0024`

**Decision addressed:** `BGB-DEC-0003` and `BGB-DEC-0004`

**Status:** Phenotype selection and source comparison accepted for internally controlled first-draft use; condition-specific medical and vascular review remains a publication gate

**Search date:** 2026-07-11

## Purpose and limits

This memo compares two possible leading phenotypes for Chapter 24:

1. **objective arterial thoracic outlet syndrome (aTOS)** involving a defined focal bony relation, objective subclavian-artery damage, and a distal arterial consequence; and
2. **thrombotic venous thoracic outlet syndrome (vTOS)** involving axillary-subclavian deep venous thrombosis attributed to the thoracic outlet, often called Paget-Schroetter syndrome or effort thrombosis.

The comparison asks which phenotype can carry the chapter's first complete mechanical-to-clinical evidence chain while keeping observation, reconstruction, diagnosis, and generalization separate. It is not a systematic review, prevalence estimate, diagnostic guide, or treatment review. Treatment studies enter only where they reveal anatomy, retained pathology, measurement limitations, or case-sourcing feasibility. Treatment response is not used to validate a causal mechanism.

### Search scope

The search used PubMed/MEDLINE, PubMed Central, Society for Vascular Surgery reporting standards, the 2026 American Venous Forum upper-extremity deep-vein-thrombosis guideline, and American College of Radiology appropriateness criteria. Targeted searches prioritized:

- current authoritative phenotype definitions;
- primary aTOS and thrombotic-vTOS cohorts;
- studies with objective anatomy, wall or luminal pathology, distal consequences, and chronology;
- asymptomatic or noncorresponding comparison evidence;
- measurement feasibility and modality disagreement; and
- sourceable cases with anatomy and endpoints visible in the same patient.

The search was English-language and human-focused. It was source-level but not exhaustive: no formal risk-of-bias instrument, pooled analysis, or complete backward-and-forward citation search was performed. The [TROTS registry protocol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9821680/) was located, but no corresponding prospective phenotype-result publication was identified in this focused search. That absence should be rechecked during the publication-stage literature refresh.

## Decision in one paragraph

Keep **objective aTOS as the leading Chapter 24 phenotype**, but narrow the case to a focal bony anomaly or healed fracture, objective arterial wall pathology, and documented distal embolic or ischemic consequence. It currently offers the most sourceable endpoint chain and the clearest separation among surrounding anatomy, deformable tissue, wall lesion, distal physiology, and presentation. Its apparent cleanliness is partly produced by the SVS definition and by highly selected operative cohorts: human studies usually **co-observe** the bony relation, artery lesion, and distal consequence but do not directly observe years of repeated contact becoming wall injury. The repeated-deformation arrow must therefore remain a reconstruction. Retain **thrombotic vTOS as the chapter's principal time-dependent comparison**, because it shows how a proposed extrinsic exposure can become a mixed state involving thrombus, residual intrinsic obstruction, and collateral adaptation. Do not ask it to carry a second full clinical chain.

## Evidence-state key

The chain tables use four labels:

- **Directly observed:** the stated variables and their temporal or spatial relation were measured in the cited patient or cohort. This still does not make the relation causally sufficient.
- **Co-observed:** the variables occurred in the same selected patient, limb, or cohort, but the arrow between them was not independently measured.
- **Reconstructed:** the arrow is inferred from retrospective chronology, pathology, a model, or clinical attribution rather than directly observed as it developed.
- **Definitionally selected:** the phenotype definition or inclusion rule requires the endpoint, enriching the evidence base for that relation.

These labels concern the evidentiary state of an arrow, not whether the proposed mechanism is physically plausible.

## Phenotype boundaries

The [SVS reporting standards](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0741521416301914) treat neurogenic, venous, and arterial TOS as separate entities that may coexist. They are reporting standards intended to stabilize terminology and outcomes, not a practice guideline or proof that any one mechanical account is complete.

### Objective arterial TOS

For this memo, objective aTOS means:

> objective subclavian-artery pathology attributed to extrinsic compression at the first rib or a related anomalous bony structure, with the selected Chapter 24 case also showing a documented distal arterial or ischemic consequence.

The [SVS executive summary](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0741521416303457) distinguishes objective arterial damage or objectively documented ischemia from pulse loss, waveform change, or asymptomatic provocative narrowing alone. The chapter's use is narrower still: it should not lead with positional arterial compression without wall pathology, nor with a generic arm-pain presentation.

### Thrombotic venous TOS

For this memo, thrombotic vTOS means:

> objectively demonstrated axillary-subclavian upper-extremity deep venous thrombosis for which a thoracic-outlet mechanical etiology is supported after relevant alternatives are considered.

The SVS standards distinguish this thrombotic phenotype from intermittent positional venous obstruction without thrombosis, commonly called McCleery syndrome. The objective thrombus establishes UEDVT; it does not, by itself, establish why the thrombus formed. The [American Venous Forum guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13141763/) classifies vTOS-associated UEDVT separately from catheter-associated, cancer-associated, and other UEDVT, which makes the attribution problem part of the phenotype rather than a dispensable caveat.

## Side-by-side editorial comparison

| Criterion | Objective aTOS | Thrombotic vTOS | Editorial consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Objective defining lesion | Arterial stenosis, aneurysmal or post-stenotic change, thrombosis, occlusion, or other wall damage associated with focal extrinsic anatomy | Axillary-subclavian DVT, sometimes with residual stenosis, chronic occlusion, fibrosis, or collaterals | Both have objective endpoints; neither endpoint alone proves the initiating mechanical history |
| Cleanest surrounding relation | Often a cervical rib, first-rib anomaly, or clavicular fracture adjacent to the subclavian artery | Usually a costoclavicular relation that can change with limb position and task | The arterial case more often offers a discrete, depictable hard-structure relation |
| Initiating mechanical arrow | Repeated contact or deformation of the artery by focal anatomy | Repeated positional compression, altered flow, and wall exposure in the costoclavicular corridor | Both are usually reconstructed; dynamic imaging observes a state, not its cumulative causal history |
| Downstream transition | Wall lesion may carry thrombus and produce distal embolization or ischemia | Thrombosis creates an intrinsic or mixed obstruction that can persist after the initiating exposure | The arterial endpoint chain is easier to keep tissue-specific; the venous chain better teaches state transition and history |
| Negative comparison | Positional arterial waveform change in healthy volunteers; bony anomaly without aTOS; objective arterial dilation without ischemic presentation | Positional venous compression in asymptomatic volunteers; symptoms or outcome not tracking residual venographic class | Both have useful noncorrespondence; venous geometry has especially low specificity by itself |
| Measurement feasibility | Radiograph or CT for bone; CTA/MRA/angiography and duplex for artery lesion and distal consequence | Duplex for UEDVT, with CTV/MRV or venography for central segments; venography and IVUS can disagree about residual stenosis | The aTOS chain is easier to show in one anatomy-plus-artery image set; vTOS often needs multiple time points and modalities |
| Main alternative explanations | Atherosclerotic, inflammatory, traumatic, cardiac or proximal embolic, hypercoagulable, and other arterial disease; incidental bone variant | Catheter, cancer, thrombophilia, systemic or local provoking factor, idiopathic UEDVT, and retained post-thrombotic disease | The vignette must report what was evaluated without implying that a negative checklist proves the proposed origin |
| Evidence-base bias | Rare, retrospective, high-volume referral and operative cohorts, partly definitionally enriched | Predominantly retrospective referral and operative cohorts with heterogeneous chronology and prior treatment | Neither supports population-level or universal-posture claims |
| Best Chapter 24 job | First complete, bounded mechanical-to-clinical chain | Demonstration that extrinsic, intrinsic, and mixed states can change through time | Lead with aTOS; retain vTOS as the time-dependent comparison |

## Candidate arterial causal chain

The reader-facing chain should be no stronger than:

> focal bony relation and relevant exposure history → proposed repeated arterial deformation → objective local wall lesion → mural thrombus, distal embolization, or occlusion → documented distal ischemic consequence → presentation

The word *proposed* is load-bearing. The primary human sources support different links at different strengths.

| Link | Evidence state | Source-level support | Limit that must remain visible |
|---|---|---|---|
| Focal bony abnormality or prior fracture and adjacent subclavian-artery pathology | **Co-observed; definitionally selected in major cohorts** | In a retrospective 30-year operative series, [Pitcher et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35378247/) defined aTOS as subclavian pathology caused by extrinsic compression from a bony abnormality and reported 41 patients and 45 treated limbs. In another tertiary-center operative series, [Vemuri et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28189360/) reported 40 patients: 30 had cervical ribs, five first-rib abnormalities, four clavicular fractures, and one was a reoperation without remaining bony abnormality. | The cohorts enrolled patients already classified and treated as aTOS. Co-location and operative judgment do not independently measure the history of contact or establish that the bony feature is sufficient. |
| Task or position changes local arterial geometry | **Potentially directly observed for the imaged state; historical repetition reconstructed** | Dynamic duplex, CTA, MRA, or angiography can compare selected limb positions. A two-case report of [cervical-rib-associated aTOS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8147708/) shows CTA anatomy and artery-wall findings in neutral/provocative clinical assessment. | An imaging position is not a standardized dose and may not reproduce the initiating task. Probe, protocol, arm angle, and selection matter. |
| Repeated imposed deformation produces intimal or wall injury and post-stenotic change | **Reconstructed** | The operative cohorts and imaging cases repeatedly pair focal bone anatomy with wall thickening, dilation, aneurysm, thrombosis, or occlusion. They are consistent with this arrow but do not observe wall injury developing longitudinally from repeated deformation. | This is the chain's weakest and most important arrow. It should be presented as a mechanism supported by convergent anatomy and pathology, not as a directly watched transformation. Individual tissue state and nonmechanical arterial pathology remain possible contributors. |
| Objective wall lesion is the source of local thrombosis or distal embolization | **Co-observed, with stronger spatial than causal evidence** | [Pitcher et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35378247/) reported acute limb ischemia in 13 of 45 limbs and recurrent thromboembolic events before delayed decompression in eight of those 13. A sourceable imaging case shows a fused cervical/first rib, ipsilateral subclavian pseudoaneurysm, and brachial arterial filling defect in the same CTA examination ([case report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10371763/)). | A selected case can make the spatial sequence visible but cannot exclude every other embolic source or establish a general rate. The recurrent-event cohort is not an untreated natural-history cohort. |
| Distal arterial embolus or occlusion produces objective ischemic consequence | **Directly observed in selected clinical cases and cohorts** | The Pitcher cohort documented acute limb ischemia and brachial occlusion; the Vemuri cohort included 21 ischemic or embolic upper-extremity presentations and two posterior strokes. | This is ordinary arterial clinical reasoning once the lesion is demonstrated, but the exact source and contribution still require patient-specific evaluation. Do not merge ischemia, pain, coldness, and all arm symptoms into one endpoint. |
| Objective distal consequence produces the selected clinical presentation | **Directly observed, symptom-specific** | The cohorts report ischemic presentation, embolic events, pain, weakness, and vascular findings. | Symptoms are not unique to aTOS. The case should begin or end with objective vascular findings rather than asking symptoms to identify the cause. |

### What the arterial evidence does and does not establish

It can support a bounded narrative in which a relatively shape-preserving bony relation and a deformable arterial structure occupy the same constrained corridor, objective artery damage is present, and a distal arterial consequence is documented. That is a useful concrete test of the hard/soft and maintained-constraint framework.

It does not establish that:

- ordinary posture or common positional compression causes aTOS;
- a cervical rib or other bony variant is inherently pathological;
- every arrow was directly observed as it developed;
- decompression outcome retroactively proves the initiating mechanism;
- posterior organization, a global preferred route, or the Book II coordinate model follows from this focal example; or
- the rare focal-anomaly case generalizes to venous or neurogenic TOS.

## Candidate thrombotic-venous causal chain

The most defensible chain is:

> relevant upper-extremity exposure plus individual susceptibility → proposed repeated costoclavicular venous deformation and altered local flow/wall state → axillary-subclavian thrombosis → retained thrombus, fibrosis, stenosis, occlusion, and collateral adaptation → a changed response to later demand and a clinical presentation

This chain is richer than the arterial chain but less clean at its beginning. Thrombus is an objective event; the attribution of that event to repeated outlet mechanics is usually reconstructed.

| Link | Evidence state | Source-level support | Limit that must remain visible |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arm position or task changes venous geometry or outflow at the thoracic outlet | **Directly observed for the tested position** | Dynamic MR, ultrasound, venography, and IVUS can demonstrate position-dependent caliber or flow changes. The [Demondion MR comparison](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12637678/) measured the outlet in 35 asymptomatic volunteers and 54 symptomatic patients in neutral and elevated-arm states. | Positional venous compression was also frequent in asymptomatic volunteers. A transient image is exposure evidence, not a diagnosis or cumulative load history. |
| Repeated deformation and altered flow produce endothelial injury and a thrombogenic local state | **Reconstructed** | Clinical phenotype, activity history, focal thrombosis location, and pathological accounts are consistent with repeated wall and flow disturbance. The focused search did not identify a prospective human study directly measuring the progression from repeated costoclavicular exposure to wall injury and then thrombosis. | This arrow also includes susceptibility. Anatomy, coagulation state, systemic risk, task dose, recovery, and chance cannot be reduced to one compression variable. |
| The mixed local and individual state produces axillary-subclavian DVT | **Thrombus directly observed; mechanical attribution reconstructed and definitionally selected** | The [American Venous Forum guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13141763/) recognizes vTOS-associated UEDVT as an etiologic category distinct from catheter- and cancer-associated UEDVT. Primary cohorts and case reports objectively identify axillary-subclavian thrombosis. | A DVT does not reveal its origin. Catheter, cancer, thrombophilia, other provoking factors, and idiopathic UEDVT must remain in the differential. |
| Thrombosis becomes retained intrinsic or mixed obstruction | **Directly observed after the index event in selected cohorts** | A 2024 retrospective series of [103 completely occluded axillosubclavian veins](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38914374/) stratified patients by time from the inciting vTOS event to operation, making chronic occlusion and recanalization measurable over time. Completion venography studies document residual stenosis and persistent occlusion. | These are treated surgical cohorts. Prior thrombolysis, anticoagulation, venoplasty, and selection alter the state and prevent reconstruction of an untreated course. |
| Residual stenosis or occlusion determines later symptoms and function | **Not reliably corresponding in available selected evidence** | In a retrospective cohort of 51 treated veins, [completion venographic class](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38945363/) did not correlate with symptomatic relief, despite groups ranging from no stenosis to complete occlusion. | This is not evidence that geometry is irrelevant. It shows that a single post-treatment luminal class cannot stand in for collateral flow, tissue state, symptoms, or function. |
| Acute DVT and venous outflow disturbance produce swelling, discoloration, pain, or heaviness | **Directly observed but not specific** | A sourceable case of a [22-year-old collegiate pitcher](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27525184/) documents acute arm pain and swelling preceding eventual axillary-subclavian DVT diagnosis after initially negative studies. | The case is valuable for presentation and measurement fallibility, not for proving the proposed repetitive-injury mechanism. Symptoms also occur in other vascular, inflammatory, and musculoskeletal conditions. |

### What the venous evidence adds

Thrombotic vTOS is the stronger example of a state transition:

1. an extrinsic relation is proposed at the beginning;
2. thrombus and wall change create an intrinsic state;
3. collaterals and recanalization change later physiology; and
4. present geometry no longer recovers the initiating history.

That is exactly the material protected by `BGB-DEC-0004`. It should not be compressed into a generic statement that a narrow space reduces flow. Its editorial value is that the system acquires history.

## Asymptomatic and noncorresponding evidence

These comparisons belong inside the argument, not in a disclaimer.

| Finding | What it rules out | What it does not rule out |
|---|---|---|
| In 19 healthy volunteers scanned bilaterally in seven positions, [Bishop and Bartlett](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33868673/) found a waveform interpreted as significant arterial compression in at least one position in 28.9% of participants. | A provocative arterial waveform alone cannot define aTOS or show artery-wall injury. | A focal bony relation with objective wall damage and distal ischemia. |
| In the Demondion MR study, postural venous compression was found in asymptomatic volunteers at multiple outlet locations, including 54% at the costoclavicular space. | Positional venous narrowing alone cannot identify thrombotic vTOS or a clinically consequential outflow state. | A complete chain containing objective thrombus, chronology, physiology, and alternatives. |
| In the Vemuri aTOS operative series, 17 of 40 patients had nonvascular presentations, including six with an asymptomatic neck mass or incidentally discovered subclavian dilation. | Objective arterial pathology does not guarantee an ischemic symptom presentation. | The clinical importance of a particular wall lesion; the cohort cannot supply population natural history. |
| The completion-venography cohort found no association between residual venographic class and symptom relief. | Post-treatment lumen class cannot be used as a complete proxy for symptoms or function. | Effects mediated through flow, collaterals, prior pathology, or other variables not captured by that class. |
| Cervical ribs occur in people who have not been selected for aTOS, including [0.74% of 1,352 chest radiographs](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19280652/) and [2.0% of 3,404 adult cervical CT examinations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7965591/) in two different studies. | The presence of a cervical rib cannot diagnose aTOS. | Whether a particular rib-artery relation in a particular limb contributed to objective wall injury. |

The studies use different populations, protocols, and thresholds. Their percentages should not be combined or presented as a single prevalence estimate.

## Alternatives that the leading vignette must preserve

### Arterial branch

- cardiac, aortic, or proximal arterial source of embolus;
- atherosclerotic, inflammatory, vasculitic, aneurysmal, or other intrinsic arterial disease;
- acute or remote trauma unrelated to the proposed recurrent outlet contact;
- hypercoagulability or another contributor to thrombosis;
- vasospasm, distal arterial disease, and nonvascular explanations for symptoms; and
- incidental bony variation near an independently diseased artery.

The vignette need not become a differential-diagnosis survey. It should state which alternatives the source actually evaluated, which remain possible, and why the selected case is being used despite that residual uncertainty.

### Venous branch

- central or peripheral venous catheter and other device-associated UEDVT;
- active cancer;
- known or unrecognized thrombophilia and other systemic prothrombotic states;
- another local provoking event, trauma, inflammation, or mass;
- primary or idiopathic UEDVT without demonstrated outlet attribution; and
- reverse causation in which thrombosis, edema, scarring, or protective movement changes the observed outlet geometry.

The chapter must not imply that youth, athletic activity, or a negative initial laboratory panel proves a mechanical origin.

## Measurement feasibility

| Question | Feasible measurements | Principal limitation for Chapter 24 |
|---|---|---|
| Is focal bone anatomy present? | Radiography and CT; MRI for selected soft-tissue relations | Presence does not establish contact dose, tissue injury, or clinical relevance |
| Is objective arterial damage present? | Duplex, CTA, MRA, catheter angiography, operative and pathology descriptions | Protocols and limb position vary; operative cohorts are selected; provocative change without injury is nonspecific |
| Is there a distal arterial consequence? | Distal pulse and perfusion measures, duplex, CTA or angiography, documented embolus or occlusion, objective ischemia | Symptoms alone are insufficient; exact embolic source can remain uncertain |
| Is axillary-subclavian DVT present? | Duplex as first-line; CTV or MRV for central anatomy or inconclusive studies; catheter venography in selected clinical contexts | The clavicle limits visualization of the proximal subclavian vein. The AVF guideline cites a 21% false-negative initial duplex rate in one surgically selected vTOS series; that number is not general-population test performance. |
| What residual venous geometry remains? | Venography, cross-sectional venography, and IVUS | Modality and threshold matter. In 18 post-decompression cases, [IVUS detected five greater-than-50% stenoses not apparent on single-plane venography](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30217714/); this small treated cohort shows modality disagreement, not diagnostic superiority for an untreated population. |
| Did repeated deformation cause the lesion? | No routine clinical modality directly observes the full cumulative history | This must be reconstructed from anatomy, task history, lesion location, pathology, chronology, and alternatives, with the reconstruction labeled |

The [ACR UEDVT criteria](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32370975/) support duplex as the first imaging modality for suspected UEDVT and CT or MR venography for central structures or incomplete evaluation. Those recommendations describe appropriate measurement pathways; they do not validate the mechanical attribution required for thrombotic vTOS.

## Vignette-sourcing feasibility

### Recommended arterial form: an explicitly sourced composite

Use a compact composite whose facts are individually traceable, rather than presenting a fictional patient as though one paper directly observed every arrow. The primary visual and factual anchor is now the 2026 open-access case, [*Arterial thoracic outlet syndrome due to a first-rib anomaly causing brachial artery embolic occlusion*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.radcr.2026.02.020). Its bilateral first-rib anomalies, unilateral focal bony relation, ipsilateral subclavian stenosis and post-stenotic dilation, local thrombus, distal occlusion, collateral circulation, and objective side-to-side pressure difference make both the positive chain and an internal noncorrespondence visible. The composite can include:

1. a focal cervical-rib/first-rib fusion, first-rib anomaly, or healed clavicular fracture;
2. objective adjacent subclavian-artery wall pathology;
3. mural thrombus, distal embolus, or a documented occlusion;
4. objective distal ischemic findings; and
5. a clearly labeled reconstruction of the repeated-deformation history.

The 2023 [cervical-rib case](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10371763/) remains a strong secondary visual base because one CTA set contains fused bony anatomy, an ipsilateral subclavian pseudoaneurysm, and a brachial thromboembolus. It is not a complete stand-alone vignette: the patient was lost to follow-up, exposure history is sparse, and the repeated-injury arrow was not observed. The Pitcher and Vemuri cohorts can supply the population boundary and demonstrate that the anatomy-lesion-distal-consequence pattern recurs in selected specialist cohorts.

Every sentence in the vignette should be tagged during drafting as one of:

- source-observed patient fact;
- cohort context;
- clinical interpretation;
- mechanical reconstruction; or
- project-framework application.

This provenance should be removed from reader-facing prose only after the claim-evidence ledger preserves it.

### Venous comparison form: an event-to-retained-state mini-timeline

Do not build a second character-rich patient story. Use a short sourced sequence:

> an objective axillary-subclavian DVT occurs → later imaging shows some combination of residual thrombus, stenosis, occlusion, recanalization, or collaterals → the later system cannot be treated as the original extrinsic geometry

The 103-patient chronic-occlusion cohort provides chronology and objective retained state. The completion-venography cohort provides a useful noncorrespondence between luminal class and symptoms. An athlete case can make the acute presentation legible, but its activity history must not be used as proof of causation. Any treatment details should be omitted unless needed to explain why a later measurement cannot represent untreated natural history.

## Medical and editorial risks

The final chapter requires medical-coauthor and relevant vascular review because the selected examples concern limb ischemia and DVT. The main risks are:

1. **Reader self-diagnosis.** Pulse change, arm symptoms, a provocative waveform, or positional narrowing must not be presented as a home test or diagnostic shortcut.
2. **Under-triage through postural framing.** Acute ischemia and UEDVT are medically consequential states. The chapter must not imply that they should be interpreted or managed as alignment problems.
3. **Treatment inference.** Better outcomes after decompression, reconstruction, thrombolysis, anticoagulation, or venoplasty cannot be used here to prove a mechanism or recommend an action.
4. **Phenotype transfer.** Arterial evidence must not be transferred to venous or neurogenic TOS. Arterial pressure-flow relations must not be written under a generic vascular label.
5. **Anomaly moralization.** A cervical rib, first-rib shape, clavicular history, or ordinary positional compression is not inherently defective.
6. **Causal completion by label.** *Arterial TOS*, *effort thrombosis*, and *Paget-Schroetter syndrome* should not be treated as if their names directly measured every initiating arrow.
7. **Referral-cohort generalization.** The main cohorts are rare-disease, high-volume-center, retrospective, and operative. They do not estimate what happens in an unselected population.
8. **Figure-as-protocol risk.** No figure should invite the reader to reproduce provocative positions, compare pulses, or infer diagnosis from a body position.

## Reserve and contrast phenotypes

### Nonthrombotic venous TOS or McCleery syndrome

Preserve this phenotype as a reserve because it may isolate a more reversible relation among position, geometry, and outflow without the confounding intrinsic state created by thrombus. Do not promote it now. Positional venous compression is common in asymptomatic people, accepted diagnostic thresholds and prospective natural history remain limited, and the focused search did not recover a cohort capable of completing geometry → physiology → symptom → diagnosis with the same clarity as the objective arterial case.

### Neurogenic TOS

Preserve neurogenic TOS as a within-region contrast only. It usefully demonstrates why artery and vein evidence cannot establish brachial-plexus contact pressure, strain, excursion, intraneural circulation, conduction, symptoms, or diagnosis. Chapter 24 should not borrow a vascular chain to complete a neural one. The selected Chapter 13 tendon–SSCT comparison provides the non-lumen transfer test without duplicating this neural contrast.

## Editorial recommendation and explicit limits

### Recommendation

The accepted first-draft direction is:

- **Lead:** objective aTOS with defined focal bony anatomy, objective subclavian-artery damage, and documented distal embolic or ischemic consequence.
- **Vignette form:** explicitly sourced composite anchored by one image-complete case and bounded by two specialist cohorts.
- **Weakest arrow:** repeated imposed deformation → wall injury; mark as reconstructed throughout drafting.
- **Required negative comparison:** healthy provocative arterial waveform change and bony variation without aTOS.
- **Time-dependent comparison:** thrombotic vTOS, limited to the transition from proposed extrinsic exposure to retained intrinsic or mixed pathology.
- **Reserve:** nonthrombotic venous TOS.
- **Within-region contrast:** neurogenic TOS, without a borrowed causal chain.

### Limits on the chapter's conclusion

The worked case may conclude that the framework helps separate:

1. surrounding anatomy and task;
2. proposed tissue exposure;
3. objective wall or luminal pathology;
4. distal physiology;
5. clinical presentation; and
6. alternative explanations.

It may also show that a deformable structure can acquire a consequential material state when the surrounding relation and time history matter. It may **not** conclude from this case that the skeleton is always the correct carrier, that posterior organization prevents vascular disease, that common posture is a root cause of TOS, that an anatomical variant predicts symptoms, or that the focal arterial chain proves the broader preferred-configuration program.

### Closure conditions before publication-stage prose approval

1. The narrower teaching job remains a complete focal chain, not a representative account of all TOS; this decision is accepted for the first draft.
2. The medical coauthor confirms the aTOS and thrombotic-vTOS definitions, the alternative list, and the safety boundary before publication approval.
3. A source table maps every vignette fact and every causal arrow to the evidence-state key.
4. The selected image source has usable rights or is redrawn from source facts with attribution and no diagnostic-instruction framing.
5. The chapter explicitly identifies definition-based and referral-based enrichment.
6. The conclusion preserves the reconstructed status of the repeated-injury arrow.
7. The 2026-07-11 refresh located no TROTS result publication in the focused search; it did locate two new 2026 aTOS case reports. Recheck for prospective phenotype evidence during publication review.
