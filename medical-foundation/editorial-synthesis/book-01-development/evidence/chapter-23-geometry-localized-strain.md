## Editorial decision and accepted-outline delta

Chapter 23 should lead with a source-grounded knee construction centered on stance-phase walking and bounded weight-bearing comparisons. It should then test the same relations in the lumbar disc–facet–endplate system. This is an evidence-led revision of the accepted outline's provisional lumbar lead, not a change to the chapter's job or an ablation of the archive's axial program.

The knee has the stronger lead architecture because the evidence set includes:

- direct in-vivo internal force measurements from instrumented knee replacements;
- a direct counterexample in which a reduced calculated external moment did not guarantee a reduced measured medial contact force;
- in-vivo native-knee contact-deformation and intratissue-strain measurements;
- fixed-apparatus cadaver experiments that alter an interface while holding nominal external demand constant;
- validated but visibly imperfect finite-element bridges from joint loading to local fields;
- prospective population evidence with compartment-specific associations and internal nulls; and
- ordinary alignment and imaging findings in asymptomatic people.

No source observes the complete chain in one native knee or one person. The lead is therefore a **multistudy reconstruction**, not a continuous case history. Its sources use walking, gait modification, a single-leg lunge, controlled MRI loading, cadaveric axial loading, computational models, explants, and population follow-up. Those tasks and evidence layers must remain separate even when they answer adjacent questions.

The lumbar system remains required because it carries the project's axial and spinal intent and makes the transfer test difficult in the right way. It has direct intradiscal pressure evidence, cadaveric disc–facet load-sharing and internal-stress evidence, subject-specific models, dose-responsive animal evidence, and strong finding–symptom noncorrespondence. It also exposes why a clean limb-joint construction cannot be generalized without accounting for neighboring segments, muscle redundancy, coupled disc–facet–endplate behavior, material state, and level-specific effects.

The protected authorial program remains affirmative:

> Whole-body and axial geometry may participate in the redistribution and concentration of demand, and mechanically mediated degenerative progression remains a legitimate condition-specific research program.

Chapter 23 does not need a preferred global configuration, skeletal-primary routing, universal posterior priority, signed generalized modes, TCM correspondence, breath geometry, or correction to establish its Book I argument. Those later hypotheses may motivate questions, but no missing knee or lumbar arrow may be filled by them.

## Central thesis

> For a declared task, reference frame, timescale, and system boundary, geometry is one input that can change moment arms, contact location and area, load sharing, and the distribution and orientation of local stress and strain. Those changes can be directly measured in selected settings or estimated with models whose assumptions and validation domain are explicit. They do not by themselves establish injury, degeneration, pain, diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment. Consequence depends on exposure history, material state, biological context, capacity, recovery, and time; in some comparisons the proposed geometric change has little measurable effect.

The compact mechanical result is:

> Similar external demands can produce different internal distributions, and similar joint or segment resultants can conceal different local fields.

The compact clinical boundary is:

> A local strain field is an exposure description. It becomes an adverse claim only through receiver-specific dose, response, and outcome evidence.

## Quantity ontology and declared free-body relation

The reader-facing chapter must not use *load* for every node. The lead construction requires the following quantity distinctions:

| Quantity | Working definition in the knee construction | Evidence state commonly available | Prohibited substitution |
|---|---|---|---|
| External contact force | Ground or apparatus force crossing the selected boundary | `MEAS` by force platform or loading device | bodyweight as knee contact force |
| Segment force and moment balance | Dynamic equilibrium of a declared segment | `CALC` from kinematics, inertia, and external contacts | a unique muscle, ligament, or compartment solution |
| Net joint resultant or moment | Equivalent residual required by the segment model | `CALC`, or selected components `MEAS` in an implant | one anatomical carrier or local tissue field |
| Compartment contact force | Resultant contact contribution assigned to a named compartment | implant `MEAS` or model `MOD` | contact area, pressure peak, or strain |
| Contact area and location | Interface region satisfying the selected contact definition | imaging reconstruction `MOD/MEAS` or pressure-film `MEAS` | tissue stress or biological consequence |
| Contact pressure or traction | Force distribution acting across an interface | sensor `MEAS` or contact model `MOD` | intratissue stress tensor or strain |
| Stress | Internal force-per-area tensor at a declared material point and frame | usually `MOD`; selected components experimentally estimated | pressure, external force, or damage |
| Strain | Deformation relative to a declared reference configuration and measure | imaging `MEAS/CALC`, local model `MOD`, or explant `MEAS` | injury, degeneration, or pain |
| Dose or exposure history | Receiver-specific time organization of magnitude, mode, rate, repetition, duration, recovery, and prior state | `MEAS`, `INF`, or reconstructed sequence | one peak or cumulative body-wide scalar |
| Tissue response | Cellular, matrix, material, inflammatory, repair, fatigue, or failure output | receiver-specific `MEAS` | finding, symptom, diagnosis, or outcome |

For a distal lower-limb free body, take moments about a point \(O\) fixed in an inertial frame. The declared relation is:

\[
\sum \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}} + \mathbf{F}_{J} = m\mathbf{a}_{G},
\qquad
\sum \mathbf{M}_{O,\mathrm{ext}} + \mathbf{r}_{J/O} \times \mathbf{F}_{J} + \mathbf{M}_{J}
= \frac{d\mathbf{H}_{O}}{dt}.
\]

Here, the measured ground contact, segment mass properties, and measured kinematics can support a calculated knee resultant \(\mathbf{F}_{J}\) and net moment \(\mathbf{M}_{J}\), subject to measurement and model uncertainty. The balance does **not** identify how muscle, ligament, meniscus, medial contact, lateral contact, and other internal actions share those equivalents.

All vector terms in a particular construction must be expressed in one declared frame and sign convention. Changing the boundary or frame changes the bookkeeping and may change the numerical components without changing the physical event.

At the joint scale, for a separately declared cut through the anatomical carriers, a schematic force balance may be written:

\[
\mathbf{R}_{J}
= \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{medial}}
+ \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{lateral}}
+ \sum_i \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{muscle},i}
+ \sum_j \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ligament},j}
+ \cdots .
\]

Here, \(\mathbf{R}_{J}\) is the equivalent force resultant of the contributions crossing that cut; it is not automatically identical to \(\mathbf{F}_{J}\) above unless the two boundaries and sign conventions coincide. This is a bookkeeping relation, not a solvable decomposition until the unknowns, contacts, constraints, and model are specified. At an interface, traction is \(\mathbf{t}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf{n}\). A scalar average \(F_n/A\) can describe a declared normal resultant over an area, but it does not recover the spatial pressure distribution, shear traction, three-dimensional stress tensor, material-dependent strain field, or local maximum.

Required figure convention: observed geometry, measured forces, calculated net moments, measured implant forces, reconstructed contact, modeled stress, measured or modeled strain, and hypothesized biological arrows must use different labels and redundant visual styles.

## Lead construction and evidence-state declaration

The lead task class is **stance-phase walking**, supplemented by bounded weight-bearing and fixed-apparatus experiments that isolate adjacent links. The system includes the femur, tibia, tibiofemoral contacts, menisci, relevant passive restraints, muscles crossing the knee, and the ground contact when the entire distal segment is drawn. The central receiver is tibiofemoral articular cartilage; the meniscus enters as an interface and load-sharing structure, not as a passive cushion or fixed “soft tissue.”

The construction is not a patient case. Its base-state table is:

| Stage | Exact object | State | Principal source form | Consequential unknown |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Task and environment | stance-phase walking or declared laboratory loading condition | `OBS/MEAS` | gait laboratory, loading device, or cadaver apparatus | whether tasks across studies are mechanically equivalent |

| Exposure history | repeated walking, cyclic loading, or experimental dose | `MEAS` within a protocol; longer history `INF/reconstructed` | ordinary-life exposure, recovery, and prior tissue state |

No rhetorical transition may fill the native-human gaps. The chapter's result is a transparent reconstruction whose strongest links are mechanical and whose later biological and clinical links narrow in strength.

## Multiscale knee evidence chain

### External demand and internal distribution

Kutzner and colleagues measured forces and moments in five people with instrumented total knee replacements during activities of daily living, including walking.[^kutzner2010] The study directly supports task-dependent internal joint resultants in those implants. It does not measure native cartilage stress, strain, or a population norm.

Walter and colleagues provide the decisive surrogate counterexample. In one instrumented-knee participant, gait modifications that reduced the first peak of the calculated external knee-adduction moment did not necessarily reduce the corresponding first peak of measured medial contact force. A combined adduction-plus-flexion-moment model tracked the measured force more closely, and the adduction moment was sensitive to reference-frame definition.[^walter2010]

Permitted conclusion:

> A calculated external moment can inform an internal-distribution hypothesis without uniquely specifying the compartment force of interest.

Not permitted: the knee-adduction moment is useless, all gait modification fails, or one prosthetic knee establishes a population relation.

### Native-knee contact, deformation, and local strain

Bingham and colleagues combined MRI-derived cartilage geometry with dual fluoroscopy in 11 healthy knees during a weight-bearing single-leg lunge. Contact location and estimated deformation varied with flexion and compartment.[^bingham2008] The method reconstructs cartilage overlap; it did not measure ground reaction force, separate femoral from tibial deformation, or observe stress.

Thorhauer and Tashman later validated a related biplanar-radiography and MRI contact-estimation method against direct surface measurements in three cadaver knees across static poses from 0° to 35° of flexion and axial loads of one, two, and three times a nominal bodyweight. They reported useful but nonzero error in contact area and centroid.[^thorhauer2015] This source should accompany—not erase—the uncertainty attached to image-derived contact.

Chan and colleagues synchronized MRI with cyclic loading equivalent to one-half bodyweight at the foot in nine healthy volunteers. They measured regional displacement and intratissue strain in a sagittal plane of the medial tibiofemoral cartilage and found complex, spatially variable strain fields, including substantial shear.[^chan2016] This is the packet's strongest direct human support for the proposition that a nominal external load and scalar average pressure do not specify the local deformation field. Its limits are one constrained task, one imaged plane and compartment, a small healthy sample, and a strain calculation dependent on image processing and reference state.

Lad and colleagues measured site-specific cartilage-thickness changes in eight healthy knees before and after 20 minutes of treadmill walking. Compartment-average compressive strains were smaller than localized maxima, including a medial-tibial local maximum above 8% under the study's measurement convention.[^lad2016] The result establishes normal spatial heterogeneity and prevents *localized strain* from becoming a synonym for damage. Delayed post-activity MRI, recovery during transfer, thickness-based strain, small sample, and healthy participants bound the value.

These four sources are adjacent, not interchangeable:

```text
weight-bearing kinematics and reconstructed contact       Bingham: OBS/MEAS -> MOD/CALC
method validation under cadaver loading                   Thorhauer: MEAS comparison
controlled in-vivo intratissue strain                     Chan: MEAS -> CALC
pre/post ordinary-walking thickness strain                Lad: MEAS -> CALC
```

### Fixed-load interface intervention and a structural null

Ihn and colleagues tested five human knee specimens and reported reduced contact area and increased stress concentration after partial or total meniscectomy under their in-vitro loading conditions.[^ihn1993] This is a direct physical demonstration that changing an interface while holding the apparatus condition can redistribute contact.

Muriuki and colleagues tested 11 fresh-frozen knees under 1000 N at four flexion angles. Created radial split tears did not significantly change contact pressure or area; vertical tears produced directionally different but statistically nonsignificant changes, and repair returned measured values toward the intact condition.[^muriuki2011] This is not a failed example. It is the required structural null:

> A named geometric or morphological alteration need not produce a detectable change in the selected local quantity under the tested boundary conditions.

The two cadaver studies use different specimens, interventions, apparatuses, and measurement methods. Neither establishes living adaptation, pain, osteoarthritis initiation, or a posture rule.

### Models bridge missing quantities only within context of use

Haut Donahue and colleagues varied meniscal size and shape in a knee finite-element model and found predicted contact variables sensitive to those perturbations, especially for the medial meniscus.[^hautdonahue2004] The work supports a bounded geometry-sensitivity proposition. Its numerical sensitivity is not a biological tolerance threshold.

Kiapour and colleagues developed an anatomic knee finite-element model and compared model outputs with cadaveric kinematics, ligament strain or force, and cartilage pressure across static, quasi-static, and dynamic validation experiments.[^kiapour2014] The study illustrates multi-observable validation; it does not make one model universally credible for every subject, task, output, or disease state.

Steineman and colleagues compared subject-specific finite-element contact predictions in five cadaver knees under MRI-compatible axial loading with electronic pressure-sensor results. Agreement and discrepancy both varied by metric and specimen.[^steineman2025] This source makes the correct publication lesson visible: validation is graded, output-specific, and conditioned by the experimental comparator.

The chapter should adopt the FDA's context-of-use, model-risk, and credibility-evidence discipline for any computational claim.[^fda2023] ASME V&V 40 supplies the corresponding authoritative verification-and-validation framework.[^asme2018] Neither source validates a particular knee or spine model.

Every retained model must declare:

1. object represented and intended claim job;
2. system boundary, task, and scale;
3. geometry and reference configuration;
4. contacts, active forces, restraints, and boundary conditions;
5. constitutive law and material parameters;
6. mesh, solver, convergence, and numerical uncertainty where relevant;
7. validation comparator and output;
8. sensitivity and uncertainty for the quantity used in prose; and
9. what would make the result inapplicable or non-discriminating.

### Dose, receiver state, and biological response

Chapter 19 owns the general mechanobiology. Chapter 23 should use only enough evidence to show why a local field could matter and why it does not decide the response.

Quinn and colleagues applied sub-impact compression to adult bovine articular-cartilage explants while varying peak stress and strain rate. Matrix and cell injury patterns depended on the tested combination and were spatially heterogeneous.[^quinn2001] Coleman and colleagues exposed bovine cartilage explants to repeated cyclic loading and found load- and duration-dependent changes in cellular energetics and oxidative state without extensive cell death or gross macroscopic damage under the reported protocol.[^coleman2016] Patwari and colleagues showed that injurious compression and exogenous cytokine conditions interacted in bovine and human cartilage explants.[^patwari2003]

Together these sources support:

```text
local exposure + rate/repetition/duration + receiver state/biology
    -> measured response / no gross structural failure under the protocol
```

They do not supply a human walking threshold, prove that the in-vivo strains above were injurious, or establish knee osteoarthritis, pain, or treatment response. Ordinary local strain in healthy walking must remain beside the adverse explant branches.

### Bounded clinical bridge and noncorrespondence

Sharma and colleagues followed knees with and without radiographic osteoarthritis in the MOST cohort. Varus and valgus alignment were associated with compartment-specific incident or progressive radiographic outcomes after adjustment for selected covariates.[^sharma2010] This supports a population-level, compartment-specific association. Alignment was not direct local stress, and the study does not reconstruct an individual's initiating exposure.

Bennell and colleagues analyzed 144 people with medial knee osteoarthritis over 12 months. Knee-adduction-moment impulse, but not peak moment, was associated with medial tibial cartilage-volume loss; the tested moment measures were not significantly associated with every other MRI outcome.[^bennell2011] This supplies a positive branch and an internal null across exposure metrics and endpoints. The adduction moment remains a surrogate rather than a direct medial-contact or cartilage-strain measurement.

Bellemans and colleagues studied 250 asymptomatic adults aged 20–27 and found substantial natural variation away from neutral mechanical alignment, including at least 3 degrees of varus in 32% of men and 17% of women.[^bellemans2012] This refutes a universal-neutral assumption. It does not establish lifetime safety, individual efficiency, or the preferred configuration.

Guermazi and colleagues found MRI abnormalities to be common in Framingham participants both with and without knee pain.[^guermazi2012] This establishes finding–symptom non-equivalence in that population. It does not make every finding incidental or prohibit a condition-specific association.

The strongest permitted clinical conclusion is:

> Geometry and geometry-sensitive surrogates can contribute to compartment-specific structural exposure questions and population associations. Their relevance to one person's finding, pain, function, diagnosis, or care remains a separate, condition-specific inference.

## Required lumbar transfer test

The transfer test carries the same relations into a lumbar motion segment while preserving anatomical and measurement differences.

| Relation | Knee lead | Lumbar transfer | Transfer result |
|---|---|---|---|
| declared task | stance-phase walking and bounded loading tasks | one declared daily-life posture or controlled compression task | task specification transfers; numerical demand does not |
| external calculation | force-platform and inverse-dynamics quantities | external forces, kinematics, and muscle/model inputs | balance transfers; redundancy remains region-specific |
| internal direct measurement | selected components in instrumented prostheses | intradiscal pressure in one instrumented disc | both are partial internal quantities, not complete fields |
| load sharing | medial/lateral contact, meniscus, muscle, ligament | disc, facets, endplates, ligaments, muscles, neighboring segments | sharing transfers; compartments and carriers do not |
| local exposure | cartilage contact, pressure, deformation, strain | nucleus pressure, annular/endplate/facet stress and strain | local-field question transfers; constitutive behavior does not |
| receiver state | cartilage thickness, composition, injury, inflammation | disc degeneration, hydration, endplate and annular state, age | state dependence transfers; state variables differ |
| response and finding | cartilage response and OA morphology | disc/endplate/facet response and spinal imaging | node separation transfers; clinical correspondence differs |
| null | surrogate failure, ordinary alignment, asymptomatic MRI | model sensitivity, asymptomatic imaging, finding–symptom discordance | low-priority interpretation transfers |

Wilke and colleagues measured intradiscal pressure in the nondegenerate L4–L5 disc of one 45-year-old male volunteer during approximately 24 hours of activities.[^wilke1999] This directly supports task- and muscle-activity-dependent pressure in one disc. Pressure does not specify annular strain, endplate stress, facet load, pain, or a universal lifting relation.

Adams and Hutton loaded cadaveric lumbar motion segments in simulated lordotic-standing and slightly flexed-sitting conditions after sustained preload. Under their apparatus conditions, posterior-element sharing differed substantially between the two positions.[^adamshutton1980] This is a direct disc–facet sharing construction but not living muscle control, ordinary symptom production, or an optimal-posture result.

Adams, McNally, and Dolan measured internal compressive “stress” profiles in 87 cadaveric lumbar discs and found different distributions with age and degeneration, including changes in the central hydrostatic region and annular peaks.[^adams1996] The important direction is reciprocal: material and structural state alter the field produced under a demand. The authors' pain interpretation is not imported as proof.

Müller and colleagues constructed simplified subject-specific models from CT data for 28 lumbar spines without visible degeneration and applied a standardized modeled compression. Predicted compression, shear, bending moments, and facet forces varied with lordosis, level, output quantity, and multifidus activation rather than moving in one uniform direction.[^muller2021] This supports geometry-sensitive modeled distribution and defeats a global “more versus less load” summary. It does not establish symptoms, degeneration, an ideal curve, or treatment.

Dehghan-Hamani and colleagues used five geometrically scaled, radiography-driven detailed finite-element models and reported substantial intersubject variation in calculated joint and facet forces while comparing selected outputs with available in-vivo pressure evidence.[^dehghan2019] Its job is to show subject geometry, kinematics, muscle-force estimation, and model structure entering the answer; it is not direct exposure measurement in five living spines.

Lotz and Chin applied static compression at several magnitudes and durations to mouse-tail discs and measured increasing cell death with exposure under the model.[^lotz2000] This is a receiver-specific magnitude–duration response, not a human postural threshold or proof that ordinary spinal geometry initiates degeneration.

Jensen and colleagues found disc bulges, protrusions, and other MRI findings in many of 98 participants without back pain.[^jensen1994] This supplies the required lumbar finding–symptom noncorrespondence. It does not establish that every selected spinal finding is clinically irrelevant.

The lumbar result should be stated positively but narrowly:

> Axial geometry, active support, level, contact, and material state can alter disc–facet–endplate load sharing and modeled or measured internal quantities. The spine therefore remains a central application of the framework. The current evidence does not convert visible curve or imaging morphology into a unique local field, disease history, symptom, or preferred correction.

## Evidence-layer and transport boundaries

| Evidence layer | What it can establish here | What it cannot establish here |
|---|---|---|
| native-human in-vivo measurement | task-specific kinematics, deformation, thickness change, or selected strain components | full three-dimensional stress field, cellular response, long-term causal history |
| instrumented human implant | selected internal resultants or compartment forces under measured tasks | native anatomy, young or unselected populations, local cartilage field |
| cadaver experiment | controlled contact or load-sharing response to a declared intervention | active regulation, remodeling, symptoms, living chronology |
| finite-element or musculoskeletal model | conditional distribution, sensitivity, and quantities inaccessible to direct measurement | unique truth outside context of use, damage, diagnosis, or clinical effect |
| animal in-vivo model | receiver response to controlled magnitude, mode, or duration | direct human threshold, ordinary task meaning, symptoms |
| explant or cell experiment | local biological and material response under controlled exposure | whole-joint distribution, circulation, behavior, pain, or clinical disease |
| prospective population study | temporal association between selected predictor and defined outcome | complete intermediate mechanics, individual causal explanation, treatment effect |
| cross-sectional asymptomatic study | ordinary variation and noncorrespondence | lifetime harmlessness or irrelevance of all findings |

The chapter should not use the number of sources to simulate one continuous chain. Convergence is meaningful only when adjacent sources study compatible objects and their transfer limits remain visible.

## Alternatives, discriminators, and required nulls

Alternatives enter at the affected node:

| Node or arrow | Principal alternatives | Discriminating observation | Null or narrowing result |
|---|---|---|---|
| visible geometry -> internal distribution | muscle recruitment, co-contraction, gait strategy, neighboring joints, support, speed, coordinate frame | direct internal force or validated subject-specific estimate under the same task | geometry difference with no meaningful internal-force difference |
| joint resultant -> compartment sharing | ligament force, muscle force, meniscal state, contact location, model assumptions | instrumented compartment force or validated contact estimate | external surrogate changes while measured contact force does not |
| compartment force -> local field | contact area, material heterogeneity, cartilage thickness, meniscus, reference state | spatial pressure or strain measurement and sensitivity analysis | predicted local field insensitive within uncertainty |
| local field -> tissue response | adequate capacity, adaptation, recovery, inflammatory or metabolic state, prior injury | repeated receiver-specific exposure and response measurement | ordinary deformation with recovery or no detected adverse response |
| response -> structural finding | age, development, genetics, injury, biology-first state, care | longitudinal local response preceding a defined finding | response without finding or finding predating exposure |
| finding -> symptom/function | sensitization, expectation, sleep, mood, another tissue, compensation, incidental finding | condition-specific correspondence and within-person temporal evidence | asymptomatic finding or symptom not tracking the selected finding |
| complete account -> clinical value | existing diagnosis or rehabilitation account, measurement burden, nonunique history | incremental reliability, discrimination, prediction, or decision relevance | no added value over a simpler established account |

At least these nulls must remain in the first draft:

1. reduced knee-adduction moment did not guarantee reduced measured medial contact force;
2. a created radial meniscal tear did not significantly alter the selected contact measures;
3. healthy walking produced localized cartilage strain without an injury conclusion;
4. asymptomatic adults displayed non-neutral alignment and common MRI findings;
5. one exposure metric associated with one structural outcome while other tested metrics or outcomes did not;
6. lumbar model outputs changed by level and quantity rather than in one global direction; and
7. the full geometry route may be lower priority than another biological, developmental, injury, symptom-driven, or contextual history.

## Reader architecture authorized by this packet

### Opening — one task, several meanings of load

Begin with stance-phase walking and two gait or configuration conditions. Show the ground contact and calculated knee moment before revealing the instrumented-force counterexample. The opening question is not which gait is correct; it is which internal quantity the external measurement actually constrains.

### 23.1 Define the knee system before naming a problem

Declare the boundary, phase, frame, contacts, active contributors, passive restraints, interface structures, receiver, timescale, and evidence-state table. State immediately that the chapter is a multistudy reconstruction across nonidentical tasks.

### 23.2 How geometry changes moments, contacts, and the internal question

Apply the free-body relation, distinguish net joint moment from anatomical sharing, and use Walter's result to prevent external moment from becoming medial contact force. Introduce flexion-dependent contact and active-control alternatives.

### 23.3 From joint demand to localized cartilage strain

Move through implant force, contact area, local pressure, deformation, and intratissue strain without merging them. Use Chan and Lad to show spatial heterogeneity and Ihn/Muriuki to pair a controlled redistribution with a structural null.

### 23.4 Dose, material state, response, and recovery

Apply rather than reteach Chapters 10 and 19. Keep ordinary healthy deformation beside rate-, repetition-, duration-, and biology-sensitive explant responses. State the missing human longitudinal bridge.

### 23.5 From response to findings and symptoms

Use Sharma and Bennell for bounded structural associations, then Bellemans and Guermazi for ordinary variation and noncorrespondence. Do not make OA the chapter's diagnosis or final answer.

### 23.6 Lumbar disc–facet–endplate transfer

Carry the same rows into the spine: task, balance, sharing, local quantity, state, response, finding, symptom, alternative, and null. Use Wilke, Adams and Hutton, Adams et al., Müller, Dehghan-Hamani, Lotz and Chin, and Jensen. Make the shared relation and nontransferable quantities equally visible.

### 23.7 Competing histories, discriminators, and low-priority outcome

Compare morphology, development, genetics, injury, inflammation, total activity, pain-driven strategy, deconditioning, active compensation, measurement error, and incidental findings at their affected nodes. End with support, narrowing, unresolved, and no-added-value outputs.

### Conclusion and Chapter 24 handoff

Reconstruct the full chain with its evidence states and weakest arrows. Geometry can alter internal distribution without becoming a diagnosis. Chapter 24 changes the central object from local concentration within a load-bearing joint or segment to deformation or lost excursion maintained around a compliant structure.

## First-draft authorization and limits

The reviewed evidence set authorizes a reader-facing first draft that:

- records the evidence-led change from the accepted outline's provisional lumbar lead to a knee lead;
- presents stance-phase walking and bounded loading as a multistudy construction rather than a case history;
- includes one dimensionally valid free-body relation without treating equilibrium as a unique internal solution;
- distinguishes every measured, calculated, modeled, inferred, reconstructed, hypothesized, and unknown object;
- shows direct human internal-force and local-strain evidence alongside prosthetic and measurement limits;
- pairs controlled geometry/contact redistribution with a true experimental null;
- makes ordinary local deformation a coequal outcome rather than a pre-damage stage;
- connects exposure to response only through receiver-specific, evidence-layer-labeled bridges;
- uses prospective structural associations without upgrading them to individual causation;
- includes alignment and imaging noncorrespondence;
- performs a substantive lumbar disc–facet–endplate transfer test;
- preserves all seven archive obligations and the wider axial program; and
- ends with support, narrowing, unresolved, and low-mechanical-priority outcomes.

It does not authorize:

- a universal alignment, posture, gait, curve, route, or local-strain optimum;
- native-knee inference from prosthetic measurements without an explicit transport limit;
- model peak as measured tissue history or damage;
- human thresholds inferred from cadaver, animal, explant, or cell evidence;
- geometry or imaging as diagnosis, prognosis, or symptom cause;
- osteoarthritis, disc degeneration, or back pain as a mechanically complete category;
- posterior or preferred configuration as the answer to the worked narrative;
- prevention, treatment, correction, exercise, self-test, referral, or individual care guidance; or
- claims of clinical utility before incremental-value evaluation.

Before publication, the packet and resulting chapter require:

1. knee joint, contact-mechanics, and gait-biomechanics review;
2. lumbar disc, facet, endplate, and spinal-load-sharing review;
3. formal mechanics, coordinate-frame, dimensional, and equation review;
4. finite-element verification, validation, uncertainty, and context-of-use review;
5. cartilage and disc mechanobiology review;
6. musculoskeletal radiology and imaging-correspondence review;
7. orthopedics, rheumatology, rehabilitation, causal-methods, and medical-coauthor review;
8. population, statistical, surrogate, and measurement-method review;
9. provenance review against `BGB-S-1104`, `BGB-S-1105`, `BGB-S-1110`, `BGB-S-1115`, `BGB-S-1117`, `BGB-S-1121`, and `BGB-S-1216`;
10. complete full-text metadata, page-level support, funding, conflict-of-interest, access, and newer-supportive-or-contrary-evidence refresh; and
11. figure anatomy, rights, caption, accessibility, uncertainty, and final-art review.
