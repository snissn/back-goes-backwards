# Chapter 10 evidence packet — mechanical homeostasis, capacity, dose, time, and environment

**Unit:** `BGB-U-0010` — *Mechanical Homeostasis, Capacity, Dose, Time, and Environment*

**Status:** source-grounded development packet as of 2026-07-11; sufficient for controlled salvage and a first draft; mechanobiology, occupational-biomechanics, medical, and formal-model review remain publication gates; not reader prose and not an evidence-status upgrade

**Companion controls:** [Chapter 10 brief](../chapter-briefs/BGB-U-0010-mechanical-homeostasis-capacity.md), [Book I contract](../book-contract.md), [Chapter 2 evidence packet](chapter-02-definitions-and-notation.md), `BGB-C-0005`, `BGB-M-0003`, `BGB-X-0001`, and the accepted Part III sequence

## Editorial decision

Chapter 10 can take a firm, useful position without pretending that the body has one measurable load meter:

> A visible task or peak force does not determine its mechanical meaning. Meaning depends on the exposure that actually reaches the selected system, that system's present task-specific capacity, the sequence and recovery history in which the exposure occurs, and the boundary conditions supplied by the environment. *Mechanical homeostasis* is the project's name for maintaining and recovering specified mechanical relations within viable, changing ranges—not for holding one posture or returning every body to one shape.

This statement combines three evidence levels that must remain distinguishable.

1. **Established mechanics:** magnitude, direction, rate, duration, repetition, sequence, and external contacts can change a mechanical exposure even when a task label or peak value is unchanged.
2. **Established but bounded biology:** cells and tissues sense and respond to mechanical conditions; experimental responses vary with tissue, state, magnitude, rate, frequency, duration, rest, and loading history.[^eichinger2021][^holzapfel2025][^lazarczuk2022][^mcbride2012]
3. **Project synthesis:** the book extends *mechanical homeostasis* into a whole-task reasoning framework that traces a declared task demand into a defined system's exposure history and relates that exposure to the system's present capacity. That extension is not yet a standardized whole-person measurement or a proof that one configuration is healthy.

The chapter establishes the positive baseline for Part III. It does not yet declare a failed handoff, stress concentration, corridor constraint, instability, compensation, biological injury, symptom cause, or corrective action. Those questions require the distinctions established here.

## The chapter's three boundaries

### Demand is not exposure

*Demand* names what the task and surroundings require at the chosen boundary: support an object, oppose gravity, accelerate a segment, remain within a workspace, or tolerate a contact. *Exposure* names the resulting mechanical history at the selected tissue, interface, segment, or modeled subsystem. The same object mass can produce different moments when distance, support, acceleration, or orientation changes. The same net joint moment can be shared differently among active, passive, pressure-mediated, skeletal, and external carriers. A task label therefore cannot substitute for a local exposure.

Chapter 10 should reuse Chapter 2's observation-to-inference ladder:

```text
task and environment
    -> measured external contacts, motion, and time history
    -> calculated segment forces and moments
    -> modeled internal sharing
    -> estimated local force, stress, strain, pressure, or deformation history
    -> separately evidenced biological or functional response
```

Calling an activity “light,” “repetitive,” “sedentary,” or “high impact” does not locate a dose. The draft must state which quantity reached which system, or admit that the local exposure is unknown.

### Capacity is not a trait label

In this chapter, *capacity* means the present ability of a defined system to meet and recover from a defined exposure under defined conditions. The word is deliberately relational. A person's ability to complete one lift is not the same as a tendon specimen's fatigue life, a muscle's maximal voluntary force, a joint's usable excursion, a person's ability to repeat the lift for an hour, or the time needed for a selected variable to recover afterward.

Capacity must therefore carry at least five qualifiers:

- **system:** tissue, interface, region, task system, or whole-person performance;
- **exposure:** quantity, direction, rate, magnitude, and configuration;
- **outcome:** completion, precision, stability, material integrity, symptom tolerance, or recovery;
- **state:** recent loading, development, training, illness, pain, sleep, medication, assistance, and other relevant modifiers; and
- **time:** one event, a sequence, a day, a training interval, or a longer trajectory.

The list of possible modifiers is not a causal checklist. Some change mechanics, some change biological response, some change perception or behavior, and some may be incidental. The chapter should name only modifiers relevant to its example and keep them in the correct column.

### Mechanical homeostasis is not one pose

Mechanical-homeostasis literature commonly concerns cells, engineered tissue equivalents, vessels, or other selected tissues seeking, maintaining, or restoring preferred mechanical states. Even in those bounded systems, the sensed variable and target may be uncertain, and target ranges can change.[^eichinger2021][^holzapfel2025] Book I borrows that disciplined idea but broadens its question: can a specified task system maintain or recover task-relevant mechanical relations within a viable range across changing exposures?

That broader use is authorized only when the prose names:

1. the system boundary and scale;
2. the task and environmental conditions;
3. the proposed regulated or observed variable;
4. the comparison, target, or viable range;
5. the time window and recovery criterion; and
6. what would count against the account.

A viable range may include motion, asymmetry, temporary deformation, fatigue, assistance, and more than one successful strategy. It is not synonymous with comfort, symmetry, health, normality, mechanical efficiency, or the preferred configuration proposed in Book II.

## Source-grounded mechanical kernels

### A range can move without becoming meaningless

Homeostasis is sometimes caricatured as rigid constancy. Eichinger and colleagues review mechanical homeostasis in cell-seeded tissue equivalents as a tendency to establish, maintain, or restore a preferred mechanical state, while emphasizing unresolved questions about what is sensed and regulated.[^eichinger2021] Holzapfel, Humphrey, and Ogden likewise describe mechanical homeostasis as central to tissue and organ modeling while stressing that mathematical and biological understanding remains incomplete.[^holzapfel2025]

This supports two moves and not a third. It supports speaking about mechanical variables that are regulated within ranges, and it supports asking how those targets or ranges change. It does not establish that the intact person has one global mechanical set point. Chapter 10's *viable range* is therefore an organizing model whose variables must be supplied case by case.

The recurring object transfer makes the distinction concrete. The person's joints, contacts, pressures, and muscle activity do not remain constant from approach through release. Viability means that the system can traverse and recover usable relations while completing the task; it does not mean freezing the body at the initial configuration. A handrail, cart, second person, or shelf contact can become part of that viable organization by changing the boundary rather than “correcting” the person.

### The peak does not contain the history

Chapter 2's exposure descriptor `BGB-M-0003` should be reused, not converted into an equation:

```text
E = {quantity, magnitude, direction, rate, duration, repetition,
     variability, sequence, task phase, rest, recovery interval,
     system boundary, state, and measurement uncertainty}
```

`E` is a structured record, not a number. Two histories can share the same peak and differ in duration. They can share nominal work and differ in rate, direction, or rest. They can contain the same events in another order and encounter a different state at the start of each event.

Human handgrip experiments provide a deliberately narrow example. Sonne and colleagues used ascending and descending submaximal force plateaus and found that the preceding force history affected measured fatigue accumulation and that some voluntary-force recovery appeared during a later lower-intensity plateau.[^sonne2015] The study does not produce a whole-body dose law. It demonstrates why a list of peaks loses sequence information.

NIOSH likewise treats intensity, frequency, and duration, along with combinations of force, posture, repetition, contact stress, vibration, and work organization, as distinct features of occupational exposure.[^niosh2024] The guidance is not a tissue-level causal model, but it supports the chapter's refusal to turn “more load” into one undifferentiated variable.

### Task success can coexist with a changed state

Enoka and Duchateau emphasize that muscle fatigue is task-specific and that a decline in maximal force or power capacity does not necessarily prevent continued submaximal performance.[^enoka2008] In a small repetitive-lifting study, van Dieën and colleagues observed substantial fatigue-related changes in muscle input-output characteristics while participants maintained an essentially constant movement performance and lumbosacral torque.[^vandieen1996]

These findings block two opposite mistakes. Continued performance does not prove that nothing changed, and a measured fatigue response does not prove tissue damage or task failure. Compensation, altered sharing, reserve use, practice, and ordinary variability are candidate explanations that require their own observations.

The Chapter 10 draft should track at least three columns during the repeated object transfer:

| Column | Example observation | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Task output | object reaches the same shelf | unchanged internal sharing, unchanged capacity, or absence of fatigue |
| Mechanical state | changed speed, contact, moment, activation, or movement | benefit, harm, injury, or symptom cause |
| Response | perceived effort, maximal-force change, recovery time, soreness, or tissue measure | one universal mechanism or diagnosis |

### Recovery is a process, not empty time

Rest means that a declared exposure has stopped or changed; it does not guarantee that every variable has returned to baseline. Recovery must name what is recovering and how it is measured.

O'Connell and colleagues applied axial creep loading and unloaded recovery to cadaveric human lumbar discs. Loading and recovery followed different time courses; in their protocol, recovery was several times slower than loading, and disc height and stiffness approached initial values only after long unloading intervals.[^oconnell2011] In living adults, quantitative MRI studies report diurnal changes in disc composition-related measures, reinforcing that time of measurement matters and that a snapshot cannot be assumed to be history-free.[^hamaguchi2023]

These disc studies are useful precisely because they are narrow. They do not specify a universal rest interval, prove that ordinary daytime loading is harmful, or permit disc behavior to stand in for muscle, tendon, cartilage, nerve, or the whole person. They show that exposure and recovery can be asymmetric and that a snapshot can depend on its place in a load history.

### Adaptation and damage are branches, not automatic opposites

Human training studies show that tendon properties can change following repeated loading and that outcomes vary with local strain and other program parameters. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Lazarczuk and colleagues found moderate changes in stiffness and large changes in modulus across healthy lower-limb tendon interventions, with higher-strain resistance protocols associated with larger changes; the evidence was concentrated in Achilles and patellar tendons and did not identify one universal optimum.[^lazarczuk2022]

Experimental literatures also show adverse branches. Shepherd and Screen review tendon-fatigue models in which repeated loading can produce progressive mechanical and microstructural changes, while noting that cellular responses and translation from models to tendinopathy remain complex.[^shepherd2013] Controlled animal loading studies reviewed by McBride and Silva distinguish adaptive lamellar bone formation from loading-associated injury responses, with magnitude, frequency, rest insertion, age, and model affecting the result.[^mcbride2012] Cartilage mechanobiology similarly varies with amplitude, rate, frequency, history, and tissue state.[^sanchezadams2014]

Chapter 10 should use those literatures only to authorize a fork:

```text
defined mechanical exposure
    -> no material observed change
    -> successful short-term accommodation
    -> practice or altered coordination
    -> longer-term adaptation or remodeling
    -> transient fatigue with recovery
    -> sensitization or symptom change
    -> accumulated material change or injury
    -> immediate task or material failure
```

The arrows do not form one mandatory sequence. Branches can overlap, occur at different scales, or remain unobserved. Chapter 19 owns the tissue-specific mechanobiological bridge; the worked narratives own any condition-specific application.

### Environment is part of the mechanics without becoming the culprit

The environment sets contacts, constraints, available supports, timing, and repetition. Seat and backrest, floor compliance, footwear, tool shape, screen position, carried object, vehicle vibration, work pace, sport rules, gravity, another person's assistance, and mobility equipment can all alter the boundary conditions of a task. They may lower one demand, add another, distribute it differently, or make an otherwise inaccessible task possible.

NIOSH's current ergonomics guidance explicitly asks evaluators to measure job-cycle timing, workstation layout, tools, vibration, and physiological or biomechanical responses and to consider combined exposure dimensions.[^niosh2024] Chapter 10 should retain the measurement logic but not turn it into a workstation checklist or an indictment of “modern life.” Prolonged sitting, device use, repetitive work, and specialized sport are exposure contexts whose consequences depend on task, fit, duration, variability, support, state, and outcome.

Assistance belongs in the same account. A cane, orthosis, exoskeleton, cart, handrail, chair, or second person changes the mechanical system. It is neither an incidental afterthought nor evidence of a deficient body. The chapter's system diagram should visibly include these external carriers.

### A changing margin is useful only if its terms stay separate

The project needs language for the relation among task-level demand, the exposure that reaches a selected system, and that system's present capacity, but it does not yet have a validated scalar function. The draft may draw task demand, exposure, and capacity as separate, time-varying records or trajectories. It should not calculate “reserve” by subtracting quantities with different units or by assigning hidden numbers to unmeasured states.

The following notation is permitted as a schematic relation:

```text
task-level demand D_task(t, objective, external conditions)
through configuration and boundary conditions
    B(t, contacts, constraints, support, environment)
becomes a measured or modeled exposure
    E(t, system, quantity, direction, rate, history)

viability for a declared outcome is evaluated from E
relative to
    C(t, same system, outcome, direction, recovery window)
```

`D_task`, `B`, `E`, and `C` remain records or model inputs unless a bounded study defines commensurate variables. Task demand is defined at its chosen boundary; exposure and capacity must refer to the same selected system and declared outcome. A threshold may be sharp for a tested specimen under a stated loading mode, gradual for a task-performance endpoint, or unknown for a whole-person outcome. The same exposure may be below one threshold and above another.

*Reserve* can appear only as a conditional systems concept: the currently available margin to preserve a declared function while accommodating perturbation or additional demand. It must not become an invisible substance that is “depleted” whenever the author dislikes a posture.

## Opening comparison for the first draft

Return once to the familiar object transfer but change time rather than technique. Keep object, destination, and approximate external route similar across four hypothetical trials:

1. the first transfer after an ordinary rest period;
2. the same transfer late in a long sequence;
3. the transfer after an interval too short to assume recovery; and
4. the transfer after familiarity, changed support, or assistance has altered how the task is shared.

The comparison should not announce which trial is good or bad. It should ask what would have to be measured before the trials could be called equivalent: contact forces, motion and speed, moments, activation, perceived effort, performance, local tissue exposure, or recovery. This is the last use of the full recurring object-transfer narrative. Later chapters may mention a phase or result but should not replay the complete scene.

## Figure obligations

1. **Same task, different history.** Four panels with the same nominal transfer and different preceding exposure/recovery states. Caption distinguishes hypothetical teaching comparison from measured evidence.
2. **Exposure record, not dose score.** Parallel timelines with matched peaks but different duration, sequence, direction, variability, or rest. No summed “good/bad” meter.
3. **Demand, exposure, capacity, and boundary conditions through time.** Keep the external task requirement distinct from the selected system's exposure and capacity; use no unsupported subtraction. Include assistance as a boundary change.
4. **Conditional outcome fork.** Several possible responses from a defined exposure, with tissue-specific biological arrows explicitly deferred to Chapter 19.

No figure may imply one optimal posture, an all-purpose capacity threshold, cumulative damage from every repetition, or a direct arrow from mechanical exposure to disease.

## Model obligations

### `BGB-M-0003` — time-structured exposure descriptor

1. **Object represented:** A mechanical exposure history.
2. **Boundary and scale:** A declared task, interface, segment, tissue estimate, or measured subsystem.
3. **Variables:** Named force, moment, stress, strain, pressure, deformation, activation, or motion quantities plus direction, rate, duration, repetition, sequence, variability, and recovery intervals as relevant.
4. **Assumptions:** Reference frame, sampling period, phase segmentation, internal-sharing model, and relevance to the selected outcome.
5. **Explains:** Why matching a peak, task name, or nominal total does not make histories mechanically identical.
6. **Cannot establish:** Harm, benefit, adaptation, biological dose, symptom causality, diagnosis, or a universal cumulative score.
7. **Failure condition:** Revise when the selected descriptors fail to distinguish or predict the bounded exposure question.
8. **Registry action:** Reuse; do not allocate a duplicate model.

### Viable-range model — candidate to register after first-draft audit

1. **Object represented:** Maintenance and recovery of named mechanical relations during and after a task.
2. **Boundary and scale:** Must be declared for every use.
3. **Variables:** Observed or modeled states, proposed range, perturbation, and recovery criterion.
4. **Assumptions:** Viability is outcome- and time-specific; more than one strategy may satisfy it.
5. **Explains:** Why function can involve motion and variation rather than a fixed configuration.
6. **Cannot establish:** One ideal posture, health status, preferred configuration, or disease cause.
7. **Failure condition:** Narrow or discard if its variables cannot be operationalized or established stability/tolerance constructs fully perform the intended work.
8. **Registry action:** Remain provisional until model review.

### Demand–exposure–capacity relation — candidate schematic, not equation

1. **Object represented:** A route from task-level demand through configuration and boundary conditions to a defined exposure, followed by a time-varying comparison between that exposure and present ability to meet a defined outcome.
2. **Boundary and scale:** Task demand is declared at the chosen external boundary; exposure and capacity must refer to the same selected system and outcome.
3. **Variables:** Remain separately measured, modeled, or inferred.
4. **Assumptions:** Configuration, state, history, recovery window, and environment are explicit.
5. **Explains:** Conditional tolerance, changing task availability, and why snapshots can mislead.
6. **Cannot establish:** A hidden whole-person reserve, a universal failure cliff, or causal relation to pain.
7. **Failure condition:** Reject any subtraction, ratio, or score whose terms are not commensurate and validated.
8. **Registry action:** Keep as explanatory graphic pending formalization.

## Counterexamples, nulls, and falsifiers

| Tempting inference | Counterexample or alternative | Drafting consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Repetition necessarily produces adaptation | no observed change, fatigue, substitution, or damage may occur | present a fork and name the observed outcome |
| Repetition necessarily produces cumulative damage | ordinary exposure may be tolerated, recovered from, or adaptive | do not call chronology pathology |
| Successful task completion proves adequate capacity | output may be preserved while effort, sharing, or later recovery changes | measure the capacity endpoint being claimed |
| Fatigue proves failure or injury | submaximal performance can continue; fatigue mechanisms are task-specific | keep fatigue, task failure, material damage, and injury separate |
| Rest equals complete recovery | variables recover at different rates and some processing continues | state the variable and criterion |
| One posture is the homeostatic state | viable activity may include motion, asymmetry, and several strategies | define ranges and tasks, not silhouettes |
| A difficult environment is the cause | environment may be one boundary among biological, psychological, social, and historical factors | describe the changed contacts and demands |
| More variability is always protective | consistency may serve precision; variability may add exposure or reflect instability | treat variability as a descriptor, not a virtue |
| A threshold exists, so it applies to the person | specimen, tissue, performance, symptom, and whole-person thresholds differ | retain the tested scale and endpoint |
| Symptoms reveal the mechanical threshold crossing | symptoms can precede, follow, or vary independently of the selected measure | require a separate symptom bridge |

Evidence against the chapter's synthesis would include failure to define meaningful task-level variables; inability of the viable-range account to add anything beyond existing stability, tolerance, or control concepts; or repeated evidence that the proposed demand, exposure, and capacity descriptors do not distinguish bounded outcomes. Such findings should narrow or replace the model rather than be redescribed as hidden compensation.

## First-draft authorization

A controlled Chapter 10 draft is authorized if it:

- opens with the same nominal task under different histories rather than with injury or posture correction;
- distinguishes external demand, internal exposure, response, and outcome;
- uses *mechanical homeostasis* as an explicitly bounded tissue concept and project-level whole-task synthesis;
- defines capacity relationally at the same system, direction, outcome, and time window as the exposure being considered;
- reuses `BGB-M-0003` as a multidimensional record and never presents it as a universal score;
- makes task success compatible with fatigue or altered sharing without inferring hidden damage;
- treats recovery as variable-specific and time-dependent;
- shows adaptation, no change, fatigue, sensitization, remodeling, injury, and failure as conditional branches rather than a predetermined continuum;
- includes environment and assistance as boundary conditions without pathologizing modern life or disability;
- leaves task demand, resulting exposure, capacity, and boundary conditions separately inspectable unless a bounded validated model relates commensurate variables;
- contains no ergonomic advice, break schedule, self-test, movement cue, prevention protocol, diagnosis, or treatment; and
- hands Chapter 11 a positive baseline: only after demand, exposure, capacity, boundary, and chronology are named can a changed route or handoff be interpreted.

The drafting center is constructive: living mechanical systems operate through changing ranges, histories, and supports. The limiting center is equally important: neither a snapshot nor a nominal task reveals whether a specific tissue is adapting, fatiguing, compensating, recovering, becoming injured, or simply doing ordinary work.
