# Whole-Person Alternatives and Modifiers Matrix

**Status:** shared Book I development control

**Primary owners:** Chapters 1, 10, 16, and 21

**Required use:** Chapters 18–27 and any medically exposed claim, figure, or worked narrative

## Purpose

Book I argues that load history and load routing deserve explicit attention alongside biological, psychological, social, developmental, behavioral, and environmental factors. Those additional factors should change the actual mechanical and causal account; they should not appear as a ceremonial list after the preferred explanation has already been selected.

This matrix places whole-person variables at the node or bridge they can affect. It preserves mechanical reasoning as a substantive lens while preventing a mechanically coherent story from becoming complete merely because it is coherent.

The matrix is not a compulsory taxonomy of causes. A variable can be an antecedent, modifier, mediator, consequence, common cause, measurement influence, or incidental context at different times. The chapter or case should state the role being proposed and allow that role to change as the chronology changes.

## Relation roles

Use these terms only when they clarify the account. They are prompts, not labels that every case must receive.

| Relation role | Working question | Example form | Required caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Antecedent | Was this present before the selected mechanical or clinical event and did it change its probability or form? | Prior injury changes available contact or confidence | Temporal order alone does not establish cause |
| Modifier | Does this change the magnitude, direction, duration, interpretation, or consequence of another relation? | Sleep changes recovery after the same exposure | A modifier can be substantial without being the initiating cause |
| Mediator | Is this an intermediate through which one supported relation affects a later endpoint? | Local deformation alters a tissue-specific physiological variable | Every segment of the mediating chain requires independent support |
| Consequence | Did this arise after the selected event and alter the future starting state? | Pain changes task strategy and later demand | A consequence can later become a feedback input |
| Common cause | Could one factor produce both the mechanical observation and the endpoint? | Inflammatory state alters tissue capacity and movement | Co-occurrence does not select the proposed route |
| Reverse path | Could the downstream finding, symptom, diagnosis, or treatment have produced the present mechanical state? | Symptoms lead to guarding or reduced excursion | Present geometry cannot recover chronology by itself |
| Access or option constraint | Does this alter which strategies, supports, environments, or recovery resources are available? | Work control or assistive access changes task variability | Do not redescribe structural constraints as personal choice |
| Measurement influence | Does this affect what was reported, observed, sampled, or modeled? | Attention, test context, or referral filters alter the record | Measurement influence does not mean the experience is unreal |
| Incidental context | Is this present but unsupported as important to the selected relation? | Anatomical variation without exposure or functional correspondence | Context should remain visible without being forced into the mechanism |

No single term should be used to rank all mechanical contribution. The useful task is to identify which relation is supported for the selected question, outcome, and time.

## Lifespan and body-context controls

Development, pregnancy, disability, illness, injury, training, and aging change the system being modeled. They are not deviations from one canonical adult body.

| Context | Mechanical relevance to preserve | Interpretation boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Development and growth | Geometry, proportions, material behavior, control, experience, and available strategies change over time | Variation in route or timing is not automatically delay, deficit, or dysfunction |
| Training and repeated practice | Strength, endurance, tissue capacity, timing, stiffness, confidence, skill, and task selection may change | Adaptation is outcome-specific; repetition does not guarantee improvement |
| Pregnancy and postpartum change | Mass distribution, joint state, pressure, fatigue, task demands, and support may change | One reference posture, recovery sequence, or mechanical meaning does not apply universally |
| Disability and assistive use | Contacts, external supports, available paths, task goals, and successful strategies may differ | Assistance and asymmetry are not inferior compensation by definition |
| Injury and rehabilitation history | Material state, sensation, confidence, capacity, and learned strategies may alter later demand | Present behavior may be protection, consequence, preference, or unrelated rather than persistent defect |
| Acute or chronic illness | Capacity, recovery, circulation, neural control, fatigue, and priorities may change | Mechanical change need not be initiating or clinically primary |
| Aging | Geometry, strength, tissue state, sensory function, experience, pacing, support, and reserve may change in different directions | Population averages do not determine individual decline or posterior insufficiency |

## Alternatives by Part III family

| Mechanical family | Whole-person alternatives or modifiers that belong inside the model | Required nonconfirming possibility |
|---|---|---|
| Dose and capacity | Sleep, illness, inflammation, prior exposure, medication, nutrition, stress, task control, recovery opportunity | The nominal exposure is tolerated, or another process better explains reduced capacity |
| Continuity, interruption, and leakage | Pain behavior, learned strategy, assistance, anatomy, preference, environmental support, measurement limits | The proposed route is unnecessary, remains available, or no meaningful redistribution occurs |
| Redistribution and concentration | Active control, tissue state, geometry variation, external support, common task driver | Visible configuration changes without a material local-field difference |
| Maintained constraint | Intrinsic tissue change, inflammation, scar, developmental anatomy, ordinary positional variation, active regulation | Deformation recovers, excursion remains adequate, or function does not correspond |
| Instability and discoordination | Pain, confidence, sensory state, fatigue, learning, attention, material laxity, task novelty | Laxity with good function, altered performance without laxity, or successful variability |
| Compensation and rerouting | Goal, preference, assistance, disability adaptation, environmental constraint, symptom consequence | The strategy is useful, neutral, transient, or unrelated to later cost |
| Scale transition | Shared task, common biological state, global adaptation, reverse causality, measurement covariance | Local change stays local, global pattern has no selected local effect, or direction is reversed |

## How context changes a mechanical model

Whole-person integration should lead to at least one concrete change in the model or interpretation. Examples include:

- changing the system boundary because an assistive device or external support carries part of the demand;
- changing the available-path set because anatomy, pain, disability, environment, or task goal differs;
- changing the exposure history because work control, caregiving, training, or recovery opportunity differs;
- changing material or active-state assumptions because inflammation, prior injury, illness, medication, fatigue, or training differs;
- changing the selected outcome because comfort, participation, endurance, tissue response, or clinical risk are not interchangeable;
- adding a reverse arrow because symptoms, diagnosis, or treatment may have changed later mechanics;
- adding a common-cause branch because one biological or contextual variable can affect both mechanics and outcome;
- reducing causal confidence because measurement, selection, or missing history differs; or
- concluding that a mechanical account is low priority for the question being asked.

A paragraph that merely states “biological, psychological, and social factors also matter” without changing any node, arrow, assumption, alternative, or conclusion has not performed this integration.

## Worked-narrative prompts

For Chapters 23–26, answer only the prompts that materially affect the case:

1. What could have produced the observed configuration besides the proposed mechanical route?
2. What changes the estimated internal distribution despite a similar external task?
3. What changes the tissue's present response to the same candidate exposure?
4. What could make the finding and symptom correspond, diverge, or reverse in time?
5. What constraint or resource changes the person's available strategies and recovery?
6. What could make compensation protective, neutral, necessary, or consequence rather than cost?
7. What observed result would lower the priority of the mechanical account?
8. Which remaining uncertainty is an absent measurement, and which is genuine causal plurality?

The answers should enter the case timeline and evidence table. They should not be collected into a generic “biopsychosocial considerations” subsection detached from the mechanism.

## Figure and language rules

1. Put modifiers and alternatives beside the node or arrow they affect.
2. Do not draw a large surrounding “context” cloud that leaves the central mechanical cascade unchanged.
3. Show assistance, environment, and task design as boundary conditions where appropriate.
4. Use several viable bodies and strategies; do not depict one body as the mechanical norm.
5. Distinguish experience from tissue response without treating either as less real.
6. Do not use *psychological* as shorthand for unexplained or nonmechanical.
7. Do not use *mechanical* as shorthand for objective, simple, local, or primary.
8. State whether a variable is observed, measured, inferred, hypothesized, or reconstructed.
9. Permit roles and causal directions to change through time.
10. Keep social and environmental constraints as causal inputs when they alter exposure or options, not as personal-background decoration.

## Acceptance criteria

This matrix is functioning when:

- whole-person variables alter named nodes, arrows, assumptions, options, or conclusions;
- mechanics remains a substantive explanatory lens rather than disappearing into generic holism;
- no factor receives a permanent causal rank outside a selected question and time;
- development, pregnancy, disability, illness, injury, training, and aging are modeled as different systems rather than deviations;
- symptoms and disease can alter later mechanics through explicit reverse paths;
- assistance, access, work control, and environment can change boundary conditions and available strategies;
- every medically exposed chapter includes a real low-mechanical-priority or nonconfirming possibility;
- context is not confined to a disclaimer subsection; and
- a coherent mechanical narrative can be narrowed or disfavored without erasing the broader research program.
