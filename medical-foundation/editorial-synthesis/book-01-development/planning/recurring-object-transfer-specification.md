# Recurring Object-Transfer Specification

**Control object:** `BGB-X-0001` — moving and carrying an object

**Status:** selected book-wide teaching task; exact prop and visual casting remain open to figure review

**Primary use:** Chapters 1–8

**Permitted callbacks:** Chapters 10–12 and 27

## Why this task recurs

Book I needs one ordinary event that can become more legible as the reader gains new tools. The task is therefore not a demonstration of correct lifting, a diagnostic test, or a miniature proof of the posterior hypothesis. It is a stable object of inquiry: a person moves an ordinary object from one support to another and carries it several steps.

The task is simple enough to redraw around different system boundaries and rich enough to contain contact, gravity, acceleration, moments, changing support, active force, pressure, material deformation, balance, sensation, fatigue, environment, and more than one viable solution. Each return should add one layer and preserve the layers already earned.

## Canonical scene

A person standing on a level surface moves an ordinary portable object from a support near ordinary working height, carries it several steps, and places it on a second support. The default scene should feel like daily life rather than sport, therapy, or industrial labor.

The final prop may be a small box, basket, bag, or comparably neutral container. Figure review should choose one form that:

- makes hand–object and object–support contacts visible;
- can be shown at rest, accelerating, carried, and placed;
- does not imply a standardized occupational load;
- does not require a specialized grip or athletic technique;
- remains recognizable in whole-body and simplified mechanical drawings; and
- can be varied in mass or handle geometry without becoming a different task.

No numerical mass, height, speed, or distance is canonical until a worked calculation or evidence source requires it. “Comfortably manageable” is a scene-setting condition, not an assertion about universal capacity.

## Canonical phases

| Phase | Observable event | Mechanical change made available | What the phase must not imply |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Initial support | Object rests on the first support; person has not yet accepted its weight | Object–support contact and the initial boundary conditions are visible | A required starting posture or symmetrical stance |
| 1. Contact and acceptance | Person establishes contact and begins to change how the object is supported | A new contact and a changing division of support appear | That grip alone reveals internal loading |
| 2. Departure | Object leaves the first support | Support reaction transfers from the surface to the person–object relation; acceleration may be nonzero | Static equilibrium during the full lift |
| 3. Redirection or stabilization | Object velocity or direction changes before carrying | Force, moment, active control, yielding, and stiffening can change by phase | One tissue or side acting alone |
| 4. Carry | Person and object travel several steps | Repeated support exchange, balance, locomotion, and changing whole-body configuration enter | That a visually steady object means constant internal demand |
| 5. Placement | Object contacts the second support and its weight is progressively transferred | Deceleration, shared support, controlled release, and another role exchange become visible | That lowering is simply lifting in reverse |
| 6. Release and aftermath | Person releases contact; object remains supported | The task ends while fatigue, sensation, or changed capacity may persist | Injury, benefit, or recovery from one ordinary repetition |

The phases are an editorial grammar, not a required motion-capture segmentation. A chapter may combine phases when the distinction does no work.

## System-boundary ladder

Every diagram or passage should name its selected system before naming forces or paths.

| Boundary | Typical external interactions | Legitimate teaching job | Inference still unavailable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Person plus object | Gravity, ground reaction, first or second support contact where present | Whole-event momentum and external support accounting | Person–object contact as an external force; local tissue exposure |
| Person alone | Gravity, ground reaction, object contact, other external support | Demand entering the person and changing support conditions | A unique internal route or joint force |
| Object alone | Gravity, hand contact, support contact | Acceptance, acceleration, hold, deceleration, and release | How the person produces the contact force |
| Selected body segment | Neighboring joint/contact resultants, gravity, external contact where applicable | Segment force and moment accounting | Exact muscle sharing or tissue stress |
| Joint or interface | Contact, muscle/tendon action, ligamentous restraint, geometry | Moment, contact, restraint, and load-sharing hypotheses | A unique pressure distribution without geometry and material assumptions |
| Tissue or local region | Boundary traction, pressure, constraint, active state, material relation | Stress, strain, pressure, deformation, or excursion model | Biological response, symptoms, diagnosis, or outcome without further bridges |

This ladder implements `BGB-M-0001` and `BGB-M-0002`. Moving inward does not merely magnify the same quantity; it changes the model, required inputs, and uncertainty.

## Baseline and controlled variants

The baseline should remain intentionally unoptimized. Variants change one editorially relevant feature at a time where possible.

| Variant family | Candidate contrast | Principal use | Required caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Support | Unsupported object versus partial assistance or a sliding support | Boundary conditions; compensation; external design | Assistance is not inherently better or worse |
| Object position | Near versus farther from the body; low versus higher support | Moment arms and configuration | Visible distance does not determine tissue stress |
| Time | Hold, smooth acceleration, faster redirection, repeated transfers | Rate, repetition, duration, fatigue, and recovery | Do not create a universal dose score |
| Locomotion | Stationary transfer versus several steps | Changing foot contact and whole-body coordination | Gait variation is not pathology |
| Grip and object form | Handles, no handles, one- or two-hand contact | Contact options and task constraints | Do not moralize symmetry or prescribe grip |
| Environment | Clear path, constrained reach, different support height, time pressure | Boundary conditions and available strategies | Environment can contribute without being the sole cause |
| Person state | Rested versus fatigued; familiar versus unfamiliar task | Capacity and history | State differences do not establish injury or deficit |
| Strategy | More than one viable configuration for the same external task | Nonuniqueness and load partition | One version must not be visually coded as the correct form |

Population variation should be represented deliberately across the figure set. Body size, age, disability, pregnancy, use of assistance, and movement strategy change the available system; they should not be introduced only as deviations from one canonical body.

## Chapter-by-chapter accumulation

| Chapter | New work performed on the same task | Repetition limit |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Mechanical dimension | Show that mechanical, biological, experiential, and environmental descriptions coexist in one embodied event | No equations or internal-route claims |
| 2. Language of load | Change system boundaries; distinguish force, moment, pressure, stress, strain, deformation, dose, work, and transfer | Do not become a glossary or infer local exposure from object weight |
| 3. Hard and soft roles | Map simultaneous shape-preserving and form-receiving roles and show that roles can exchange by phase and direction | Do not color tissues permanently hard or soft |
| 4. Configuration and paths | Compare room, segment, joint, and tissue frames; show geometry-dependent multicarrier transfer | Do not turn the task into Book II's signed generalized-mode derivation |
| 5. Skeleton and joints | Trace changing contact and relatively shape-preserving skeletal possibilities | Do not portray bone as an isolated load-bearing frame |
| 6. Active, tensile, compliant, and pressure systems | Add muscle, tendon, ligament, cartilage, fascia, interfaces, and pressure-mediated support as interacting roles | Do not use component continuity as proof of one body-wide cable |
| 7. Posterior anatomy | Identify bounded posterior structures that can participate at selected phases and scales | Anatomy and large muscle size do not prove universal priority |
| 8. Ordinary posterior function | Vary position, rate, support, walking, and fatigue to compare task-specific posterior contribution with other viable strategies | No ideal technique, posterior prescription, or injury inference |
| 9. Whole body and lifespan | Recall the completed trace only long enough to place it among development, disability, pregnancy, illness, aging, and reserve | Do not restart the task or make one body the norm |
| 10–12 callbacks | Reuse selected variants for dose/capacity, changed transfer, or concentration | One compact callback per mechanism; no full retelling |
| 27 callback | Use the familiar task to demonstrate the final reasoning questions | The questions must not become a self-test or score |

After Chapter 8, the task should visibly yield to the adverse-organization taxonomy and the worked narratives. Chapter 9 marks that transition. Later callbacks should feel like retrieval of a known control object, not a return to the book's opening example because a new example was unavailable.

## Hard/soft use within the task

Hard and soft are relational mechanical roles motivated by TCM hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts; they are not fixed tissue classes or an exact translation of the traditional terms.

Within one phase, a relatively hard role preserves or resolves a task-relevant relation while a relatively soft role receives form, conforms, glides, contains, expands, buffers, or otherwise remains available to change. The same tissue or assembly can combine roles in different directions, or exchange them as the task changes.

The task may illustrate a candidate handoff among active tissue, tendon or ligament, bone, and joint geometry, but it must also show branching and simultaneous support. It may not stand as evidence that every healthy load follows one anatomical sequence.

## Representation and provenance rules

Every serious use of the task should distinguish as applicable:

- **observed:** visible task, contact, position, timing, or qualitative motion;
- **measured:** instrument-derived force, motion, pressure, activity, or other quantity;
- **calculated:** result obtained from declared measured or assumed inputs;
- **modeled:** internal quantity dependent on a stated mechanical representation;
- **inferred:** proposed but not directly observed relation;
- **hypothesized:** project claim awaiting formal or empirical test; and
- **embodied:** reported felt organization or internal connection, preserved as observation rather than converted into a measured force path.

Illustrations should use stable redundant notation for these states. A smooth line through the body must never make an inferred multicarrier path look like a directly observed cable.

## Figure and continuity program

The initial figure family remains `BGB-FIG-01-001` through `BGB-FIG-01-005`. Later figures may reuse the same silhouette, object, supports, phase labels, and boundary notation, but should add only the layer owned by the chapter.

Continuity depends on visual restraint:

1. Keep the prop, general scene, and phase names recognizable.
2. Change the system boundary or explanatory overlay rather than redrawing a wholly different task.
3. Preserve uncertainty notation as the view moves from external demand toward internal exposure.
4. Show at least two viable strategies before any illustration could be read as posture instruction.
5. Encode mechanical roles on relations or states, not as permanent anatomical colors.
6. End the recurring figure family before Part III's adverse examples become dominant.

## Evidence and review obligations

The qualitative scene requires mechanical correctness even when it has no numerical claims. Any number, anatomical moment arm, joint resultant, muscle force, contact distribution, pressure, stress, strain, energy cost, or fatigue effect requires an identified source, calculation, or validated model.

Before final illustration or prose production, the task needs:

- mechanics review of every free body, boundary crossing, sign convention, and dynamic/static assumption;
- anatomy and biomechanics review of any internal overlay;
- accessibility review of the chosen object, body representations, assistance, and text alternatives;
- figure-rights and asset-provenance review; and
- authorial review of any embodied force-line panel.

## Excluded uses

`BGB-X-0001` must not be used to establish:

- one correct lifting or carrying technique;
- one ideal posture or configuration;
- a universal posterior priority;
- a unique tissue-level load path;
- an injury threshold or disease mechanism;
- the preferred-configuration hypothesis;
- a diagnostic observation, home test, corrective cue, or exercise; or
- superiority of an unassisted, symmetrical, youthful, or nondisabled body.

## Completion criteria

The recurring task is production-ready when:

- the prop and base scene have been selected without changing the mechanical specification;
- phases and system boundaries are used consistently across Chapters 1–8;
- every chapter adds a unique layer and obeys its repetition limit;
- at least two viable task strategies and one assisted or environmentally modified strategy are represented;
- the model/evidence state of every internal arrow is visible;
- the task has a deliberate endpoint after Part II; and
- technical, accessibility, intent, and rights reviews have been assigned.
