# Adverse-Organization Family Matrix

**Status:** shared Book I development control

**Primary owners:** Chapters 10–16

**Required use:** Chapters 18 and 22–27; any evidence pack or figure that combines more than one Part III family

## Purpose

Part III develops several ways that mechanical demand can be managed, redistributed, maintained, or compensated through time. These are analytical views of a task and system, not diagnoses, permanent body types, or competing names for one hidden defect.

This matrix keeps the families distinct while allowing them to coexist. It also prevents *adverse* from being assigned by appearance alone. A relation becomes a candidate adverse organization only when the task, mechanical quantity, dose, relevant capacity, and lost function or changed exposure have been specified. The same observable configuration can be useful, neutral, costly, or unrelated in different conditions.

The matrix inherits:

- the quantity, scale, and model boundaries in the [causal-chain and evidence-state grammar](causal-chain-and-evidence-state-grammar.md);
- the changing shape-preserving and form-receiving roles in Chapters 3 and 11;
- the multidimensional dose and dynamic-capacity baseline in Chapter 10; and
- the authorial principle that load should preferentially be resolved through mechanically capable relations so that form-receiving structures retain the freedom needed to yield, conform, glide, contain, expand, and recover.

The hard/soft distinction is **motivated by TCM hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts but is not definitionally identical to them**. In Book I it is an explicit mechanical polarity open to formal and empirical revision.

## Governing distinctions

### Baseline before an adverse family

Chapter 10 supplies the comparison condition rather than one more failure label. A route, exposure, deformation, strategy, or variation cannot be called adverse until it is compared with:

- a declared task and phase;
- a named force, moment, pressure, stress, strain, deformation, excursion, activation, or other quantity;
- direction, rate, duration, repetition, variability, sequence, and recovery as relevant;
- capacity at the same tissue, interface, segment, task, or person scale;
- current biological and material state;
- environmental support and constraint; and
- a selected functional, mechanical, biological, or medical outcome.

Mechanical homeostasis is the proposed maintenance and recovery of task-relevant relations within viable dynamic ranges. It is not a single posture, symmetry target, fixed set point, or assurance that every exposure produces adaptation.

### Family is not consequence

An interruption, concentration, constraint, instability, or compensation may be mechanically intelligible without producing injury, symptoms, diagnosis, or reduced function. Conversely, symptoms or disease can alter configuration, control, capacity, and strategy. Part III describes organization; Chapters 19–21 own the bridges to tissue response and medical meaning.

### Families are views, not exclusive bins

One event may include several families. A maintained constraint may alter distribution, provoke substitution, and change timing. Those are separate propositions requiring separate evidence states and alternatives. Adding labels does not strengthen the account unless each view identifies a different measurable object.

## Hard/soft as a cross-family lens

Hard and soft do not add another diagnosis or taxonomy family. They ask how mechanical labor is divided in a stated direction and phase.

| Question | Required specification | Permitted interpretation | Interpretation to prevent |
|---|---|---|---|
| What must preserve a relation? | Task, direction, timescale, geometry, and required stability | Bone, joint contact, active stiffening, tensile assembly, pressure-mediated assembly, or a combination may perform a relatively shape-preserving role | Bone is always hard; every other tissue is soft |
| What must remain available to receive form? | Required yielding, conformity, glide, containment, expansion, pressure response, or recovery | A tissue or assembly may perform a relatively form-receiving role while still bearing and transmitting substantial load | Soft means weak, unloaded, passive, or medically vulnerable |
| What would constitute a mismatch? | Named quantity, receiver, lost freedom, dose, capacity, and consequence or disfavoring result | Demand may be poorly matched when it persistently occupies a structure or role whose required freedom is thereby reduced | Any deformation, anterior loading, pressure, or deviation is misrouting |
| Can the roles exchange? | Phase, activation, pressure, direction, and material state | The same tissue or assembly may combine or exchange shape-preserving and form-receiving roles | Yin/Yang or hard/soft is a permanent tissue map |

The project-native shorthand—*load wants to travel through the hard body so that the soft body can remain soft*—is retained as an authorial organizing intuition. In publication prose it should be unpacked into the task-specific relation above. It does not mean that soft tissues bear no force or that skeletal transmission is the only legitimate route.

## Discrimination sequence for a chapter or case

Before applying a family label, work through this sequence:

1. **Name the event.** Define the person or population, task, phase, environment, contacts, and relevant history.
2. **Choose the system and scale.** State what crosses the boundary and what remains internal.
3. **Name the quantity.** Replace generic *load* with force, moment, pressure, stress, strain, deformation, excursion, activation, work, or a clearly labeled candidate demand.
4. **Establish the baseline.** Specify dose, capacity, state, time, recovery, and the selected outcome.
5. **Identify what changed.** Separate route availability, regional distribution, local concentration, maintained excursion, stability, timing, strategy, and scale.
6. **Name the carrier and receiver.** For leakage, substitution, or constraint, identify what relation carries the demand and what structure or freedom receives the changed exposure.
7. **Assign evidence states.** Mark each observation, measurement, calculation, model, inference, hypothesis, embodied observation, and reconstructed interval.
8. **Place alternatives at the affected link.** Include normal variation, useful adaptation, reverse causality, common cause, model failure, and no meaningful mechanical contribution where applicable.
9. **State what the family adds.** A second family label should explain a distinct object or prediction, not intensify the first label rhetorically.
10. **Stop at the supported endpoint.** Do not cross from mechanical organization to tissue response, symptoms, diagnosis, or treatment without the corresponding Chapter 19–21 bridge.

## Worked-narrative allocation

The Part V chapters are tests of the matrix, not illustrations required to confirm it.

| Chapter | Families expected to carry the main analytical work | Required comparison or failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| 23. Geometry and localized strain | Redistribution, concentration, dose/capacity, and tissue-response boundary | Visible difference without meaningful exposure; concentration without symptom or damage; reverse causality |
| 24. Maintained constraint | Maintained deformation or reduced excursion, hard/soft mismatch, possible concentration, and functional branch | Narrowing without demonstrated maintained constraint; deformation that recovers; low-mechanical or noncorresponding outcome |
| 25. Instability and repeated demand | Instability, discoordination, changing capacity, compensation, and recurrence through time | Laxity with good function; functional difficulty without gross laxity; useful or neutral compensation; exposure-led recurrence |
| 26. Silent compensation and mixed chronic presentation | Compensation, scale transition, cumulative dose, changing reserve, and mixed causal chronology | Symptoms preceding strategy; no correspondence; another explanation doing more work; unresolved plurality |

Chapter 24 owns the most direct test of the author's hard/soft proposition: whether an ongoing relation imposes strain on a form-receiving conduit, sliding interface, or compliant structure and thereby changes its task-relevant freedom. The consequential object is not simply a narrowed space. It is the maintained mechanical relation, resulting deformation or lost excursion, and separately tested functional effect.

## Figure rules

1. Give every family its own visual symbol and definition; do not use one red pinch or broken arrow for all adverse states.
2. Show the system boundary and phase before internal paths.
3. Label the mechanical quantity on each consequential arrow.
4. Distinguish regional redistribution from local stress or strain fields.
5. Distinguish concentrated exposure from a relation that maintains deformation through time.
6. Show instability and discoordination as separate panels unless their relation is itself supported.
7. Begin compensation figures with the function preserved.
8. Draw scale transitions with intermediate nodes; never use a single local-to-systemic arrow.
9. Include useful, neutral, reverse, and null branches where the figure's teaching job reaches consequence.
10. Keep mechanical organization visually upstream from tissue response, function, symptoms, and medical interpretation.

## Repetition and ownership limits

- Chapter 10 owns the viability baseline. Later chapters apply dose and capacity rather than redefining them.
- Chapter 11 owns route-state terms, leakage, and substitution at one task or phase.
- Chapter 12 owns redistribution and concentration across nested mechanical scales.
- Chapter 13 owns maintained constraint, lost excursion, and extrinsic/intrinsic/mixed time states.
- Chapter 14 owns instability, timing, sequencing, and sensory-control mismatch.
- Chapter 15 owns compensation through time and begins every account with preserved function.
- Chapter 16 owns cross-scale transitions and separation of physiological branches.
- Chapters 19–21 own tissue response, causal scope, and medical interpretation.
- Chapters 23–26 may combine families only by citing these definitions and supplying case-specific evidence.
- Chapter 27 may turn the discrimination sequence into a portable reasoning scaffold, but not a diagnostic score, self-test, or corrective protocol.

## Acceptance criteria

The matrix is functioning when:

- *adverse* cannot be inferred from posture, asymmetry, deformation, or family label alone;
- every family identifies a distinct mechanical object and discriminating observation;
- leakage identifies a carrier, quantity, receiver, mismatch, and null branch;
- concentration and maintained constraint remain distinguishable;
- instability and discoordination remain distinguishable;
- compensation begins with successful functional preservation and may remain useful;
- scale transitions expose every intermediate bridge and permit both directions;
- hard/soft remains relational, task-specific, and accurately signposted as motivated by TCM;
- several families can coexist without becoming a syndrome or implied diagnosis;
- null, reverse, beneficial, low-mechanical-priority, and unresolved outcomes are legitimate; and
- worked narratives can fail to support a proposed family without being treated as failures of the book.
