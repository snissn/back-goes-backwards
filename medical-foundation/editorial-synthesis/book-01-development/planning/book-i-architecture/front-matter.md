# Book introduction — A mechanical history

## Introduction proposition

Every ordinary action has a mechanical history. Book I develops a language for tracing that history through a living person's changing structures, capacities, circumstances, and medical context.

## Introduction movement

Open with the recurring task, state the book's thesis, locate load routing within established medical mechanics, introduce hard/soft roles and posterior anatomy, present the explanatory chain and worked-case method, orient the reader to the six Parts, and explain evidence states once so later chapters can use them without repeated defensive framing.

### I.0 Opening — An ordinary action has a history

- **Proposition:** Moving an object between supports requires a changing organization of contact, force, geometry, active control, deformation, pressure, sensation, prior state, and environment.
- **Develop:** Tell the scene concretely; allow several bodies, strategies, supports, and forms of assistance; reveal the internal history gradually; end with the phrase “mechanical history.”
- **Evidence/example:** The recurring neutral object transfer.
- **Continuity:** Opens the reader's curiosity and hands I.1 the thesis.
- **Target:** 400–550 words.

### I.1 The body is a living biomechanical system

- **Proposition:** Living tissues and structures continuously receive, distribute, transform, transmit, and dissipate forces and moments while managing pressure and deformation over time.
- **Develop:** State the working thesis; connect living material state to mechanical behavior; locate mechanical history alongside biological, psychological, social, developmental, and environmental conditions through a few concrete links.
- **Evidence/example:** Two concise examples outside and inside the musculoskeletal system.
- **Continuity:** Establishes the book's secure foundation and hands I.2 the proposed addition.
- **Target:** 350–500 words.

### I.2 Longitudinal load routing is the book's organizing question

- **Proposition:** Load routing traces how a defined demand is partitioned among candidate carriers through a declared task, state, and period.
- **Develop:** Define demand, path, branching, phase, external support, and indeterminacy at a high level; state that *longitudinal* here means across dated events and intervals rather than along the anatomical axial direction; distinguish local endpoint expertise from the longer history this book assembles; name force, moment, pressure, stress, strain, deformation, excursion, repetition, and recovery as the quantities later chapters will separate.
- **Evidence/example:** One compact path diagram for the opening task.
- **Continuity:** Names the book's object and hands I.3 the division of labor.
- **Editorial boundary:** State once that a path is a bounded model, not a literal hidden pipe.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### I.3 Support divides mechanical labor

- **Proposition:** Relatively shape-preserving relations establish and resolve gross paths while relatively form-receiving structures yield, conform, glide, contain, expand, and recover.
- **Develop:** Introduce the compact intuition—“Load wants to travel through the hard body so that the soft body can remain soft”; state that either role may carry substantial force and exchange by direction, phase, scale, or state; signpost that the language is motivated by TCM hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts.
- **Evidence/example:** One shape-preserving/form-receiving relation.
- **Continuity:** Introduces the project's distinctive vocabulary and hands I.4 the first anatomical system.
- **Target:** 400–550 words.

### I.4 Posterior anatomy deserves sustained attention

- **Proposition:** The posterior body contains large, layered, mechanically consequential structures that participate in stance, gait, propulsion, lifting, braking, balance, reaching, transfer, and recovery.
- **Develop:** Name the major regions and large muscle groups; place them inside whole-body cooperation; introduce the title's immediate meaning and the stronger later-series question.
- **Evidence/example:** One posterior-system figure with scope declared.
- **Continuity:** Gives the book an anatomical center and hands I.5 the medical bridge.
- **Target:** 400–550 words.

### I.5 Mechanical history becomes medically meaningful through intermediate links

- **Proposition:** Task and environment become clinical meaning through configuration, distribution, receiver exposure, tissue or system response, function, finding, symptom, and interpretation over time.
- **Develop:** Present the recurring chain; place capacity, recovery, compensation, biology, development, behavior, and social context at named points; introduce the six load-management families as changes in organization that may alter exposure.
- **Evidence/example:** The book's full explanatory-chain figure.
- **Continuity:** States the burden of the argument and hands I.6 the case method.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### I.6 Worked narratives test the chain

- **Proposition:** Four cases test whether the framework can clarify geometry, maintained constraint, recurrence, and mixed chronic history without forcing them to the same conclusion.
- **Develop:** Name the four cases and their increasing incompleteness; explain system boundary, chronology, receiver, evidence state, alternatives, and bounded output; keep treatment outside the case job in one sentence.
- **Evidence/example:** A four-case overview table.
- **Continuity:** Shows how the book will demonstrate usefulness and hands I.7 the Part map.
- **Target:** 350–500 words.

### I.7 The six Parts form one cumulative argument

- **Proposition:** The book moves from mechanical foundations to cooperative support, load management, medical meaning, worked narratives, and development.
- **Develop:** Give each Part one question, answer, and handoff; avoid miniature chapter summaries; show how the posterior example and mechanical-history chain recur.
- **Evidence/example:** The six-Part movement table.
- **Continuity:** Orients the reader and hands I.8 evidence language.
- **Target:** 350–500 words.

### I.8 Evidence states preserve how each link became known

- **Proposition:** Observed, reported, measured, calculated, modeled, inferred, reconstructed, embodied, hypothesized, and unknown relations can coexist when their provenance remains visible.
- **Develop:** Define the terms once; explain exact source jobs; state how broad negative evidence and narrower hypotheses should be compared directly; show how a chain can remain useful when one link is measured and the next is inferred or unknown.
- **Evidence/example:** A small evidence-state legend used throughout the book.
- **Continuity:** Centralizes epistemic guidance and hands I.9 the reader contract.
- **Target:** 400–550 words.

### I.9 Reader contract — Follow the chain as far as the evidence allows

- **Proposition:** The reader is invited to trace mechanical relations within the whole person, retain useful local results, and stop where an intermediate link remains unsupported.
- **Develop:** State the additive clinical posture; preserve the wider hypothesis without requiring assent; return to the opening task; end on the first question of Chapter 1.
- **Evidence/example:** One abbreviated version of the opening task whose chain ends honestly at an unknown intermediate link.
- **Continuity:** Hands Part I an oriented reader and an ordinary event.
- **Target:** 250–350 words.
