# Part I — Mechanical foundations of living structure

## Part proposition

Living bodies continually organize mechanical demand. A precise account begins by defining the system and quantities involved, then distinguishes the roles structures perform and the configuration through which those roles become a path.

## Reader movement

The reader enters with an ordinary, mostly external idea of load. The Part moves from embodied demand, to mechanical quantities, to a division of mechanical labor, to the three-dimensional organization of that labor. The reader exits able to describe a candidate mechanical history without treating posture as exposure, tissue names as fixed roles, or a path as a hidden cable.

## Part opening — Entering the mechanical account

- **Proposition:** The opening task can now be followed from visible action into a precise account of living material, mechanical quantity, functional role, and configuration.
- **Develop:** Recall the already-established scene in one sentence; name the four questions Part I will answer; make clear that the Part examines the event rather than narrating it again.
- **Evidence/example:** A four-question orientation keyed to the opening task, without a second scene or new illustration.
- **Continuity:** Inherits I.0–I.9 and hands Chapter 1 the first comparison inside the event.
- **Editorial boundary:** Do not relaunch the object-transfer narrative or preview the later taxonomy.
- **Target:** 180–220 words.

## Chapter 1 — The mechanical dimension of life

### Chapter proposition

Mechanical conditions are a continuous dimension of living function, and their explanatory importance can be traced within a whole-person account.

### Chapter movement

Begin with material embodiment, establish reciprocal mechanics–biology interaction, move across scales, place the history inside the person's circumstances, and end by defining the broader upstream question Book I will investigate.

### 1.0 Opening — The body in the event

- **Proposition:** The object transfer occurs through living materials whose state changes what the task demands and how it is carried.
- **Develop:** Describe the same visible action on two different days or with two different supports; show that external success does not reveal internal organization; pose the chapter question: what makes a mechanical description relevant?
- **Evidence/example:** The recurring task under two contrasting states such as rested/fatigued or unsupported/assisted.
- **Continuity:** Inherits the Part's orientation and creates the need to distinguish presence from importance.
- **Target:** 250–350 words.

### 1.1 Embodied life has continuous mechanical conditions

- **Proposition:** Cells, tissues, organs, and whole-body functions operate while carrying force, pressure, contact, constraint, and deformation.
- **Develop:** Move from weight and support to joint contact, breathing, circulation, and tissue deformation; show that mechanics belongs to ordinary function as well as injury; state material embodiment as the chapter's secure foundation.
- **Evidence/example:** One musculoskeletal and one non-musculoskeletal example, such as joint contact and lung expansion.
- **Continuity:** Establishes the domain; hands 1.2 a living rather than passive material system.
- **Editorial boundary:** Physical presence establishes relevance for inquiry, not causal priority for a selected outcome.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 1.2 Biological state changes mechanical behavior

- **Proposition:** Living material properties and active control vary with biological state.
- **Develop:** Explain how hydration, composition, inflammation, healing, fatigue, activation, development, age, and prior exposure can change stiffness, compliance, friction, pressure, strength, or recovery; make state part of every later mechanical model.
- **Evidence/example:** A healing or inflamed tissue encountering an otherwise similar task.
- **Continuity:** Converts material embodiment into living mechanics; prepares the reciprocal direction in 1.3.
- **Target:** 400–550 words.

### 1.3 Mechanical exposure can change biological state

- **Proposition:** Defined mechanical exposures can participate in signaling, remodeling, fluid exchange, sensitivity, damage, and repair.
- **Develop:** Introduce mechanobiological reciprocity with tissue-specific examples; distinguish exposure from response; show why magnitude, direction, rate, repetition, state, and recovery will later matter.
- **Evidence/example:** Two bounded contrasts, such as bone remodeling and tendon or cartilage response.
- **Continuity:** Completes the bidirectional relation and hands 1.4 a chain that crosses scale.
- **Editorial boundary:** Avoid a generic claim that all load produces adaptation or that mechanotransduction determines clinical outcome.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 1.4 One event contains several non-equivalent scales

- **Proposition:** Task, segment, interface, tissue, and cellular descriptions can concern one event while requiring different observations and models.
- **Develop:** Revisit the object transfer at each scale; name what is visible or measurable at that level; identify the missing information between levels; establish that a whole-body shape cannot directly specify a local stress field or cellular response.
- **Evidence/example:** A single vertical scale-stack figure using the recurring task.
- **Continuity:** Turns reciprocity into an inspectable sequence; prepares the broader determinants in 1.5.
- **Target:** 500–650 words.

### 1.5 The whole person changes demand, capacity, and meaning

- **Proposition:** Biological, psychological, social, developmental, behavioral, and environmental conditions enter the mechanical history through named relations.
- **Develop:** Show how work organization or assistance changes demand; sleep, illness, or inflammation changes recovery; expectation or pain changes control and sensation; resources and environment change available strategies; keep each factor connected to a node rather than appended as a disclaimer list.
- **Evidence/example:** Extend the recurring task through a changed environment or available assistance.
- **Continuity:** Places the scale chain inside the person–task–environment system; prepares the upstream inquiry.
- **Target:** 500–650 words.

### 1.6 Mechanical history can matter before a discrete lesion is visible

- **Proposition:** Repeated demand, changing reserve, compensation, and recovery can create a medically relevant history even when no single initiating event explains the present state.
- **Develop:** Contrast a discrete impact with a cumulative or uncertain chronology; state the kinds of contribution the book will investigate—exposure, modulation, maintenance, consequence, or context—without forcing an early causal rank; identify the intermediate links that Parts III–V must supply.
- **Evidence/example:** A clear fracture chronology beside a deliberately unresolved cumulative history.
- **Continuity:** States Book I's research problem and hands Chapter 2 the need for controlled language.
- **Editorial boundary:** This is an inquiry and organizing proposition, not a conclusion about chronic disease in general.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 1.7 Conclusion — Trace the material history

- **Proposition:** Mechanics becomes explanatory when its quantities, scales, paths, receivers, timing, and alternatives are made explicit.
- **Develop:** Gather embodiment, reciprocity, scale, and whole-person context into one affirmative methodological statement; return briefly to the task; identify the ambiguity still hidden inside the word “load.”
- **Evidence/example:** Reuse the chapter's paired task states to show what the material history adds beyond the shared visible endpoint.
- **Continuity:** Completes the opening case and hands Chapter 2 a finite vocabulary problem.
- **Target:** 220–300 words.

## Chapter 2 — A working language of load

### Chapter proposition

A mechanical history becomes inspectable when demand, distribution, exposure, material response, and time are assigned precise quantities and system boundaries.

### Chapter movement

Move from the chosen system to external interactions, internal exposure, material behavior, dose, and energy transfer. End with a bounded definition of load path that the rest of the book can reuse.

### 2.0 Opening — One word hides several mechanical questions

- **Proposition:** “Load” is a useful umbrella whose meaning must be resolved by the question being asked.
- **Develop:** Place object weight, joint moment, tendon force, vessel pressure, tissue strain, and repeated exposure side by side; show why their differences matter to the narrative rather than offering a glossary in advance.
- **Evidence/example:** Six labels on one recurring-task diagram.
- **Continuity:** Inherits Chapter 1's ambiguity and creates the chapter's sequence.
- **Target:** 250–350 words.

### 2.1 The system boundary determines the mechanical account

- **Proposition:** Forces and transfers are described relative to a selected system.
- **Develop:** Define system, environment, boundary, and external interaction; compare person-plus-object, person, limb, segment, and tissue systems; show how a contact changes status when the boundary moves.
- **Evidence/example:** Two free-body diagrams of the same task with different boundaries.
- **Continuity:** Establishes where demand enters; hands 2.2 the external quantities acting across that boundary.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 2.2 Force and moment describe directed demand

- **Proposition:** Force describes directed interaction, while moment describes its turning effect about a chosen point or axis.
- **Develop:** Introduce magnitude, direction, point of application, line of action, moment arm, torque, force couple, and static or dynamic balance; use a close-versus-distant object position to make geometry consequential.
- **Evidence/example:** A compact free-body comparison with dimensionally consistent notation.
- **Continuity:** Gives the external event direction and leverage; hands 2.3 distributed contact and pressure.
- **Target:** 500–650 words.

### 2.3 Pressure and contact distribute force over a boundary

- **Proposition:** Distributed contact and pressure specify how force is applied across an area or within a fluid-mediated system.
- **Develop:** Distinguish resultant contact force from its spatial distribution; introduce pressure without treating all pressure systems alike; connect area, boundary shape, and containment to joints, supports, cavities, and conduits.
- **Evidence/example:** One joint-contact image and one pressure-boundary schematic.
- **Continuity:** Moves the account from external resultant to distributed boundary condition; hands 2.4 the material interior.
- **Target:** 400–550 words.

### 2.4 Stress, strain, and deformation describe internal exposure

- **Proposition:** Stress and strain describe internal distributions and relative deformation within a selected material or tissue.
- **Develop:** Define normal and shear components; introduce tension, compression, shear, bending, torsion, multiaxial state, deformation, displacement, and strain rate; emphasize that these require a receiver and reference state.
- **Evidence/example:** An idealized strip or beam followed by one bounded tissue translation.
- **Continuity:** Crosses from boundary demand to local exposure; hands 2.5 the receiver's response properties.
- **Target:** 550–650 words.

### 2.5 Stiffness, compliance, strength, and capacity answer different questions

- **Proposition:** Resistance to deformation, ability to deform, failure threshold, and present ability to complete a task are related but distinct properties.
- **Develop:** Define stiffness and compliance over stated conditions; distinguish material, structural, active, and system behavior; separate strength from current capacity; introduce state, fatigue, pain, coordination, and recovery as living contributors.
- **Evidence/example:** Same structure under different boundary or activation states.
- **Continuity:** Gives the receiver a state-dependent response; hands 2.6 exposure history.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 2.6 Mechanical dose is an organized history

- **Proposition:** Exposure depends on magnitude, direction, rate, repetition, duration, variability, sequence, and recovery intervals.
- **Develop:** Show how equal peaks or equal repetition counts can create different histories; define cumulative exposure without treating it as a universal damage counter; make chronology part of the model.
- **Evidence/example:** Two timelines with the same total count and different temporal organization.
- **Continuity:** Adds time to the receiver relation; hands 2.7 energy and transfer.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 2.7 Work, energy, power, and cost describe change through a task

- **Proposition:** Mechanical work and energy track storage, return, transfer, and dissipation, while muscular or metabolic cost requires its own measurement.
- **Develop:** Define work and power at an accessible level; distinguish energy absorption, storage, return, dissipation, and active cost; explain why “absorbing load” needs a named process.
- **Evidence/example:** Lowering, pausing, and raising the same object.
- **Continuity:** Completes the main quantity set and hands 2.8 a path model that can combine carriers.
- **Target:** 400–550 words.

### 2.8 A load path is a bounded model of transfer

- **Proposition:** A load path represents how a defined demand is partitioned among candidate carriers within a declared task, state, and period.
- **Develop:** Distinguish force line, line of action, and inferred internal route; allow branching, changing carrier, active force, pressure, contact, and indeterminacy; require assumptions and evidence states to travel with the path.
- **Evidence/example:** One branching path diagram for the recurring task.
- **Continuity:** Synthesizes the formal path model and hands 2.9 a distinction among three uses of force-line language.
- **Editorial boundary:** The path is an explanatory representation, not a literal substance or single hidden cable.
- **Target:** 500–650 words.

### 2.9 “Force line” can name three different objects

- **Proposition:** An external line of action, an inferred internal mechanical path, and an experienced or cultivated internal continuity are three meaningful but non-equivalent uses of force-line language.
- **Develop:** Define the external geometric line; define the multicarrier internal path; preserve the embodied or Yin-associated sense of felt internal connection as an observation and hypothesis source; state the transformations and measurements required to relate the three.
- **Evidence/example:** One three-column comparison, including a bounded embodied example without converting it into an anatomical or clinical proof.
- **Continuity:** Preserves the project's embodied vocabulary while keeping the mechanical categories exact; hands 2.10 the chapter synthesis.
- **Editorial boundary:** Extended embodied-force-line, TCM-channel, and practice development belongs to Books III–V.
- **Target:** 350–500 words.

### 2.10 Conclusion — Define before inferring

- **Proposition:** A credible mechanical narrative names its system, quantities, direction, receiver, time course, and uncertainty before assigning consequence.
- **Develop:** Reconstruct the object transfer with the chapter's vocabulary; state the remaining question: how do different structures divide the labor of preserving relation and receiving deformation?
- **Evidence/example:** Reuse the chapter's branching path diagram with its system, quantities, receiver, and evidence states labeled.
- **Continuity:** Hands Chapter 3 the functional-role problem.
- **Target:** 220–300 words.

## Chapter 3 — Hard and soft mechanical roles

### Chapter proposition

Living support depends on a changing division of mechanical labor between relatively shape-preserving roles and relatively form-receiving roles.

### Chapter movement

Begin from the positive need for persistence and change, define each role, show a candidate tissue handoff, demonstrate role exchange across directions and phases, and establish the model's TCM-motivated provenance and test of usefulness.

### 3.0 Opening — Support combines persistence with change

- **Proposition:** Effective support preserves some relations while allowing other structures to yield, glide, expand, contain, and recover.
- **Develop:** Use the object transfer to identify relations that must remain sufficiently organized and deformations that make movement possible; frame hard and soft as complementary jobs.
- **Evidence/example:** A single task frame with shape-preserving and form-receiving functions highlighted.
- **Continuity:** Inherits Chapter 2's carriers and asks what work each performs; hands 3.1 the origin and purpose of the project vocabulary.
- **Target:** 250–350 words.

### 3.1 A TCM-motivated distinction becomes a mechanical proposal

- **Proposition:** TCM hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts motivate this vocabulary, while Book I translates the intuition into roles that can be described and tested mechanically.
- **Develop:** State provenance directly; distinguish motivation, mechanical translation, later correspondence, and proof; introduce shape-preserving and form-receiving as flexible working terms; reserve detailed channel mapping for Book III.
- **Evidence/example:** A short provenance note and a compact motivation/translation/correspondence table.
- **Continuity:** Signposts the traditional source before the reader naturalizes the vocabulary; hands 3.2 the first mechanical role.
- **Target:** 300–450 words.

### 3.2 The hard role preserves and resolves gross relation

- **Proposition:** A hard role comparatively preserves shape, establishes a path, constrains relation, or resolves gross geometry under declared conditions.
- **Develop:** Use bone as the clearest example; show how active muscle, tensioned tendon or ligament, fascia, pressure, or a coordinated assembly can perform a hard role in a direction or phase; connect the role to route establishment rather than material hardness alone.
- **Evidence/example:** Bone-led route plus one non-bone role reversal.
- **Continuity:** Defines the first side of the division; hands 3.3 the complementary capacity.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 3.3 The soft role receives form while preserving functional freedom

- **Proposition:** A soft role is relatively form-receiving and shape-conforming, allowing yielding, glide, containment, expansion, pressure response, buffering, or local shape change.
- **Develop:** Describe softness as a positive capability; show that a soft-role structure can transmit substantial force; identify excursion, recoverability, and accommodation as functional variables.
- **Evidence/example:** A vessel, sliding interface, muscle, or cartilage example chosen for clear form reception.
- **Continuity:** Completes the polarity and hands 3.4 the relation between the two roles.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 3.4 Mechanical labor is divided across a relation

- **Proposition:** The roles become meaningful when a shape-preserving relation allows a form-receiving structure to perform its needed function.
- **Develop:** Identify maintaining structure, receiving structure, task, direction, phase, and required freedom; show that neither role is morally superior; connect the compact formulation—load through the hard body so the soft body can remain soft—to a relational analysis.
- **Evidence/example:** One well-bounded containing or sliding relation.
- **Continuity:** Turns two definitions into an organizing model; prepares the candidate handoff in 3.5.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 3.5 A soft-to-hard handoff is one candidate transfer pattern

- **Proposition:** Active or form-receiving structures can gather and shape demand before tensile or skeletal relations resolve it into a more persistent route.
- **Develop:** Trace a provisional muscle/fascia → tendon/ligament → bone/joint sequence; distinguish actuation, gathering, transmission, constraint, and contact; state where actual anatomy requires a more distributed account.
- **Evidence/example:** One regional myotendinous-to-skeletal handoff with a simple diagram.
- **Continuity:** Makes the polarity dynamic; hands 3.6 changes across phase and direction.
- **Editorial boundary:** This first-order pattern is a candidate recurring organization, not a universal tissue sequence.
- **Target:** 500–650 words.

### 3.6 Roles exchange with direction, state, scale, and phase

- **Proposition:** The same tissue or assembly can preserve relation in one dimension and receive form in another, and can change roles as the task unfolds.
- **Develop:** Compare tension/compression or active/relaxed states; show simultaneous multidirectional roles; use task phases to demonstrate exchange; establish the declared-condition rule for every later hard/soft claim.
- **Evidence/example:** A tendon, muscle, or pressurized cavity shown in two mechanical states.
- **Continuity:** Prevents tissue taxonomy and hands 3.7 the model's discriminating use.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 3.7 The model earns its place through discriminating questions

- **Proposition:** Hard/soft language is useful when it specifies a relation that ordinary tissue labels or undifferentiated stiffness language leaves obscure.
- **Develop:** State the questions the model should answer; require named roles and conditions; include a reversal or null case; connect the model to maintained deformation, handoff, and compensation without developing those later mechanisms here.
- **Evidence/example:** A two-case comparison in which the same tissue label performs different roles.
- **Continuity:** Completes the functional framework and hands Chapter 4 the organization of roles into paths.
- **Target:** 400–550 words.

### 3.8 Conclusion — From roles to organization

- **Proposition:** Support is a changing relation among structures that preserve geometry and structures that receive form.
- **Develop:** Return to the object task; identify the unresolved variables—orientation, contact, activation, pressure, material state, and available motion—that determine which role appears.
- **Evidence/example:** Reuse the chapter's two-state role-exchange example to gather the division of labor without adding another tissue case.
- **Continuity:** Hands Chapter 4 the concept of configuration.
- **Target:** 220–300 words.

## Chapter 4 — Three-dimensional configuration and load paths

### Chapter proposition

Configuration organizes geometry, contact, activation, pressure, material state, and available motion into the changing conditions through which demand is distributed.

### Chapter movement

Begin with one three-dimensional event, choose and transform reference frames, distinguish geometry from complete mechanical state, show how paths branch and change, then separate the several three-part descriptions used across the series.

### 4.0 Opening — One event supports several valid descriptions

- **Proposition:** The same object transfer can be described through external motion, segment relations, internal carriers, and material directions without any one view containing the whole event.
- **Develop:** Present the scene from front, side, and local anatomical views; identify what each reveals and omits; pose the configuration problem.
- **Evidence/example:** Three synchronized views or diagrams of the recurring task.
- **Continuity:** Inherits roles and quantities from Chapters 2–3 and prepares reference frames.
- **Target:** 250–350 words.

### 4.1 Reference frames make direction explicit

- **Proposition:** Position, orientation, force, moment, and deformation components acquire meaning within a declared frame.
- **Develop:** Define global, segment, anatomical, and material-local frames; explain origin, axes, sign, and transformation; use left/right and curved anatomy to show why local and global descriptions may differ.
- **Evidence/example:** A pelvis or vertebra with global and local axes.
- **Continuity:** Establishes the reporting system; hands 4.2 the choice of model dimension.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 4.2 Model dimension follows the question

- **Proposition:** A planar model can answer a bounded question within a three-dimensional event, while coupled or out-of-plane behavior requires a richer representation.
- **Develop:** State when sagittal, frontal, or transverse simplification is useful; identify assumptions and omitted couplings; show how model complexity is justified by the outcome rather than by realism alone.
- **Evidence/example:** One problem solved adequately in 2D and one whose relevant moment or contact requires 3D.
- **Continuity:** Establishes modeling economy; hands 4.3 a fuller definition of configuration.
- **Target:** 350–500 words.

### 4.3 Configuration includes the full mechanical state

- **Proposition:** Configuration includes relative geometry, contacts, activation, pressure, tissue state, supports, and available motion at a declared phase.
- **Develop:** Contrast identical visible postures with different activation, support, or pressure states; distinguish pose, movement, and configuration; show how changing one relation changes leverage and candidate paths.
- **Evidence/example:** Two outwardly similar task frames with different internal or environmental conditions.
- **Continuity:** Combines the Part's vocabulary into one state description; hands 4.4 path formation.
- **Target:** 500–650 words.

### 4.4 Configuration determines the available carrier set

- **Proposition:** Geometry and state determine which contacts, tensile structures, active forces, pressure relations, and external supports can participate in a task.
- **Develop:** Identify available versus recruited carriers; show how contact appears or disappears, moment arms change, and assistance enters; connect carrier availability to the hard/soft division of labor.
- **Evidence/example:** The recurring task before and after a hand, chair, rail, or helper changes the boundary.
- **Continuity:** Turns configuration into mechanical possibility; hands 4.5 actual distribution.
- **Target:** 400–550 words.

### 4.5 Load paths branch, combine, and change through time

- **Proposition:** Internal distribution is a time-varying allocation among carriers rather than a single uninterrupted line.
- **Develop:** Trace branching and phase change; distinguish availability from actual recruitment; include indeterminate allocations and equivalent endpoints; show how a carrier can change role during the same task.
- **Evidence/example:** A phased path diagram for lift, carry, and placement.
- **Continuity:** Completes the load-path model and prepares the comparative direction vocabulary in 4.6.
- **Target:** 500–650 words.

### 4.6 Three direction languages answer different questions

- **Proposition:** Spatial coordinates, generalized modes, and material directions are three useful but non-equivalent descriptions of direction and organization.
- **Develop:** Show that Cartesian or anatomical axes locate spatial components and transformations; show that generalized modes describe coordinated degrees of freedom and their conjugate forces or moments; show that material directions describe anisotropy, principal behavior, or an optional local orthotropic approximation; distinguish each family from the other two in one comparative sequence; name the future axial, opening/rotational, and expansive program only as a later application.
- **Evidence/example:** One three-column comparison using a locally oriented tissue or segment, a reduced mechanical mode, and a fiber-reinforced or orthotropic material example.
- **Continuity:** Prevents category collapse while keeping Book I's path account generic; hands 4.7 the anatomical carriers.
- **Editorial boundary:** Signed preferred modes, extended transformations, orthotropic constitutive formalism, and principal-direction derivations belong to the Book II source packet.
- **Target:** 500–650 words.

### 4.7 Conclusion — Anatomy supplies the carriers

- **Proposition:** A mechanical history now has a system, quantities, roles, configuration, and candidate paths; its next question is anatomical.
- **Develop:** Reconstruct the object task in one concise chain; name bones, joints, muscle, tensile tissues, sliding structures, and pressure systems as the carriers Part II will study.
- **Evidence/example:** Reuse the chapter's phased path diagram, now annotated with the three direction-language distinctions only where they clarify the carrier account.
- **Continuity:** Closes Part I and hands Part II a concrete architecture problem.
- **Target:** 220–300 words.

## Part I synthesis — From embodiment to organized paths

- **Proposition:** Embodied life continuously manages mechanical demand through defined quantities, complementary roles, changing configurations, and branching paths.
- **Develop:** Gather the four chapter outputs without retelling them; show the cumulative sequence; state the reader's new ability to trace a proposed history from boundary to carrier and receiver; pose the anatomical question that opens Part II.
- **Evidence/example:** One completed recurring-task diagram annotated with the Part's vocabulary.
- **Continuity:** Hands Part II the positive question of how living structures cooperate to perform these jobs.
- **Target:** 300–450 words.
