# Part II — The architecture of support

## Part proposition

Living support emerges from cooperation among skeletal geometry, joint contact, active force, tensile transfer, form-receiving tissues, sliding interfaces, pressure, and control. Posterior anatomy is a large and consequential participant in this shared architecture.

## Reader movement

The reader enters with abstract mechanical roles. Chapters 5–6 turn them into anatomical relations. Chapters 7–8 then map and test posterior participation. Chapter 9 restores whole-body and lifespan variation and separates three proposition levels: anatomical existence, task-specific importance, and the later preferred-configuration hypothesis.

## Part opening — Support emerges through cooperation

- **Proposition:** Living support is produced by a timed exchange among structures, control processes, external contacts, and forms of assistance with different mechanical capabilities.
- **Develop:** Name skeletal, active, tensile, form-receiving, sliding, pressure-mediated, control, environmental, and assistive contributions; connect each to Part I's roles and paths; establish the Part's movement from general architecture to posterior example.
- **Evidence/example:** A layered diagram of the recurring object transfer.
- **Continuity:** Inherits Part I's path model and hands Chapter 5 the clearest shape-preserving architecture.
- **Target:** 300–450 words.

## Chapter 5 — Skeleton, joints, and shape-preserving paths

### Chapter proposition

Skeletal geometry, joint contact, and recruited restraint create changing routes that preserve gross relations while permitting movement.

### Chapter movement

Move from geometry to material behavior, joint redirection, ligamentous guidance, configuration-dependent route selection, linked segments, and task-specific stability.

### 5.0 Opening — Anatomy makes some routes possible

- **Proposition:** The dimensions and relations of bones and joints establish a field of mechanical possibilities before active recruitment selects among them.
- **Develop:** Introduce length, curvature, cross-section, orientation, articulation, and contact; connect possibility to configuration rather than ideal alignment.
- **Evidence/example:** A simple linked-segment version of the recurring task.
- **Continuity:** Inherits Chapter 4's carrier set and prepares skeletal geometry.
- **Target:** 250–350 words.

### 5.1 Skeletal geometry shapes leverage and contact

- **Proposition:** Bone geometry and relative orientation alter moment arms, contact possibilities, bending, torsion, and the routes available through a region.
- **Develop:** Explain length, curve, cross-section, axis, and relation to neighbors; show how geometric change modifies a problem without determining its full solution.
- **Evidence/example:** One long-bone or vertebral geometry comparison.
- **Continuity:** Establishes structural possibility; hands 5.2 the living material that realizes it.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 5.2 Bone preserves gross relation while deforming

- **Develop:** Cover cortical/trabecular organization at a high level; connect compression, tension, bending, torsion, and adaptation to the declared task; distinguish gross preservation from rigidity.

- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 5.3 Joint surfaces redirect and distribute demand

- **Proposition:** Articular geometry and contact convert a chain of bones into a moving route whose distribution changes through range and phase.

- **Continuity:** Turns separate bones into a changing route; hands 5.4 constraint and guidance.
- **Target:** 500–650 words.

### 5.4 Ligaments and capsules guide changing relation

- **Develop:** Explain slack, progressive recruitment, orientation, rate, and joint position; show that guidance can preserve one freedom while limiting another; connect to active control.
- **Evidence/example:** A joint with sequential ligament recruitment.
- **Continuity:** Adds path guidance; hands 5.5 the configuration-dependent result.
- **Target:** 400–550 words.

### 5.5 Configuration changes skeletal leverage and route availability

- **Develop:** Compare two configurations in the recurring task; trace changed moment arms, contact, and available restraint; preserve several viable strategies.

### 5.6 Skeletal routes are linked across regions

- **Develop:** Trace ground or support through foot, limb, pelvis, axial skeleton, shoulder girdle, and hand at a deliberately coarse scale; identify joints where carrier, direction, or constraint changes.
- **Evidence/example:** One linked-segment path diagram for the object transfer.
- **Continuity:** Extends local mechanics toward a whole task; hands 5.7 stability.
- **Editorial boundary:** The linked route remains shared with active and form-receiving systems.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 5.7 Stability is maintained relation under demand

- **Proposition:** Mechanical stability is the capacity to maintain or recover a task-relevant relation within a usable range.
- **Develop:** Define relation, tolerance, disturbance, maintenance, and recovery; distinguish structural, active, and environmental contributions; connect stability to movement and adaptability.
- **Evidence/example:** A small perturbation during the recurring task.
- **Continuity:** Summarizes skeletal possibility while exposing its dependence on living support; hands Chapter 6 the missing systems.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 5.8 Conclusion — The skeleton provides changing possibilities

- **Proposition:** Bones, joints, and restraints establish adaptable shape-preserving routes that become functional only through cooperation with active, tensile, form-receiving, sliding, and pressure systems.
- **Develop:** Gather geometry, finite bone deformation, changing joint contact, recruited restraint, linked routes, and task-specific stability into the chapter's positive skeletal contribution; identify the living functions still to be added.
- **Evidence/example:** Reuse the linked-segment task diagram with the skeletal possibilities and missing cooperative functions marked.

## Chapter 6 — Active, tensile, form-receiving, sliding, and pressure-mediated systems

### Chapter proposition

Living support becomes adaptable through active force, tensile transmission, controlled deformation, relative glide, containment, pressure, and baseline mechanical state.

### Chapter movement

Add each capability as one job in the same transfer, then synthesize the systems into a cooperative architecture and bound broad network models.

### 6.0 Opening — Skeletal possibility becomes living support

- **Proposition:** The available skeletal route is selected, actuated, stabilized, and continually revised by living tissues and control.
- **Develop:** Return to the object transfer; identify the mechanical work left unexplained by bone and joint geometry; preview the chapter's carriers as a sequence of jobs.
- **Evidence/example:** The Chapter 5 route diagram with missing functions highlighted.
- **Continuity:** Inherits skeletal architecture and sets the chapter's common task.
- **Target:** 250–350 words.

### 6.1 Muscle generates force and changes system state

- **Proposition:** Muscle contributes through active force, braking, stiffness modulation, joint compression, power, and anticipatory organization.
- **Develop:** Introduce length, velocity, activation, leverage, co-contraction, and eccentric/concentric/isometric roles; show that muscle changes both movement and the conditions under which other structures carry demand.

### 6.2 Tendons and aponeuroses transmit, store, and return

### 6.3 Cartilage and other form-receiving tissues manage contact

- **Develop:** Describe cartilage as a fluid–solid material at an accessible level; include meniscus, labrum, disc, or fat pad only by named job; connect deformation to time, rate, and recovery.

### 6.4 Sliding interfaces preserve relative excursion

- **Develop:** Define excursion, relative motion, friction, lubrication, shear, and tethering; select only a few structures whose function is clear; show how glide can coexist with substantial force transmission.
- **Evidence/example:** Tendon sheath or nerve-interface example used mechanically, not clinically.

### 6.5 Containment makes pressure mechanically useful

- **Proposition:** Pressure contributes to support and transfer through deformable contents acting within a boundary.
- **Develop:** Distinguish pressure from force and stiffness; identify source, contents, boundary, outlet, and phase; introduce thoracoabdominal pressure and other cavities as ordinary mechanics; show how active boundary control changes the relation.

### 6.6 Fascia has scale-specific mechanical jobs

- **Proposition:** Fascial tissues contribute through local attachment, enclosure, separation, force sharing, and permitted glide, with different evidence at different scales.
- **Develop:** Distinguish named local anatomy, regional continuity, and proposed whole-body transfer; describe the mechanical jobs at each scale; preserve fascia as one participant rather than a universal explanation.
- **Evidence/example:** Thoracolumbar fascia or another region with bounded anatomy and measured mechanics.
- **Continuity:** Integrates local and regional networks; hands 6.7 initial state.
- **Target:** 450–600 words.

### 6.7 Tone, prestress, pressure, and control set the starting condition

- **Proposition:** A task begins from an already organized mechanical state shaped by activation, tension, pressure, contact, and expectation.
- **Develop:** Define baseline tone and prestress without treating them as fixed; show how anticipatory control and prior state alter stiffness and route selection before visible movement; connect to phase transitions within the task.
- **Evidence/example:** Two trials with similar motion and different preparatory state.
- **Continuity:** Adds temporal preparation; hands 6.8 whole-system synthesis.
- **Target:** 400–550 words.

### 6.8 Support is a networked handoff among distinct jobs

- **Proposition:** Skeletal, active, tensile, form-receiving, sliding, and pressure-mediated systems form a cooperative network because their jobs are coordinated, not because every structure belongs to one continuous cable.
- **Develop:** Reconstruct the object transfer from ground/support to object; identify carrier and role changes; introduce tensegrity as one illustrative model of distributed tension and compression; state what the model clarifies and what requires direct anatomy and measurement.
- **Evidence/example:** A layered handoff diagram plus a bounded tensegrity comparison.
- **Continuity:** Completes general support and hands Chapter 7 a specific large anatomical system.
- **Target:** 550–650 words.

### 6.9 Conclusion — Cooperation creates adaptable support

- **Proposition:** Living support is the timed coordination of persistence, actuation, transmission, deformation, glide, pressure, and control.
- **Develop:** Reassemble the chapter's distinct jobs into one handoff sequence; state how their coordination creates a support system whose distribution can change with task and phase.
- **Evidence/example:** Reuse the layered handoff diagram, without adding another network model.
- **Continuity:** Opens the posterior body as a major test of the cooperative account.
