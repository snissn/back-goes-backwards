# Causal-Chain and Evidence-State Grammar

**Status:** shared Book I development control

**Primary owners:** Chapters 18, 19, and 21

**Required use:** Chapters 20–27 and any medically exposed figure or worked narrative

## Purpose

Book I repeatedly follows a possible relation from an ordinary task toward a local mechanical exposure, a tissue response, and a medical interpretation. Without a shared grammar, a smooth paragraph or arrow can silently convert visible position into internal force, modeled exposure into observed injury, association into cause, or symptom modulation into diagnosis.

This control keeps every object and bridge visible. It does not require every chapter to display the full chain, and it does not imply that a complete chain exists in every case. A useful result may be a well-supported partial chain, a mixed account, an unresolved branch, or evidence that the proposed mechanical relation adds little.

## Canonical explanatory chain

```text
task and environment
    -> configuration and available paths
    -> external demand and internal distribution
    -> local mechanical exposure
    -> tissue or system response through time
    -> function, finding, or symptom
    -> clinical interpretation or outcome
```

Each arrow is a separate claim. Each node may contain several variables, competing models, or parallel paths. A later node cannot be used as retrospective proof of an earlier one.

## Node definitions

| Node | Object being described | Minimum specification | Common shortcut to prevent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Task and environment | The event, purpose, external supports, contacts, constraints, and temporal context | Person or population; task; setting; assistance; duration; sequence; relevant history | Naming a generic posture without the task or boundary conditions |
| Configuration and available paths | Geometry, orientation, contact, active state, pressure, material state, and available movement at a stated phase | System boundary; spatial frame; phase; observed versus modeled variables; candidate carriers | Treating visible posture as the complete configuration or route |
| External demand and internal distribution | Forces, moments, pressure, work, acceleration, contact resultants, and their sharing among structures | Quantity; units; direction; boundary; frame; calculation or measurement method | Treating object weight as joint force or one resultant as a unique internal path |
| Local mechanical exposure | Stress, strain, pressure distribution, shear, contact, deformation, excursion, friction, or another tissue-specific quantity over time | Tissue or interface; location; reference state; material assumptions; dose descriptor | Treating a visible angle, narrowed image, or inferred path as local exposure |
| Tissue or system response | Mechanical state change, fatigue, remodeling, injury, repair, inflammation, regulation, retained deformation, or recovery | Response variable; tissue state; timescale; baseline; competing biological influences | Using “responds to load” as a universal direction or clinical consequence |
| Function, finding, or symptom | Performance, range, strength, conduction, flow, imaging, laboratory result, sensation, pain, fatigue, or other selected endpoint | Endpoint definition; timing; measurement; meaningful difference; discordant cases | Pooling function, image, physiology, and subjective experience as one result |
| Clinical interpretation or outcome | Diagnosis, prognosis, risk, treatment response, disability, quality of life, or another medical conclusion | Population; criteria; comparator; follow-up; scope; professional review | Treating a mechanically intelligible story as diagnosis or treatment guidance |

The term *load* should be replaced at each node by the actual object meant. When the actual quantity is unknown, say *demand*, *candidate exposure*, or *routing hypothesis* rather than borrowing precision from a nearby measurement.

## Base evidence-state legend

Book I inherits the following labels from the Chapter 2 definitions and notation pack:

| Label | Meaning | Required disclosure | What it cannot become silently |
|---|---|---|---|
| `OBS` | Directly observed description | Observer or source, task, timing, resolution, and ambiguity | A measured force, internal route, or cause |
| `MEAS` | Instrument-derived quantity | Instrument, protocol, units, validity, uncertainty, and processing | A different quantity or a clinically meaningful effect without validation |
| `CALC` | Calculated result from declared inputs | Relation or equation, inputs, assumptions, units, and propagation of uncertainty | A direct observation or validated model output |
| `MOD` | Output or behavior of a specified model | Boundary, variables, constitutive assumptions, validation domain, and sensitivity | The living system itself or a unique explanation |
| `INF` | Inference from observations, measurements, calculations, or models | Premises, inference rule, alternatives, and confidence | An observed intermediate state |
| `HYP` | Proposed relation or prediction awaiting sufficient test | Exact proposition, expected observation, alternatives, and disfavoring result | A conclusion merely because it is mechanically plausible |
| `EMB` | Reported embodied or felt organization | Reporter, task, side, phase, language, repeatability, and candidate correlates | A measured force line, anatomical cable, or universal experience |

An item can carry more than one label in a declared sequence—for example, `MEAS -> CALC -> MOD`—but the labels must not be collapsed into the strongest-looking term.

### Reconstructed histories

*Reconstructed* is a temporal qualifier, not presently a replacement for the base legend. A reconstructed exposure or route is assembled from later observations, dated events, anatomy, task history, models, and exclusions. It should state which elements are `OBS`, `MEAS`, `CALC`, `MOD`, or `INF`; what interval is missing; and which alternative histories remain compatible.

Do not label a reconstructed history as longitudinally observed. Present state can retain evidence of prior events without uniquely revealing their sequence or initiating cause.

## Arrow grammar

An arrow should be included only when its relation is named. Recommended relation classes are:

| Relation | Question | Typical support | Required alternative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mechanical transformation | How do boundary conditions and geometry transform one mechanical quantity into another? | Balance, kinematics, contact mechanics, constitutive model, direct measurement | Different geometry, carrier, active force, or model giving another distribution |
| Temporal accumulation or state transition | How does repeated or maintained exposure change the state receiving the next event? | Repeated measurement, fatigue/remodeling model, longitudinal observation | Recovery, adaptation, intermittent exposure, unrelated state change |
| Biological bridge | How does a defined local exposure produce or modify a defined tissue response? | Tissue-specific mechanobiology with dose, state, and time | Biological primary, nonresponse, opposite response, or threshold not crossed |
| Functional bridge | How does a tissue or system state affect selected function? | Physiology, performance, imaging-function relation, intervention or natural experiment | Compensation, collateral route, reserve, measurement artifact |
| Symptom or experience bridge | How does mechanical or biological state contribute to reported experience? | Temporal, within-person, experimental, clinical, and contextual evidence | Sensitization, expectation, sleep, mood, social context, unrelated cause |
| Clinical inference | How does the evidence support classification, prognosis, or care relevance? | Validated criteria, comparative studies, guideline or specialist review | Incidental finding, selection bias, reverse causality, competing diagnosis |

The relation class controls the evidence burden. Mechanical validity at an early arrow cannot be cited as support for a later biological or clinical arrow.

## Time and retained state

Every worked chain should identify at least three times when relevant:

1. **candidate initiating or recurring exposure;**
2. **observed response or state transition;** and
3. **current measurement, finding, or presentation.**

These times may involve different maintaining relations. An extrinsically imposed deformation can recover, persist, or contribute to an intrinsic or mixed state through material change. A current lesion or symptom can alter future configuration and demand. Treatment can change the quantity later measured. The diagram should therefore permit:

- recovery to the prior range;
- adaptation with preserved or improved capacity;
- retained intrinsic change after the external relation is absent;
- mixed state with both current constraint and retained change;
- substitution or compensation that changes later exposure;
- reverse causality from disease or symptoms to mechanics; and
- unresolved chronology.

A single forward cascade is rarely adequate for a chronic case.

## Alternatives enter the chain, not an appendix

Competing explanations should enter at the node or arrow they affect.

| Entry point | Example alternative family | Question it changes |
|---|---|---|
| Task and environment | Job control, assistance, access, equipment, social demand, prior learning | What exposure actually occurred and what options existed? |
| Configuration and paths | Anatomical variation, pain behavior, habit, fatigue, disability, external support | Why was this organization present and was it constrained? |
| Distribution and exposure | Unmodeled muscle force, pressure, material anisotropy, multiple paths, measurement artifact | Was the local exposure estimated correctly or uniquely? |
| Tissue response | Age, genetics, immune state, infection, metabolism, medication, sleep, prior injury | Why did this tissue respond this way, if it did? |
| Function or symptom | Reserve, collateral pathways, sensitization, expectation, mood, attention, compensation | Why do findings and experience correspond or diverge? |
| Clinical interpretation | Definition, referral filter, prevalence, selection bias, comorbidity, another diagnosis | Does the case belong to the proposed class and does the relation matter clinically? |

Whole-person context is strongest when it modifies a named node or arrow. A generic list at the end of the chapter is not an adequate alternative analysis.

## Figure rules

1. Use redundant shape, line style, and text labels; never rely on color alone.
2. Put an evidence-state label on every load-bearing node or arrow.
3. Use gates, branches, or separate panels where one relation is conditional.
4. Show alternatives beside the affected arrow rather than in a detached disclaimer box.
5. Distinguish the current observed state from reconstructed exposure history.
6. Do not use a single narrowing, red pinch mark, glowing line, or posture silhouette as a complete mechanism.
7. Do not draw an arrow from mechanics directly to a syndrome name when tissue response, function, symptoms, and criteria are separate questions.
8. Use a question mark or unresolved cell only with a text description of what evidence is missing.
9. Caption what the figure cannot establish.
10. Ensure the text alternative preserves branches, evidence states, and missing links rather than merely naming visible anatomy.

## Acceptance criteria

The grammar is functioning when:

- every node names an object rather than using *load* or *mechanics* as a catch-all;
- each arrow has its own proposition, posture, evidence state, and alternative;
- task, geometry, external demand, local exposure, response, function, symptom, and diagnosis remain distinct;
- reconstructed histories disclose their observed and inferred components;
- time permits recovery, adaptation, retention, substitution, reverse causality, and unresolved paths;
- whole-person factors enter specific nodes or bridges;
- null and low-mechanical-priority outcomes remain legitimate;
- a figure cannot look more certain than its claim ledger; and
- Chapter 27 can reuse the grammar without turning it into a diagnostic score or self-test.
