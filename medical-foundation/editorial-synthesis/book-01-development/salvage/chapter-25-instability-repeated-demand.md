## Recovery decision

The archive proposes that a small local loss of control can require repeated correction, bracing, altered timing, and secondary compensation. If those responses persist, it proposes, they can change the demand received locally and elsewhere. Its strongest versions then move directly from segmental instability to proprioceptive disturbance, chronic global tension, energetic cost, pain, degeneration, recurrent injury, systemic dysfunction, and correction through posterior loading and breath.

That program should be preserved as a sequence of researchable questions. It should not be flattened into the weak observation that recurrence is multifactorial, and it should not be published as one completed cascade. Chapter 25 can recover its positive mechanical center through a state-transition model:

```text
state before task n
    + task, environment, and exposure n
    -> task behavior and internal distribution n
    -> success, perturbation, giving way, injury, or no event
    -> material, sensory, motor, experiential, and behavioral response
    -> recovery, adaptation, retained change, or unresolved state
    -> state before task n+1
```

The key claim is not that every episode worsens the system. It is that a later event cannot automatically be modeled as the same demand applied to an unchanged receiver. Injury, healing, swelling, pain, fatigue, training, confidence, learned protection, environmental support, exposure, and ordinary development may all change the next starting state. Some changes restore or increase capacity. Some protect the task without an important later cost. Some create a measurable local or regional redistribution. Some proposed links will remain absent, nonspecific, or lower priority.

Recurrent lateral ankle instability is selected as the lead because it permits material restraint, passive laxity, perceived giving way, task performance, time-resolved control, exposure, recurrence, and noncorrespondence to be compared without assuming that any one variable defines the others. The chapter will not present “chronic ankle instability” as one homogeneous mechanism or use a diagnostic label as evidence. It will construct a source-grounded comparison across clearly described cohorts, tasks, measures, and times.

Spinal segmental control remains the axial transfer question. The transfer should ask whether the state-transition logic survives when the segment is harder to isolate, passive motion and active control are more coupled, ordinary imaging and symptoms correspond variably, and the relevant internal exposure is less directly measured. Sacroiliac, costovertebral, rib, and other archive candidates remain in the inventory rather than being treated as established variants of the ankle account.

## Source-obligation reconciliation

### BGB-S-1116 — segmental instability, repeated correction, bracing, and secondary compensation

**Preserved:** A local relation may fail to remain within task-sufficient bounds or may require repeated active correction. Bracing, co-contraction, altered foot placement, external support, reduced excursion, and changed timing can preserve function while changing stiffness, force, work, variability, or exposure.

**Reconstruction:** The chapter must distinguish a material restraint state, passive displacement under a declared test, a task-level event, a reported experience, and an inferred mechanism. Repeated correction is not automatically injury, inefficiency, or proof of a hidden lesion. Bracing can be a successful solution. A possible cost requires a denominator and timeframe.

**Protected future:** The archive's spinal instability, deep-versus-global recruitment, proprioceptive feedback, energetic, degenerative, and systemic hypotheses remain active. Their promotion requires region-specific measurement and causal bridges rather than analogy from ankle recurrence.

### BGB-S-1118 — timing and sequencing distinct from strength and material failure

**Preserved:** A task can depend on when force develops, how motion and contact are sequenced, how a perturbation is detected, and how the relation is recovered. Maximum strength and static shape do not specify those time-dependent relations.

**Reconstruction:** A timing difference is meaningful only relative to an event marker, task, direction, signal definition, comparison, and consequence. Surface electromyography, motion, force, and pressure observe different objects. Cross-sectional timing differences cannot by themselves establish whether the difference preceded an injury, followed pain, reflects training, or contributes to recurrence.

### BGB-S-1120 — kinetic-chain rerouting and delayed compensatory cost

**Preserved:** Protecting one joint or altering one task strategy can change contacts, moments, work, power, motion, or exposure at adjacent segments, the other limb, or the environment. A local event can therefore motivate a legitimate multiscale routing question.

**Reconstruction:** “The chain compensates” is not a measurement. The chapter advances one scale only when it names the task, segment, carrier, quantity, direction, time, receiver, and evidence form. A proximal kinematic difference is not automatically proximal tissue overload. A contralateral difference is not automatically caused by the ankle. A downstream symptom does not identify the initiating route.

**Null:** The local protective strategy may have no detected proximal or contralateral cost, may reduce a selected demand, may be transient, or may be indistinguishable from ordinary variation.

### BGB-S-1108 and BGB-S-1306 — a mechanically intelligible case and instability as a core pattern

**Preserved:** Chapter 25 should be capable of showing a substantial mechanical contribution, not merely listing alternatives. A documented injury can change ligament behavior, joint mechanics, task strategy, and later exposure; a recurrent-control problem can therefore be a strong mechanical narrative.

**Reconstruction:** Instability is an analytical family, not a diagnosis or verdict. It earns priority only for the declared question and endpoint. The same person may have a well-supported material relation, an uncertain recurrence mechanism, and a symptom pattern for which the added routing account has little value.

### BGB-S-1216 and BGB-S-1218 — handoff, congruence, timing, fatigue, and rerouting

**Preserved:** Joint contact, passive restraint, active support, timing, fatigue, prior injury, habit, repetition, and recovery can alter how a task is resolved. The ankle can make these variables concrete without requiring a literal single path through fixed tissue classes.

**Hard/soft allocation:** The distinction is motivated by TCM hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts but is not definitionally identical to either. Bone, articular geometry, ligament, muscle, tendon, ground, footwear, and brace can take more shape-preserving roles in a selected direction and phase; cartilage, synovial structures, skin, vessel, nerve, and other structures can take more form-receiving roles. Roles are relational. A ligament can preserve a relation while deforming; a muscle can yield while controlling motion; cartilage receives and redistributes contact while bearing load. No tissue is classified permanently as hard or soft.

### BGB-S-1210 and BGB-S-1212 — posterior capacity and observable markers

**Preserved:** Capacity, performance, fatigue, stability, injury exposure, pain, balance, compensation, and transitions can be represented as distinct variables when a source supplies a defined observation. Chapter 25 uses source-specific passive, task, waveform, report, recurrence, and recovery measures to compare selected states.

**Protected future:** The posterior-specific interpretation remains an active later-book proposition rather than an explanation imposed on the ankle evidence. Chapter 25 does not convert its selected measures into a self-screen or general marker set; Chapters 27 and 29 own the reusable measurement scaffold and measurement-development program.

### BGB-S-1301 and BGB-S-1305 — mechanism-first, cumulative, bounded medical framing

**Preserved:** Build the episode sequence before discussing diagnosis or care. Keep external demand, internal distribution, local exposure, response, finding, symptom, and function distinguishable. Let repetition matter through changing state, dose, and recovery.

**Boundary:** The chapter can explain a mechanical state-transition model and compare observations. It cannot supply diagnostic criteria, a recurrence score, return-to-activity rule, prognosis, brace recommendation, exercise, neuromuscular program, posterior correction, breath cue, or clinical protocol.

### BGB-S-1121, BGB-S-1311, and BGB-S-1312 — candidate inventory, delayed consequence, and generated vignette

**Preserved:** Spinal, SI, costovertebral, rib, gait, knee, hip, shoulder, craniofacial, and other recurrent-control settings remain candidates for later development. A changed strategy can persist between events and can alter a future starting state without one dramatic new injury.

**Reconstruction:** “Delayed” means that the proposed exposure and outcome are separated in time; it does not prove that the earlier event caused the later one. “Silent” means the selected change was not captured by the chosen observation or ordinary function continued; it does not mean occult damage. Delayed, distal, and systemic claims require a dated chain, intermediate measures, alternatives, and a disfavoring result.

**Protected/quarantined:** The generated office-worker micro-vignette remains assigned to Chapter 26 and cannot serve as a source-grounded Chapter 25 case. Chapter 25 preserves only its missing-history and competing-explanation discipline.

## Structural recovery from the archive

The archive's intended sequence can be retained in full if its links remain separate:

```text
material change, ordinary variation, fatigue, or altered exposure
    -> changed restraint, contact, sensory input, or active strategy
    -> task-specific relation loss or repeated correction
    -> successful protection, giving way, new injury, or no important event
    -> swelling, pain, fatigue, confidence, learning, and behavior
    -> recovery, adaptation, persistent strategy, or reduced capacity
    -> changed exposure and starting state
    -> possible local, proximal, contralateral, or later consequence
```

The sequence is branching rather than inevitable. A useful chapter must include:

- documented acute injury and unclear-origin paths;
- passive laxity with capable function;
- perceived instability without the selected passive or task-level measure;
- task-level difference without later recurrence;
- recovery and increased capacity;
- persistent but useful protection;
- exposure-led recurrence;
- pain- or fear-led strategy change;
- an unmeasured or nonunique history; and
- a complete no-added-value result.

These branches make a strong mechanical result more meaningful because it must compete with outcomes the framework permits rather than absorbing them all.

## Clean conceptual recovery

### Preserve directly

- instability is task-, direction-, phase-, and tolerance-specific;
- mobility and variability are not instability by definition;
- passive laxity, functional instability, perceived instability, pain, and confidence are different variables;
- a recurrent event occurs in a state changed by prior exposure and recovery;
- timing and sequencing can matter independently of maximum strength;
- passive restraint, active support, sensation, behavior, and environment can interact without being interchangeable;
- bracing and co-contraction can be useful shape-preserving responses;
- compensation begins by protecting a task, structure, confidence, or participation;
- repeated correction can create a measurable history of activation, force, work, effort, or restricted options;
- a changed local strategy can motivate proximal or contralateral measurement;
- adaptation, successful return, and no later cost are affirmative branches;
- an injury can have a strong local mechanical role without explaining every symptom or recurrence; and
- low mechanical priority for one endpoint does not mean mechanics is absent from the event.

### Reconstruct rather than erase

- “looseness causes instability” becomes a comparison among passive restraint, dynamic task behavior, reported experience, and recurrence;
- “proprioception is damaged” becomes measure-specific sensory or sensorimotor findings with direction and chronology left open;
- “delayed muscle activation causes giving way” becomes an event-locked timing proposition with task, signal, comparator, reliability, and outcome;
- “the body braces around instability” becomes a measured or hypothesized change in co-contraction, stiffness, excursion, foot placement, support, or behavior;
- “repeated correction wastes energy” becomes a task-specific comparison of activation, mechanical work, metabolic cost, effort, success, and recovery;
- “each sprain weakens the ankle further” becomes a state timeline with injury, healing, adaptation, exposure, and null branches;
- “the hip compensates for the ankle” becomes a named proximal kinematic, kinetic, work, or muscle relation under the same task;
- “the other side takes the load” becomes bilateral or contralateral contact and distribution evidence with causal alternatives;
- “local instability becomes whole-body dysfunction” becomes a series of scale bridges whose failure can stop the claim; and
- “restore stability to prevent recurrence” becomes a later intervention hypothesis requiring target, comparator, adherence, safety, mediator, and outcome evidence.

### Protect forward

- spinal, atlantoaxial, facet, SI, costovertebral, rib, craniofacial, and other diagnostic candidates;
- local instability as a general initiator of degeneration, pain, fatigue, or systemic illness;
- chronic bracing as a whole-body energetic, autonomic, immune, endocrine, or recovery burden;
- a preferred global configuration or generic efficiency metric;
- skeletal-primary routing as a Book II modeling assumption;
- posterior participation as possibly necessary but insufficient for preferred organization;
- axial, rotational, and expansive signed modes and local-frame transformations;
- Six-Division, channel, force-line, and TCM correspondences;
- breath, pressure, phase, hyoid, bandha, and torus organization;
- assessment, self-observation, stabilization, correction, retraining, yoga, tai chi, rehabilitation, and professional practice; and
- diagnosis-, prognosis-, prevention-, referral-, or treatment-specific claims.

Protection means later work can test these propositions without Book I implying their answer.

## Reader-facing sequence

1. **Open with superficially similar recurrence and different states.** Use a clearly disclosed multistudy comparison or source-grounded composite, not an invented diagnostic story.
2. **Declare the event and task.** Name what counts as giving way, sprain, perturbation, landing, cutting, walking, or another event and what was measured.
3. **Reconstruct the first relevant state.** Separate documented lesion, passive restraint, swelling, pain, range, strength, sensory measure, exposure, and behavior.
4. **Build the three-way correspondence table.** Compare material laxity, task-level stability, and perceived instability, including every noncorresponding cell.
5. **Follow one task through time.** Place contact, motion, force, activation, perturbation, correction, and recovery on one clock without treating any signal as the whole mechanism.
6. **Make repetition a state transition.** Show how the event, response, recovery, training, confidence, and exposure set the next starting state.
7. **Treat compensation first as protection.** State what function, participation, tissue, or confidence the strategy preserves.
8. **Test local and proximal redistribution separately.** Require a carrier and quantity at each scale; allow the proposed route to stop at the ankle.
9. **Overlay only the patterns that add information.** Instability, discoordination, substitution, concentration, constraint, and compensation remain analytical views rather than accumulated diagnoses.
10. **Compare competing histories.** Include exposure, new acute events, incomplete recovery, morphology, generalized laxity, pain, fear, learning, conditioning, environment, care, and measurement error.
11. **Run the nulls.** Let passive laxity, perceived instability, task difference, recurrence, and proximal change fail to correspond.
12. **Transfer the state logic to the spine.** Carry only the relational questions and leave ankle-specific variables and thresholds behind.
13. **Close with a bounded result.** State the supported local links, reconstructed transitions, weakest consequential arrow, alternative, missing observation, and point at which the account stops.

No examination maneuver, test battery, diagnostic score, return-to-sport rule, exercise, cue, brace selection, or treatment sequence belongs in the reader narrative.
