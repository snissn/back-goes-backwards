## Recovery decision

The archive's ambition extends beyond explaining anatomy. It proposes a new public language for mechanical health, professional education across disciplines, redesigned workplaces and schools, earlier prevention, institutional adoption, and eventually a cultural change in how people understand their bodies. It also contains universal posture advice, posterior-loading cues, home tests, movement drills, breathing practices, standard workspace recommendations, and claims that modern activity patterns drive pain, fatigue, inflammation, chronic disease, or systemic decline.

Chapter 28 should preserve the large ambition while splitting it into development tracks with different outcomes and burdens:

```text
public mechanical literacy
professional shared language
environmental and task design
accessible participation and option space
comfort and task-tolerance outcomes
injury and symptom prevention
disease and population-health outcomes
institutional and cultural transformation
```

Evidence at one level does not automatically carry the next. A person may understand a task better without changing behavior. A redesigned tool may change support or reach without improving comfort. Comfort may improve without altering injury risk. An injury-prevention result does not establish disease prevention. The ladder protects the stronger program by making its intermediate work visible rather than shrinking it to generic advice or publishing it as already proven.

The chapter can take two affirmative positions now:

1. mechanical literacy can be defined as a richer, non-diagnostic language of task, demand, support, duration, variability, capacity, fatigue, recovery, and available options; and
2. environments are real mechanical boundary conditions that can expand or restrict the strategies available to differently situated people.

Specific claims that an educational or design program improves comprehension, autonomy, comfort, participation, symptoms, injury, disease, or population health remain outcome-specific empirical questions.

## Source-obligation reconciliation

### BGB-S-1122 and BGB-S-1222 — environment as a mechanical boundary condition

**Preserved:** A workspace, classroom, home, vehicle, tool, support, device, object location, visual target, schedule, or assistance arrangement can change contacts, reach, direction, duration, pace, repetition, available rest, and the set of strategies a person can use. Those are genuine changes to the person–task–environment system.

**Reconstruction:** An ergonomic arrangement is not inherently health-promoting. It may reduce one demand while increasing another, improve one outcome while leaving another unchanged, or work differently for people with different bodies, tasks, capacities, preferences, and control. “Sitting,” “screen time,” “manual work,” or “device use” is too coarse to specify an exposure.

**Protected forward:** Exact equipment placement, chair design, break schedules, task rotation, wearable feedback, individualized workspace assessment, and intervention programs require population-, task-, outcome-, and feasibility-specific evidence.

### BGB-S-1124 — modern exposure and the larger health ambition

**Preserved:** Contemporary environments can produce long durations, specialized repetition, constrained visual and hand demands, limited variation, high pace, low control, insufficient recovery, or reduced access to alternative strategies. Those combinations deserve mechanical study. The archive's concern that modern organization may affect injury, symptoms, fatigue, reserve, and wider health remains an active research program.

**Reconstruction:** Modernity is not a diagnosis, and ordinary activity categories are not harmful by definition. The chapter must represent beneficial work, sport, skill, device use, support, and sitting alongside possible burdens. Each proposed consequence requires its own chain from task through exposure, response, and outcome.

**Quarantined present moves:** Screen work causes “tech neck”; sitting weakens the posterior chain; repetition inevitably produces microtrauma; constrained posture causes systemic inflammation or fatigue; environmental redesign prevents chronic disease. These remain candidate questions only after their intermediate objects are specified.

### BGB-S-1316 — bounded education, prevention, and research implications

**Preserved:** Book I can identify a useful educational object and a set of testable prevention questions before the stronger program is mature. It can argue that task demand, support, variability, capacity, and recovery deserve explicit language in public and professional reasoning.

**Reconstruction:** “Prevention” always names a population, proposal, comparator, outcome, time, measurement, alternatives, harms, and implementation context. Knowledge, communication, recognized options, comfort, task tolerance, participation, fatigue, symptoms, injury, recurrence, disease, and population health are not pooled.

### BGB-S-1613 and BGB-S-1614 — teaching without hidden diagnosis

**Preserved:** Clear language, diagrams, ordinary tasks, embodied reports, and multiple modes of explanation can make mechanics more understandable. A shared vocabulary may help disciplines coordinate questions and help people describe their environments and experiences.

**Reconstruction:** Education is not agreement with the project. Its first outcomes are comprehension, recall, usability, communication, and unintended effects. Embodied learning does not establish internal force paths or disease. Instruction should expand recognized options rather than teach continuous self-surveillance for hidden faults.

**Protected forward:** Specific teaching sequences, demonstrations, exercises, breath practices, force-line imagery, TCM correspondence, posterior organization, and individualized self-experimentation remain Book V material.

### BGB-S-1618, BGB-S-1619, BGB-S-1620, and BGB-S-1621 — lifelong learning, institutions, integration, and cultural change

**Preserved:** The project ultimately intends education in communities and schools, professional development, changes in health systems, and a culture that treats mechanics as a routine dimension of health. It also treats embodiment as lifelong iteration rather than attainment of a perfect endpoint, intends eventually to integrate structure, breath, and traditional observation, and invites practitioners, teachers, and scientists to contribute observations and findings. These are substantive authorial aims, not decorative closing material.

**Reconstruction:** Institutional ambition becomes an implementation program. It must define adopters, users, settings, training, fidelity, resources, incentives, access, equity, opportunity costs, harms, outcomes, and comparison with existing approaches. A compelling framework does not establish that adoption improves care or health.

**Protected future:** Chapter 29 owns staged research, contribution, and implementation designs. Later books may deepen the structure, breath, and traditional content being taught. Book V owns practice-facing instruction and lifelong iteration. The series can remain revisionary in ambition while Book I remains precise about present maturity.

## Layered application model

### Layer 1 — public mechanical literacy

**Object:** comprehension and usable language.

People should be able to describe what a task asks, what supports and alternatives exist, how demand changes with time, and where fatigue or recovery enters. They should also understand that internal stress, strain, and load paths usually cannot be felt or inferred uniquely from appearance.

**Outcomes:** comprehension, recall, communication, recognized options, appropriate uncertainty, and possible adverse vigilance.

### Layer 2 — professional shared language

**Object:** coordination across existing disciplines.

Physicians, nurses, therapists, movement professionals, coaches, ergonomists, engineers, designers, educators, occupational-health teams, and researchers retain their own scope and methods. A shared task–configuration–distribution–exposure–response grammar may improve handoffs and research questions; that benefit must be evaluated rather than presumed.

**Outcomes:** communication accuracy, question quality, documentation, duplication, burden, and decision relevance.

### Layer 3 — environmental and task design

**Object:** boundary conditions and realistic option space.

Design may change reach, support, visual demand, tool relation, assistance, pace, task sequence, rotation, recovery opportunities, movement access, and who controls those variables. It should compare changing the environment with asking the person to maintain a correction.

**Outcomes:** usability, accessibility, participation, comfort, performance, fatigue, task tolerance, tradeoffs, and unintended redistribution.

### Layer 4 — prevention

**Object:** a named adverse outcome in a named population and time.

The claim ladder proceeds from knowledge and communication through comfort, participation, symptoms, injury, recurrence, disease, and population health. Each rung requires an intervention, comparator, measurement, follow-up, alternative explanation, and harms assessment.

**Outcomes:** never inherited from a lower rung.

### Layer 5 — institutional and cultural development

**Object:** sustainable adoption, access, and consequence across systems.

Schools, workplaces, communities, professional curricula, healthcare organizations, and public agencies may eventually teach or operationalize parts of the framework. Development must address fidelity, resources, professional scope, equity, opportunity cost, governance, and whether existing programs perform better.

**Outcomes:** reach, adoption, implementation quality, equity, cost, unintended effects, and the particular educational or health endpoint claimed.

## Modern exposure as a conditional sequence

The archive's modern-life narrative should be redrawn as branching rather than degenerative by default:

```text
technology, work, care, transport, sport, or environment
    -> task duration, pace, repetition, support, variation, and control
    -> external demand and available strategies
    -> exposure relative to changing capacity and recovery
    -> adaptation, capable specialization, stable tolerance, fatigue,
       symptom modulation, injury, or no important effect
    -> behavior, care, environment, and later state change
```

Manual work, desk work, sport, caregiving, supported movement, and assistive-device use belong in the same model. Their consequences cannot be ranked from labels. The model must preserve the possibility that specialization produces valued skill and capacity, that sitting is comfortable and appropriate, that assistance expands participation, and that a change intended to increase movement can reduce access or concentration.

## Accessibility and option-space constraints

Option space is not the maximum number of theoretically possible movements. It is the set of strategies a person can realistically use given body, task, pain, fatigue, disability, pregnancy, health, culture, assistance, cost, schedule, safety, job control, and competing priorities. More options may help, overwhelm, conflict, or redistribute demand.

The chapter's design test is:

1. Whose task and outcome are being optimized?
2. What variables can the person actually control?
3. What supports or assistance make participation possible?
4. Which demand is reduced, and which may increase?
5. Does the arrangement expand realistic options or impose one norm?
6. What does the user report, and what is measured separately?
7. What tradeoffs, costs, exclusions, or unintended effects appear?
8. Does the result generalize beyond this person, task, or setting?

Unsupported movement, symmetry, independence, youth, thinness, strength, stillness, or constant variability cannot serve as the hidden design standard.

## Prevention-claim ladder

| Level | Example claim object | Minimum evidence question | Claim that cannot yet follow |
|---|---|---|---|
| Understanding | learner identifies task, support, duration, variation, and unknowns | Was comprehension measured for this audience? | behavior or health changed |
| Communication | people or professions describe the same task more accurately | Did handoff, agreement, or question quality improve? | decisions or outcomes improved |
| Recognized options | user identifies feasible supports or task alternatives | Were options usable and autonomy-preserving? | exposure decreased |
| Comfort or task tolerance | reported comfort or sustained performance changes | Compared with what, for whom, and for how long? | injury or disease was prevented |
| Participation or fatigue | access, completion, or selected fatigue outcome changes | What demand shifted and what tradeoff occurred? | symptoms or tissue exposure changed |
| Symptoms | incidence, recurrence, severity, or interference changes | Was the outcome prespecified and follow-up adequate? | mechanism or disease prevention established |
| Injury | defined event risk changes | Were exposure opportunity, adherence, harms, and competing events measured? | general health improved |
| Disease | a defined disease endpoint changes | Is there a condition-specific causal and intervention chain? | population benefit or cost effectiveness follows |
| Population health | distribution of benefits, harms, access, and costs changes | Did implementation reach intended groups equitably and sustainably? | the framework should be universal policy |

The ladder orders evidentiary burden, not human value. A comfort or access improvement can be important without being marketed as disease prevention.

## Example program

### Primary comparison

Use an ordinary task—such as a shared computer station, classroom activity, caregiving transfer, manual assembly task, or public-service counter—to compare two responses:

- instruct every user to maintain one presumed correct shape; and
- change support, reach, visual relation, assistance, timing, or task arrangement so several viable strategies become available.

The example must not specify universal dimensions or imply that environmental change always wins. It should expose which outcomes are measured and which remain unknown.

### Required noncorresponding example

Include a standardized “ergonomic” recommendation that reduces comfort, access, function, concentration, or autonomy for at least one differently situated user—or an environmental change that improves comfort but leaves a stronger symptom or injury outcome unchanged. The point is not that standards are bad. It is that preference, fit, comfort, access, exposure, and prevention are distinct.

### Institutional example

Show how a school, workplace, or clinical curriculum could test mechanical literacy through comprehension and communication before claiming health benefit. Include the possibility that the material increases vigilance, blame, or cognitive burden and therefore needs revision.

## Evidence-development obligations

The Chapter 28 evidence packet must support:

1. health literacy, pedagogy, and unintended effects of education;
2. ergonomics and occupational-health definitions and limits;
3. person–task–environment and participatory or universal-design principles;
4. accessibility, disability, pregnancy, aging, chronic illness, body diversity, job control, resources, and unequal agency as design variables;
5. exposure characterization for sitting, repetition, screen and hand tasks, manual work, care, sport, and assistance without treating category labels as dose;
6. positive, null, heterogeneous, and adverse results for selected educational or environmental interventions;
7. exact separation among comfort, task tolerance, fatigue, participation, symptoms, injury, disease, and population outcomes;
8. implementation and equity questions for institutional development; and
9. claim-by-claim statements of what cannot be inferred.

Primary studies should lead exact intervention and outcome claims. Authoritative frameworks and systematic reviews may establish field structure and heterogeneity. No generic ergonomic or educational source may be broadened into systemic-health evidence.
