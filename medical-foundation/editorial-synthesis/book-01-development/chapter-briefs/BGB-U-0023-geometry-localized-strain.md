## Chapter job

This chapter gives Book I its first complete worked mechanical narrative. It must take an inspectable task and anatomical system from external demand through configuration, internal distribution, localized exposure, tissue response, findings, symptoms, alternatives, and a credible null outcome. Its distinctive work is to show how geometry can redistribute demand and create a local concentration without making visible alignment, a modeled stress peak, an imaging feature, or a symptom prove the next link in the chain.

The chapter also tests transfer. The leading system remains deliberately unresolved between a lumbar motion segment and a limb joint. The brief therefore preserves two valid architectures: a coupled disc–facet–endplate system followed by a knee or hip comparison, or a knee or hip lead followed by a lumbar transfer test. Evidence quality, clarity of system boundary, and the ability to represent noncorrespondence should determine the eventual lead; the archive's strong spinal intent is not discarded while that decision remains open.

**This chapter must:**

- use the shared Part V sequence: observation → task and environment → configuration and available paths → external demand and internal distribution → local stress/strain/contact → tissue response through time → findings or symptoms → alternatives and discriminating observations;
- distinguish what is observed or measured from what is calculated, modeled, reconstructed, or hypothesized at every consequential step;
- show why the same external demand can have different internal consequences under different geometry, support, active control, material state, and timescale;
- include a transfer comparison that reveals both a general principle and a region-specific limit; and
- allow the conclusion that the proposed geometry-to-strain route adds little in a particular case.

**This chapter must not:**

- treat a posture, curve, joint angle, or imaging feature as a diagnosis or sufficient cause of pain or degeneration;
- repeat Chapter 12's generic concentration model or Chapter 19's general mechanobiology as if either were already a case;
- decide that lumbar anatomy must lead merely because it dominates the archive, or that a limb joint must lead merely because its measurements may be cleaner;
- use posterior preference, the signed generalized modes, or a preferred global configuration to complete an otherwise unsupported chain; or
- provide correction, exercise, self-test, or treatment guidance.

## Reader entry and exit

**The reader enters** knowing that geometry can alter moment arms, contact, and local exposure, but not yet having followed those ideas through one complete anatomical and medical example.

**The reader exits** able to construct and criticize a geometry-to-strain account: declare the system and task, identify what changed mechanically, track the proposed local exposure, separate tissue response from symptoms, compare another region, and state what observations would weaken the entire interpretation.

## Central question

When does a difference in geometry meaningfully redistribute demand into localized tissue strain, and what evidence is required before that mechanical account bears on findings or symptoms?

## Working answer

For a declared task and system, geometry can change line of action, moment arms, contact relations, muscular demand, and boundary conditions. Those changes can redistribute stress and strain within heterogeneous tissues, especially when dose, material state, and recovery reduce available capacity. The resulting account is mechanically useful only when each scale transition is made explicit. A visible configuration does not uniquely determine internal loading; a modeled concentration is not damage; a tissue response is not necessarily symptomatic; and a symptom may have another or mixed explanation. In some cases the geometry-to-strain route will be well supported, in some only one influence among several, and in others it will add little.

## Narrative arc

Open with two configurations carrying a superficially similar external demand but producing different internal moment and contact questions. Define the anatomical system and task before naming a lesion or problem. Follow the demand from the whole segment or joint into local stress and strain, then add dose, material state, recovery, and adaptation. Only after that should the chapter consider findings and symptoms. A second anatomical region then tests which parts of the explanation transfer. The chapter closes by comparing the proposed route with morphology, biology, activity history, sensitization, and a genuine null case.

## Argument sequence

### Opening — the same demand does not guarantee the same exposure

Use a bounded comparison rather than a diagnostic vignette: the same external weight, support, or task applied across two declared configurations of the candidate system. Show only what is directly observable at first. The opening should make the internal-distribution question unavoidable without asserting that one configuration is pathological.

If lumbar anatomy leads, use a source-grounded task model rather than an invented patient history. If a limb joint leads, choose a task whose contacts and kinematics can be described without implying that the laboratory task represents all daily movement.

### 23.1 Define the system before naming the problem

**Job:** Establish the boundary, task, reference state, structures, contacts, timescale, and evidence states required by the case.

For the lumbar candidate, specify the vertebral bodies, disc, endplates, facets, ligaments, relevant muscles, neighboring support, and external contacts included. For a knee or hip candidate, specify the corresponding articular surfaces, passive restraints, active contributors, and ground or apparatus contacts. Record the presenting observation separately from any causal interpretation.

Create a case-state table with columns for **observed/measured**, **calculated**, **modeled**, **reconstructed/inferred**, and **unknown**. This table should travel through the entire chapter rather than appear only as a methodological disclaimer.

**Exit:** The reader knows what body is being analyzed, what demand crosses its boundary, and which parts of the account are facts versus proposed relations.

### 23.2 How geometry changes moments and contact

**Job:** Translate the observed configuration into bounded mechanical consequences without claiming a unique internal solution.

Trace line of action, moment arm, curvature or joint orientation, contact location, support, and active muscular demand. Use a free-body diagram or accepted musculoskeletal model only after its assumptions are declared. Show how active control, neighboring motion, external support, speed, and co-contraction can produce more than one internal distribution for apparently similar geometry.

**Exit:** Geometry has become a set of testable moment, contact, and control questions rather than a visual judgment.

### 23.3 From whole-segment demand to localized stress and strain

**Job:** Cross scales from segment or joint resultants into the heterogeneous tissue exposure that matters biologically.

Follow compression, shear, bending, torsion, contact pressure, or combined loading into the selected tissues. Include contact area, stiffness contrast, material heterogeneity, anisotropy where relevant, fluid or poroelastic behavior where relevant, and boundary conditions. Do not imply that a scalar external load determines one scalar tissue exposure.

**Exit:** The candidate local concentration is explicit, tissue-specific, and visibly dependent on a model rather than inferred from geometry alone.

### 23.4 Dose, material state, and changing capacity

**Job:** Give the local exposure a history and a receiver.

Add magnitude, direction, rate, repetition, variability, duration, recovery, hydration where relevant, age, development, prior injury, fatigue, inflammatory or metabolic state, and remodeling. Preserve the archive's important intuition that repeated low-grade demand may matter while allowing adaptation, tolerance, neutral variation, and recovery to remain real outcomes.

**Exit:** The reader can distinguish a momentary stress distribution from a cumulative exposure whose significance depends on changing tissue capacity.

### 23.5 From tissue response to findings and symptoms

**Job:** Keep the biological and clinical arrows separate.

Trace only supported possibilities such as remodeling, matrix change, irritation, degeneration, repair, or failure. Then ask whether an imaging feature, functional change, or symptom corresponds to that response. Include asymptomatic findings, symptoms without the selected finding, and improvement without structural normalization. The chapter should not need one universal pain generator to preserve mechanical relevance.

**Exit:** A coherent mechanical exposure remains informative without being mistaken for a complete clinical explanation.

### 23.6 A cross-region transfer test

**Job:** Identify which relations generalize and which are anatomical particulars.

If the lumbar system leads, compare one knee or hip task; if a limb joint leads, test the lumbar disc–facet–endplate system. Carry the same columns—boundary, task, geometry, distribution, local exposure, material state, response, finding, symptom, alternatives—through the comparison. Do not make the second region a miniature second case; its job is to expose transfer and failure of transfer.

**Exit:** The reader has a general method, not a spine-specific or limb-specific slogan.

### 23.7 Competing explanations, discriminating observations, and the null

**Job:** Determine how much explanatory work the proposed route actually performs.

Compare morphology, genetics, development, inflammation, acute injury, total activity exposure, deconditioning, sensitization, expectation, measurement error, and incidental findings. Specify observations that would favor, weaken, or leave unresolved each arrow. Include a credible null: the observed geometry may not correspond to a meaningful difference in local exposure; the modeled exposure may remain within capacity; the finding may be common in asymptomatic people; or symptoms and function may track another variable better. In that outcome, the framework has still improved the question by showing why the geometric account should receive low priority in this case.

**Exit:** The case reaches an evidence-proportionate interpretation rather than a required confirmation.

### Conclusion and handoff

Reconstruct the complete chain in one page or figure, with evidence states and weakest arrows visible. State what the example establishes about geometry and distribution, what remains specific to the selected anatomy, and what did not follow. Chapter 24 inherits the same discipline but changes the central problem: not a local concentration inside a load-bearing structure, but a compliant structure whose deformation or excursion may be maintained by surrounding demand.

1. **Object represented:** external demand, internal distribution, local tissue exposure, and the staged clinical chain.
2. **System boundary and scale:** one declared lumbar or limb-joint task, with segment/joint and tissue scales shown separately.
3. **Variables and states:** forces, moments, contact, motion, stress, strain, material state, dose, response, findings, and symptoms; each marked measured, calculated, modeled, reconstructed, hypothesized, or unknown.

## Principal objections and responses

### “This is posture causation in more technical language.”

The chapter begins with task, boundary, contacts, support, active control, and time. Geometry opens a distribution question; it does not answer the exposure or clinical question by itself.

### “A modeled stress concentration is not a measured injury mechanism.”

Agreed. The evidence-state table and causal chain must keep modeled exposure, observed tissue response, findings, and symptoms separate. The model may organize a hypothesis without completing it.

### “Common asymptomatic findings make the example clinically irrelevant.”

They constrain the claim rather than erase the mechanics. The chapter must show whether the proposed exposure predicts anything beyond common morphology and allow the conclusion that it does not in the selected case.

### “A lumbar example cannot stand for every joint.”

The transfer comparison exists precisely to test that limitation. If the transferable relation is too thin or the lumbar case cannot be supported cleanly, a limb joint may lead instead.
