## Chapter job

This chapter establishes the dynamic baseline from which every Part III failure family departs. Earlier chapters have shown what a mechanical demand is, how living structures divide mechanical labor, and how ordinary anatomy can carry a task. Chapter 10 now explains why the same task, position, or nominal force can be viable in one history and difficult in another. It brings dose, capacity, recovery, and environmental boundary conditions into one time-dependent account without turning them into a universal score.

The chapter's distinctive contribution is a viable-range model of mechanical homeostasis. *Homeostasis* does not mean one posture, a geometrically perfect resting state, or the absence of strain. It names the proposed maintenance and recovery of task-relevant mechanical relations within ranges a living system can presently tolerate. That proposal becomes useful only when its scale, variables, task, and time window are stated. The chapter must therefore make homeostasis operational enough to guide later research while preserving several possible outcomes of exposure: accommodation, adaptation, neutral continuation, transient fatigue, sensitization, remodeling, injury, or failure.

**This chapter must:**

- define mechanical homeostasis as maintenance and recovery of viable dynamic ranges rather than a fixed shape;
- define capacity at the same scale, task, direction, and time horizon as the demand being considered;
- carry Chapter 2's multidimensional dose language into repeated and longitudinal exposure;
- show how environments shape boundary conditions without declaring modern life intrinsically pathological;
- keep adaptation, fatigue, sensitization, remodeling, injury, and failure separate; and
- establish the conditional threshold and changing margin needed by Chapters 11–16.

**This chapter must not:**

- classify a posture, task, occupation, age, or environment as healthy or harmful by appearance alone;
- treat capacity as a permanent scalar property of a tissue or person;
- assume that repeated load produces beneficial adaptation or cumulative damage;
- infer a biological response from a mechanical exposure before Chapter 19's tissue-specific bridge; or
- introduce corrective ergonomics, movement breaks, self-tests, or practice instructions assigned to later chapters and Book V.

## Reader entry and exit

**The reader enters** able to name force, stress, strain, pressure, deformation, and dose, but may still ask whether a posture or load is simply good or bad. They may picture tolerance as a fixed threshold and adaptation as the normal result of repetition.

**The reader exits** able to compare a defined exposure history with a changing, scale-specific capacity; identify how task and environment establish boundary conditions; distinguish several possible exposure outcomes; and explain why a snapshot cannot by itself establish whether a mechanical relation is viable, training, fatiguing, compensated, or consequential.

## Central question

When does a mechanical demand remain within a living system's viable range, and what additional information is required before repeated exposure can be called adaptive, neutral, fatiguing, sensitizing, remodeling, injurious, or failing?

## Working answer

A mechanical demand becomes meaningful through its full dose, the present capacity of the selected tissue, region, or person, the boundary conditions of the task and environment, and the history of work and recovery. Mechanical homeostasis is the proposed ability to maintain and recover viable task-relevant relations across those changing conditions. The same nominal exposure can support several outcomes, and any threshold is conditional on scale, direction, state, time, and the biological response being evaluated.

## Narrative arc

The chapter opens with one familiar action that changes meaning across a day rather than with a dramatic injury. It first replaces the fixed-posture image of homeostasis with a moving viable range. It then aligns capacity with the defined demand, expands dose into history, and places the environment at the system boundary. With those pieces in place, it shows why useful adaptation and adverse accumulation can begin from the same exposure. It closes by replacing a universal threshold with a changing margin and branching trajectory, thereby giving the rest of Part III a baseline against which interruption, concentration, constraint, instability, and compensation can be described.

## Argument sequence

### Opening — the same task in a different history

Return to `BGB-X-0001`. A person moves and carries the same object under several ordinary conditions: early in a rested day, after many repetitions, during incomplete recovery, and after becoming familiar with the task. Hold object mass and route approximately constant while changing history. The scene is a hypothetical teaching comparison, not a recommendation or documented clinical case. Its job is to show that neither the visible task nor its peak external force contains the whole answer.

The opening should name several possible outcomes without deciding among them. Repetition might improve coordination, leave performance unchanged, produce transient fatigue, reveal pain, provoke substitution, or exceed a tissue's current tolerance. This plurality creates the need for the chapter's model.

### 10.1 Mechanical homeostasis is a viable range, not a fixed shape

**Job:** Define the chapter's organizing hypothesis while removing fixed-posture and moral interpretations.

Mechanical homeostasis should be presented as a dynamic relation among specified mechanical quantities and living state. Depending on the question, the relevant variables might include contact, deformation, joint relation, pressure, excursion, stability, control effort, or return after perturbation. A viable range may contain asymmetry, movement, temporary strain, fatigue, and adaptation. It is not equivalent to a visually neutral pose.

Require every technical use of the term to identify the system, task, variable, range or comparison, and time window. If no such operationalization is available, the prose should retain *mechanical homeostasis* as project synthesis or hypothesis rather than established measurement.

**Exit:** Viability concerns a moving relation, so the next question is what the selected system can presently do.

### 10.2 Capacity is present ability, not a permanent tissue number

**Job:** Align capacity with the scale and exposure it is supposed to qualify.

Capacity can be discussed at tissue, interface, segment, task, or whole-person scale, but those levels cannot be exchanged without a bridge. Development, age, anatomy, training, prior injury, tissue state, sleep, pain, inflammation, illness, hydration, neural control, medication, recent loading, expectation, and available support may alter the current ability to meet or recover from a task. Some of these factors alter mechanics directly; others alter response or interpretation.

The chapter should distinguish the capacity to complete a task from the capacity to repeat it, recover from it, tolerate a particular local exposure, or preserve other options while doing it. Continued task success can coexist with declining reserve or increasing compensation, but neither may be inferred without measurement.

**Exit:** Capacity is conditional; therefore the demand must be described with matching temporal and directional detail.

### 10.3 Dose combines magnitude with direction, rate, repetition, and recovery

**Job:** Make `BGB-M-0003` do longitudinal work without inventing a universal dose equation.

Use matched small multiples: equal peak with different duration; equal nominal work with different rate; repeated unidirectional exposure versus variable sequencing; exposure separated by different recovery intervals. Direction and sign matter: the same magnitude in another orientation may engage different geometry, carriers, or material behavior. Sequence matters when one exposure changes the state encountered by the next.

Do not collapse mechanical dose and biological dose. The descriptor records the mechanical history that a later tissue-specific model will interpret. An area under an arbitrary curve is not a universal cumulative load score.

**Exit:** A dose exists within a task setting, so the chapter must now include the environment that shapes it.

### 10.4 Task and environment set boundary conditions

**Job:** Treat surroundings as mechanically consequential without condemning particular environments.

Seating, work surfaces, footwear, tools, screens, visual targets, carried objects, sport rules, vehicle use, assistance, and gravity can change external contacts, available movement, timing, repetition, and support. The same environment can be helpful, neutral, or demanding depending on task, fit, duration, variability, and the person's present capacity.

Modern environments belong here as measurable exposure contexts, not as a story of inevitable anterior collapse or disease. Workspace, device use, prolonged sitting, specialized work, and constrained visual attention are examples whose exact mechanical effects must be measured rather than supplied by cultural familiarity.

**Exit:** Once history and boundary conditions are visible, repeated exposure can no longer be assigned one automatic outcome.

### 10.5 Adaptation and accumulation can begin from the same exposure

**Job:** Build the outcome fork that prevents repetition from proving either benefit or harm.

Present several branches from a defined exposure: successful accommodation during the task; training or adaptation over a suitable interval; no material change; transient fatigue followed by recovery; sensitization or altered control; remodeling; injury; or task failure. These are not necessarily mutually exclusive, and some occur on different timescales. Prior state, dose, tissue biology, recovery, and environmental persistence help determine which branch is plausible.

Include a noncorresponding comparison: two people, or the same person at two times, can complete the same task with no meaningful adverse change despite visible differences in posture or strategy. The model earns value by explaining variation, not by redescribing every exposure as hidden overload.

**Exit:** Outcome branches make a single fixed threshold inadequate; what matters is a changing trajectory and margin.

### 10.6 The threshold is conditional and often gradual

**Job:** Replace the universal failure cliff with an inspectable time-dependent relation.

Use a provisional demand–capacity margin whose inputs remain separate rather than merged into an unsupported score. The system may compensate before symptoms, recover between exposures, lose reserve gradually, improve with training, or cross a task-specific threshold only when several conditions coincide. A snapshot can miss both recovery and cumulative change.

The model should permit hysteresis and history dependence without assuming them in every tissue. It should also show that pain, symptoms, or performance may change before, after, or independently of a measured mechanical threshold.

**Exit:** A longitudinal baseline is now available. Chapter 11 can ask how demand moves among available structures and what changes when a handoff is interrupted or reassigned.

### Conclusion and handoff

Return to the opening object transfer and explain why the nominal task was never enough. Mechanical viability depends on the dose that actually occurred, the selected system's present capacity, its history of recovery, and environmental boundary conditions. Homeostasis is the proposed maintenance of workable relations through those changes, not an ideal pose.

The chapter establishes no injury and prescribes no correction. It gives the next chapters a baseline: before calling a route interrupted, a stress concentrated, a structure constrained, or a compensation costly, the narrative must state the demand, dose, capacity, and time course against which the change is being judged.

## Model obligations

### `BGB-M-0003` — Time-structured exposure descriptor

1. **Object represented:** Mechanical exposure history.
2. **System boundary and scale:** Defined task, interface, segment, or tissue; never an unspecified whole-body “load.”
3. **Variables and states:** Named force, moment, pressure, strain, excursion, activation, or other quantity; direction, rate, duration, repetition, sequence, variability, and recovery interval.
4. **Assumptions:** Measurement frame, task phase, sampling window, and the reason a descriptor is relevant to the selected tissue or outcome.
5. **What the model explains:** Why matched peaks or nominal totals can represent different histories.
6. **What it cannot establish:** Biological response, harmfulness, adaptation, diagnosis, or one universal dose score.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Revise when the chosen descriptors do not predict or distinguish the selected exposure or response.
8. **Registry action:** Reuse `BGB-M-0003`; Chapter 10 should extend its application, not create a duplicate.

### Viable-range and changing-margin model

1. **Object represented:** The relation between a defined demand history and present capacity through time.
2. **System boundary and scale:** Tissue, interface, segment, task, or whole-person performance must be chosen explicitly.
3. **Variables and states:** Demand descriptors and capacity indicators remain separately observed, modeled, or inferred; no unsupported subtraction into a scalar score.
4. **Assumptions:** Task specificity, time window, recovery process, baseline variability, and biological modifiers.
5. **What the model explains:** Conditional thresholds, recovery, gradual reserve change, and why snapshots can mislead.
6. **What it cannot establish:** A universal optimum, health score, hidden reserve, or causal relation to symptoms.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Reject or narrow if viable outcomes cannot be operationalized or if established constructs fully cover the intended work.
8. **Registry action:** to create.

### Exposure-outcome fork

1. **Object represented:** Competing trajectories after a defined exposure.
2. **System boundary and scale:** Mechanical history followed by separately bounded functional and biological observations.
3. **Variables and states:** Accommodation, adaptation, no change, fatigue, sensitization, remodeling, injury, and failure, each labeled by evidence state.
4. **Assumptions:** Branches may coexist or occur on different timescales.
5. **What the model explains:** Why repetition alone proves neither benefit nor harm.
6. **What it cannot establish:** Which branch occurred in an individual case.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Revise if branches cannot be defined without category overlap.
8. **Registry action:** to create.

## Principal objections and responses

### “Mechanical homeostasis is just a new label for tolerance or stability.”

That may prove true in a selected domain. The chapter must define the additional work the term performs—especially recovery across changing tasks, scales, and histories—and accept replacement by established constructs if it adds no measurable distinction.

### “A demand–capacity model will blame the person for lacking capacity.”

Capacity is relational and environment-dependent. Task design, resources, assistance, work organization, recovery opportunity, illness, and social conditions can change the relation. The chapter should never reduce a mismatch to an individual deficit.

### “Dose language makes ordinary living sound toxic.”

Dose is descriptive. Ordinary and repeated exposures can be neutral, useful, training, fatiguing, or consequential. The outcome fork and negative comparisons prevent repetition from becoming harm by definition.

### “The model is unfalsifiable because capacity can always be invoked after the fact.”

Every use must state prospective or independently observable capacity indicators, task, outcome, and time window. An unspecified reserve invoked only after symptoms is not an acceptable explanation.
