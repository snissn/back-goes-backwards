## Chapter job

This chapter owns two connected but nonidentical families in the Book I taxonomy: global or regional geometric redistribution and local stress or strain concentration. Its task is to make the transition between scales mechanically explicit. A whole-body or segmental change can alter moments, contacts, and the demand arriving at a region; local geometry, curvature, congruence, material contrast, and boundary conditions can then focus that demand at a smaller interface. Neither step is visible in full from posture alone.

The chapter should be the most conventionally mechanical chapter in Part III. Free-body reasoning, contact distribution, stiffness boundaries, and a controlled local-field representation must replace inherited equations or rhetoric that leap from alignment to decompression, degeneration, or symptoms. At the same time, the project-native concern survives intact: modest global change can sometimes produce a disproportionately important local exposure. What remains open is where, under which task and boundary conditions, by what measure, and with what biological consequence.

**This chapter must:**

- distinguish global redistribution from local concentration;
- show how geometry, line of action, curvature, moment arm, contact, and material boundaries enter the scale bridge;
- demonstrate that the same resultant can produce different local distributions;
- preserve the hypothesis that a small global change can produce a larger local deformation under stated conditions;
- include ordinary variation, successful compensation, and asymptomatic concentration as comparison branches; and
- hand Chapter 13 a clear distinction between concentrated exposure and deformation maintained by an ongoing constraint.

**This chapter must not:**

- infer local stress or strain from a photograph, posture label, or curve alone;
- treat one spinal curve, joint position, asymmetry, or contact pattern as universally harmful;
- equate modeled concentration with damage, wear, symptoms, or diagnosis;
- revive dimensionally inconsistent equations or use rotation matrices as evidence of decompression; or
- convert global geometry into the signed preferred-configuration model reserved for Book II.

## Reader entry and exit

**The reader enters** able to distinguish external demand from local tissue exposure and able to describe a route, but may still assume that a visible displacement or misalignment reveals where stress went.

**The reader exits** able to trace a proposed chain from body or segment geometry to regional forces and moments, from those resultants to contact or boundary conditions, and from those conditions to a local stress or strain field—while identifying every modeling step, alternative, and missing measurement.

## Central question

How can a global or regional change in geometry alter what reaches a tissue and concentrate local stress or strain without making visible shape itself equivalent to adverse exposure?

## Working answer

Configuration and geometry enter mechanics by changing contact, line of action, moment arm, curvature, support, and boundary conditions. Those changes can redistribute a defined demand at a regional scale; local surface shape, area, congruence, material contrast, thickness, and constraint can then focus stress or strain. The extent and consequence of that concentration must be calculated or measured for a stated task, dose, and tissue. Geometry is an input, concentration an exposure description, and biological effect a later question.

## Narrative arc

The chapter opens with two joint configurations carrying the same external demand but distributing contact differently. It first reconstructs the chain from external task to regional forces and moments. It then treats moment arm and curvature, followed by contact area and congruence, before moving into stiffness contrasts and local fields. Only after those mechanisms are established does it address the project's small-global-change/large-local-effect intuition. The final section introduces dose, capacity, and asymptomatic comparison to prevent concentration from becoming injury by definition, then hands a distinct maintained-deformation problem to Chapter 13.

## Argument sequence

### Opening — the same resultant, a different local field

Use `BGB-X-0002` as an idealized hip, knee, or other joint example. Keep the external resultant comparable while changing a bounded geometric term such as contact location, moment arm, or congruence. Show that total force alone cannot specify the contact-pressure pattern or tissue strain. The example is a mechanical construction, not a clinical finding or claim about an optimal joint position.

The opening should give the reader an inspectable surprise: a small change in a modeled relationship can meaningfully alter a local distribution, but the direction and magnitude depend on geometry, constraints, active forces, and material behavior.

### 12.1 Geometry converts external demand into regional exposure

**Job:** Reconstruct the missing bridge from task and visible configuration to internal resultants.

Begin with the selected system boundary and free body. Identify external contacts, gravity, applied force, acceleration, support, and the point or axis for moments. Then state which muscle forces, joint reactions, or pressure-mediated terms are unknown and must be measured or modeled. A change in configuration may alter all of these without changing object weight.

Use nested scales rather than one arrow: task → segment or regional resultant → interface distribution → local tissue field. The geometry at one level is information for the next model, not a conclusion about it.

**Exit:** Once regional forces and moments are defined, moment arm and curvature can be considered without treating them as visual labels.

### 12.2 Moment arms and curvature redistribute demand

**Job:** Show two ordinary mechanisms by which geometry changes a regional load problem.

Demonstrate how a changed distance between a force's line of action and a chosen axis changes the balancing moment and may alter required internal forces. Then show how curvature and segment orientation can redirect compression, tension, bending, and shear in a bounded beam-, column-, or joint-like model. State where those simplified models cease to resemble active, articulated, living anatomy.

Spinal examples may appear because they are central to the project, but they must be comparative and task-specific. No equation should combine angular velocities or rotations merely to produce extension or decompression. Rotation matrices describe transformations; they do not establish a physiological benefit.

**Exit:** Regional resultants still do not reveal how an interface receives them; contact distribution must be modeled separately.

### 12.3 Contact area and congruence change local pressure

**Job:** Replace generic “joint stress” and “misalignment” with a spatial contact problem.

Explain that the same resultant can yield different contact patterns when area, surface shape, orientation, material state, active support, or congruence changes. Edge loading and focal contact are candidate descriptions, not diagnoses. Average force divided by nominal area is not automatically the true local pressure field.

Use a matched comparison and label what is calculated, modeled, and unmeasured. Include a case in which a changed position increases nominal contact area yet raises stress elsewhere, preventing a simple “more area is always safer” rule.

**Exit:** Even away from an articulating surface, material and mobility transitions can focus exposure.

### 12.4 Stiffness contrasts and boundaries can localize strain

**Job:** Extend localization beyond joint contact to tissue and device interfaces.

Introduce a simplified interface between regions differing in stiffness, thickness, mobility, attachment, or constraint. Under some boundary conditions, stress or strain becomes nonuniform near the transition. Candidate anatomical contexts include entheses, joint margins, scar or adhesion boundaries, and tissues adjacent to a rigid device or constrained segment, but each requires its own material evidence.

Avoid declaring every stiffness transition pathological. Gradients and attachments are normal design features. The chapter asks when a particular transition and dose produce a materially different local field.

**Exit:** With these mechanisms established, the small-global-change/large-local-deformation proposal can be stated precisely rather than metaphorically.

### 12.5 Small global change can become large local deformation

**Job:** Preserve and formalize the project's amplification intuition.

Construct a two-scale model in which a modest axial shortening, translation, rotation, or curvature change is distributed across a larger system while one region absorbs a disproportionate share through bending, shear, compression, tension, or altered contact. Define both the global input and local output, including units or normalization. State the boundary conditions under which amplification occurs and the conditions under which demand instead redistributes harmlessly across many regions.

The chapter should explicitly permit no amplification, a different localization site, or successful active redistribution. Appearance alone cannot select among these branches.

**Exit:** A localized field may be mechanically real and still remain biologically tolerated or clinically irrelevant.

### 12.6 Concentration is not equivalent to damage

**Job:** Reconnect local exposure to dose, capacity, and alternatives without performing Chapter 19's biology.

Distinguish a measured or modeled concentration from fatigue, material change, pain, structural wear, collapse, or diagnosed disease. Duration, repetition, strain rate, tissue state, recovery, adaptation, and competing routes determine whether the exposure matters. Include asymptomatic geometry and successful compensation as required comparison cases.

Name reverse causality: tissue degeneration, inflammation, pain, injury, or developmental anatomy can change stiffness and geometry, thereby creating the concentration observed later. A present local field does not identify what came first.

**Exit:** The next family is not simply another concentration. Chapter 13 asks what maintains deformation or prevents required excursion in a form-receiving structure.

### Conclusion and handoff

Reconstruct the opening example in four levels: external demand, regional resultant and moment, interface distribution, and local tissue field. State what was observed, calculated, modeled, and left unknown. The chapter has shown how geometry can matter substantially without turning geometry into destiny.

Chapter 13 receives a clean boundary. Chapter 12 describes how demand becomes nonuniform or locally focused. Chapter 13 describes an ongoing relation that holds a compliant structure deformed or prevents it from moving through a required excursion. A case can contain both, but the mechanisms and measurements differ.

## Model obligations

### Existing scale and free-body models

1. **Object represented:** External task, segment resultants, interface distribution, and local exposure.
2. **System boundary and scale:** Nested person, segment, joint/interface, and tissue systems.
3. **Variables and states:** Forces, moments, contact traction or pressure, stress, strain, and deformation with units and evidence state.
4. **Assumptions:** Quasi-static or dynamic state, active-force estimates, frame, material behavior, and contact conditions.
5. **What the model explains:** Why geometry is an input to, not a substitute for, local exposure.
6. **What it cannot establish:** Biological response, pain, diagnosis, preferred posture, or unique internal distribution.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Revise when assumptions or validation fail for the selected anatomy and task.
8. **Registry action:** Reuse `BGB-M-0001` and `BGB-M-0002`.

### Two-scale redistribution-to-localization model

1. **Object represented:** Mapping from global or regional geometry and resultants to a local contact or tissue field.
2. **System boundary and scale:** One bounded joint, attachment, segment, or interface embedded within a larger task.
3. **Variables and states:** Configuration, force, moment, curvature, contact area, traction, pressure, stress, strain, and material contrast.
4. **Assumptions:** Geometry, contact law, active control, material properties, boundary constraints, and timescale.
5. **What the model explains:** Conditions under which redistribution produces localization or amplification.
6. **What it cannot establish:** Harm, symptom origin, whole-body optimality, or generalization across tissues.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Replace when model predictions do not match measured distribution or when omitted active/material terms dominate.
8. **Registry action:** to create.

### Stiffness-boundary comparison

1. **Object represented:** Local field near a transition in stiffness, thickness, mobility, or constraint.
2. **System boundary and scale:** Idealized material interface followed by one anatomically reviewed example.
3. **Variables and states:** Applied demand, geometry, constitutive relation, strain or stress gradient, and time.
4. **Assumptions:** Simplified materials and boundary conditions are pedagogical unless validated anatomically.
5. **What the model explains:** Why local fields may differ from the regional average.
6. **What it cannot establish:** That ordinary interfaces are defects or that localization predicts symptoms.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Narrow if living adaptation or nonlinear material behavior changes the predicted field materially.
8. **Registry action:** to create.

## Principal objections and responses

### “Posture does not reliably predict pain.”

The chapter does not claim that it does. It asks a narrower mechanical question: under a defined task and model, does geometry alter regional and local exposure? Clinical meaning requires additional tissue, time, and whole-person evidence.

### “A simplified free body cannot represent a living body.”

It represents a selected boundary and question. Active control, pressure, contact, acceleration, and material behavior are added as the scale requires. The model is rejected or narrowed when omitted terms dominate.

### “Stress concentration is normal in anatomy.”

Yes. Attachments, gradients, curves, and contacts routinely create nonuniform fields. The chapter distinguishes existence of a concentration from dose beyond capacity and biological consequence.

### “Small global change causing large local strain sounds like a universal amplification claim.”

It is explicitly conditional. The model must define both quantities, boundary conditions, and null cases. In many tasks the system may distribute the change successfully or localize it elsewhere.
