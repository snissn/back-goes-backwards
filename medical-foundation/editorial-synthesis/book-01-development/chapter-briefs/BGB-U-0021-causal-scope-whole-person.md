## Chapter job

This chapter decides how much explanatory work mechanics is allowed to perform after the book has made a mechanical narrative coherent. It is the causal-scope chapter, not an apology for including biology, psychology, or social context and not a taxonomy that forces every presentation into a fixed mechanical role. Mechanics may organize a problem, contribute to one link, modify a symptom or task, follow from another process, interact reciprocally with it, or add little to the question at hand. Those are reasoning possibilities whose relevance must be argued case by case, not mandatory bins every clinician or reader must assign.

The chapter also makes the “whole person” operational. Biological state, cognition and expectation, emotion, behavior, social conditions, work, resources, environment, care history, and meaning must enter named nodes or arrows in the same causal account rather than being collected in a closing paragraph. Mechanical and nonmechanical explanations can compete, coexist, alternate in priority, or become inseparable over time. The valid endpoint is an evidence-proportionate account—including a conclusion that mechanics should receive low or no priority.

**This chapter must:**

- ask how much explanatory work, if any, mechanics should do for a declared question;
- distinguish mechanical plausibility from causal priority and clinical importance;
- preserve reverse causality, reciprocal causality, common causes, and alternative pathways;
- place whole-person factors at specific chain nodes and transitions;
- compare evidence strength without converting it into false precision; and
- retain a credible low-mechanical-priority or null interpretation.

**This chapter must not:**

- require every case to be labeled primary, contributor, modifier, consequence, or incidental context;
- imply that a more complete mechanical story is necessarily a better clinical explanation;
- use response to movement, unloading, support, or treatment as proof of original cause;
- reduce biological, psychological, or social factors to confounders around a mechanical center;
- present osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis as clean opposites or as a disease-causality proof;
- give diagnostic, triage, referral, contraindication, or treatment rules; or
- settle later preferred-configuration, TCM, breath, or practice claims.

## Reader entry and exit

**The reader enters** able to build a mechanically and biologically coherent chain but may still mistake coherence for causal priority or assume that the book's lens must find a meaningful mechanical contribution.

**The reader exits** able to compare several causal structures for the same observation, locate whole-person influences precisely, identify forward, reverse, reciprocal, and common-cause possibilities, calibrate conclusions to evidence, and state when mechanics adds little.

## Central question

Given a mechanically intelligible account, how much explanatory or decision-relevant work—if any—should mechanics be asked to do within the person's larger biological, psychological, social, environmental, and temporal context?

## Working answer

Mechanical explanation should be scoped to the exact question and to the weakest consequential link in its causal chain. In some contexts mechanics may organize exposure and tissue response strongly; in others it may influence function or symptoms without identifying origin; in still others it may mainly reflect an immune, inflammatory, infectious, genetic, developmental, metabolic, traumatic, behavioral, or social process, or may add little beyond existing care. These are open alternatives, not required classifications. The whole-person account becomes more rigorous when each factor enters the node or arrow it can plausibly affect and when reverse, reciprocal, and null structures remain visible.

## Narrative arc

The chapter opens with one observation and several causal structures that fit it. It first narrows causal scope to a declared question, population, timeframe, and outcome. It then surveys the different ways mechanics might enter without turning those possibilities into a checklist. A carefully bounded osteoarthritis/rheumatoid-arthritis comparison tests whether the framework can distinguish mechanical relevance from mechanical primacy. Clear nonmechanical primaries, reverse causality, and reciprocal loops then prevent a mechanical default. Whole-person factors are attached to specific nodes and arrows. The chapter closes with an evidence-strength ladder and a legitimate conclusion that mechanics may remain plausible yet low priority.

## Argument sequence

### Opening — one observation, several causal structures

Use a neutral observation such as pain or reduced task tolerance accompanied by an altered movement pattern and a structural finding. Draw at least four compatible structures: mechanical exposure preceding response; a biological process changing mechanics and symptoms; behavior or context changing both task and symptom; and a mixed reciprocal loop. Add a fifth structure in which the mechanical observation is incidental to the declared outcome.

No structure should be presented as the hidden correct answer. Label observations and measurements, then mark every causal arrow `INF` or `HYP` unless direct causal evidence supports a stronger state. The opening's job is to expose underdetermination, not to make clinical uncertainty feel limitless.

### 21.1 Scope comes before causal ranking

**Job:** Define the proposition before asking whether mechanics is important.

Require a declared question: origin, current symptom modulation, tissue exposure, recurrence, function, task tolerance, recovery, or treatment response. Specify the system boundary, timescale, population, comparator, and outcome. Mechanics may be central to one question and peripheral to another in the same person.

Use the canonical chain to separate propositions. Evidence that configuration changes local exposure does not automatically establish exposure as the origin of a tissue state; evidence that support changes symptoms does not establish the historical route; a diagnosis can be valid even when a mechanical history remains unknown.

**Exit:** Once the question is bounded, several possible mechanical roles can be considered without forcing a label.

### 21.2 Ways mechanics may enter an account

**Job:** Offer flexible reasoning possibilities rather than a compulsory classification system.

Show that mechanics may organize a task and distribution, participate in initiation, accumulate as one contributor, modify an already present tissue or symptom state, mediate an environmental demand, follow from disease or injury, interact reciprocally, or add no useful explanation. These possibilities can overlap and change through time. They should be phrased as questions—“Does the mechanical relation help explain this transition?”—rather than as boxes a reader must complete.

Avoid the overstrong rule that mechanical contribution must always be classified by degree and role. When a simple bounded statement is sufficient, use it. When the evidence cannot distinguish among possibilities, say so rather than assigning a polished category.

**Exit:** A disease-boundary exercise can now test whether the framework preserves mechanical relevance without converting it into primary cause.

### 21.3 Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis as a boundary exercise

**Job:** Use a familiar contrast to examine causal scope, not to teach or simplify either disease.

Treat osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis as heterogeneous disease families with interacting biology and mechanics. The exercise asks how local exposure, joint geometry, activity, inflammation, immune processes, pain, tissue state, and behavior might occupy different positions or weights in a chain. It must not imply that osteoarthritis is “mechanical” while rheumatoid arthritis is “nonmechanical,” nor that mechanical management follows from the comparison.

Use only publication-supported and medically reviewed statements. If the contrast proves too reductive after evidence review, replace it with a more bounded pair or retain it only as an objection box. Its job is to demonstrate that mechanical relevance and causal primacy are different questions.

**Exit:** The framework must also handle contexts in which a nonmechanical primary is clearer and mechanics is downstream or background.

### 21.4 Nonmechanical primaries and low-priority mechanics

**Job:** Establish that the lens remains disciplined when it does not lead.

Use clear classes—infectious, immune-mediated, genetic, malignant, metabolic, acute toxic, or other medically appropriate contexts—only at a general level unless condition-specific review is available. Show where mechanics may still affect task, comfort, exposure, or recovery while remaining secondary to diagnosis and established care. Include a case in which adding a routing story changes no interpretation or decision.

The chapter should state that mechanical background is not the same as clinically meaningful mechanical contribution. A body is always mechanical, but that fact alone does not make biomechanics the priority for every medical question.

**Exit:** Even where mechanics appears early in a narrative, reverse causality and common causes must still be tested.

### 21.5 Reverse causality, reciprocal loops, and common causes

**Job:** Make time order and alternate graph structure visible.

Use examples from Chapter 19: inflammation, degeneration, injury, fatigue, fear, pain, or altered sensation may change active control, posture, task selection, stiffness, and distribution. A social or occupational condition may change both exposure and recovery; medication or care may change symptoms and movement simultaneously. A reciprocal loop can develop after either entry point.

For every proposed forward arrow, write at least one reverse or common-cause candidate and one observation that could discriminate them. A cross-sectional posture, image, or symptom cannot establish order. Longitudinal evidence can strengthen temporal direction but still may not identify the sole cause.

**Exit:** Causal graphs now have places where the rest of the person's context can enter precisely.

### 21.6 The whole person enters at named nodes and arrows

**Job:** Integrate biological, psychological, social, environmental, and care contexts into the same model.

Build a node-entry matrix. Biological state can change material properties, inflammatory response, healing, sensation, or energy; cognition, expectation, emotion, and learning can change task selection, protective behavior, salience, and symptom interpretation; social and economic conditions can shape work demand, rest, nutrition, access, safety, and recovery; cultural meaning and prior care can change goals and behavior; environment and equipment can change the external task and available paths.

Do not imply that each domain always acts, or that psychological and social factors are merely reasons a mechanical treatment fails. Each factor needs a proposed node, arrow, evidence state, alternative, and relevant outcome. Patient goals and values matter to decision relevance even when they do not explain biological origin.

**Exit:** With competing structures and node-specific context visible, the conclusion can be calibrated to the actual strength of evidence.

### 21.7 Evidence strength and stopping rules

**Job:** Match conclusion strength to the chain rather than to rhetorical coherence.

Create an evidence ladder that distinguishes observation, measurement, calculation, validated model, temporal association, intervention-based modulation, mechanistic evidence, condition-specific causal inference, and clinical outcome evidence. It should not pretend that one ordering applies to every question. Instead, show what each form can and cannot establish.

The stopping rule is the weakest consequential arrow. A well-supported local mechanical relation may justify measurement or further research while remaining insufficient for causal priority, diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment. Valid conclusions include: supported for a narrow link; plausible but underdetermined; one influence among several; downstream consequence; poor fit; no added explanatory value; or unresolved. These are examples of prose conclusions, not required labels.

**Exit:** Chapter 22 can now place the lens into workflow without turning it into a parallel diagnostic system.

### Conclusion and handoff

Return to the opening observation and show that causal discipline does not demand choosing one totalizing story. It demands specifying the question, separating arrows, placing whole-person influences where they act, comparing alternatives, and stopping at the evidence. Mechanics can remain important, modest, consequential, reciprocal, or low priority without any of those outcomes threatening the larger project.

Chapter 22 inherits a practical reasoning rule: add mechanical history and routing only where they clarify a named question, preserve established diagnosis and safety processes, record evidence states and alternatives, and allow the workflow to conclude that no further mechanical account is needed.

## Model obligations

### Same observation, multiple causal structures

1. **Object represented:** Several directed graphs compatible with one set of observations.
2. **System boundary and scale:** Person–environment, regional, tissue, symptom, and care levels, with boundaries explicit.
3. **Variables and states:** Canonical chain nodes plus whole-person factors, each labeled by evidence state; causal arrows remain `INF` or `HYP` unless supported.
4. **Assumptions:** Temporal order, missing variables, measurement validity, selection, treatment, and common causes.
5. **What the model explains:** Why coherence or association does not identify causal priority.
6. **What it cannot establish:** Which graph is true, one complete cause, diagnosis, or treatment.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Revise when candidate graphs omit known dominant pathways or cannot yield discriminating observations.
8. **Registry action:** to create.

### Whole-person node-entry matrix

1. **Object represented:** Where contextual factors can alter tasks, paths, exposure, response, function, symptoms, interpretation, goals, or outcomes.
2. **System boundary and scale:** Person in biological, social, occupational, environmental, cultural, and care contexts.
3. **Variables and states:** Factor, affected node/arrow, proposed direction, evidence state, timeframe, alternative, and outcome relevance.
4. **Assumptions:** Domains overlap; factors are not universally active; patient values are not reducible to causal variables.
5. **What the model explains:** How whole-person reasoning can be specific rather than boilerplate.
6. **What it cannot establish:** A complete biopsychosocial model, causal weight, or clinical intervention.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Remove entries that cannot be connected to a defined proposition or relevant outcome.
8. **Registry action:** to create.

### Evidence-strength and stopping model

1. **Object represented:** Relation between evidence form, causal proposition, permitted conclusion, and stopping point.
2. **System boundary and scale:** One complete causal chain with evidence assessed arrow by arrow.
3. **Variables and states:** Observation, measurement, calculation, model, temporal evidence, mechanism, modulation, causal inference, outcome, and uncertainty.
4. **Assumptions:** No universal evidence hierarchy answers every question; quality and directness matter within each class.
5. **What the model explains:** Why conclusion strength is limited by the weakest consequential link.
6. **What it cannot establish:** A numeric certainty score or automatic clinical decision.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Revise if the ladder obscures domain-specific quality or encourages score-like use.
8. **Registry action:** to create.

## Principal objections and responses

### “If mechanics can occupy any role, the framework is unfalsifiable.”

The chapter does not preserve mechanics by changing its role after the fact. It requires a declared question, candidate graph, discriminating observations, alternatives, and a low/no-priority outcome before interpretation. A specific mechanical proposition can therefore be weakened or rejected even though the body remains physical.

### “Whole-person framing will dilute the book's mechanical thesis.”

Node-specific context makes the mechanical thesis more precise. It shows which exposures, responses, behaviors, and outcomes mechanics can help explain and prevents the framework from carrying claims that belong to immune, social, psychological, or other processes.

### “Osteoarthritis versus rheumatoid arthritis is too simplistic.”

That objection is valid if the comparison is presented as a dichotomy. It survives only as a bounded exercise showing that mechanical relevance and causal primacy can differ within and across heterogeneous diseases. Evidence review may replace or demote it.

### “A response to mechanical change proves that the cause was mechanical.”

It proves only the measured modulation under those conditions. Etiology, historical routing, diagnosis, and long-term outcome require separate evidence.
