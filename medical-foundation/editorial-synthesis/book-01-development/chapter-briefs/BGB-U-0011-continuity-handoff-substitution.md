## Chapter job

This chapter supplies the relational vocabulary needed to describe how a task is carried across changing skeletal, active, tensile, form-receiving, sliding, and pressure-bearing roles. It is the grammar chapter of Part III: Chapters 12–15 cannot distinguish concentration, constraint, instability, discoordination, and compensation unless *continuity*, *handoff*, *interruption*, *bypass*, *overload*, *leakage*, *substitution*, and *rerouting* have bounded meanings.

The chapter preserves an important project-native intuition: gross structural demand is generally better resolved through mechanically capable, continuous, relatively shape-preserving and force-transmitting relations so that form-receiving structures retain the freedom needed to yield, conform, glide, contain, expand, and recover. The hard/soft distinction is **motivated by TCM hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts but is not definitionally identical to them**. Book I must translate the intuition into candidate quantities and observations rather than use traditional correspondence as proof. “Leakage” and “misrouting” therefore remain hypotheses about altered exposure until the task, carrier, quantity, scale, and substitute path are named.

**This chapter must:**

- define continuity without inventing one literal cable or one universal anatomical route;
- describe handoff as a change in mechanical role, carrier, or phase;
- translate leakage and misrouting into measurable candidate exposures;
- distinguish interruption, bypass, overload, substitution, and rerouting;
- show how successful task completion can coexist with a changed route or cost; and
- require every routing narrative to name alternatives and possible disfavoring observations.

**This chapter must not:**

- teach the preferred all-Yang route, Six-Division mapping, or TCM channel model;
- classify tissues permanently as hard or soft, or posterior anatomy as good and ventral anatomy as bad;
- imply that deformation, active soft-tissue loading, pressure, or substitution is inherently adverse;
- infer a hidden route solely from posture, palpation, embodied sensation, or task success; or
- convert a routing hypothesis into diagnosis, pathology, or corrective instruction.

## Reader entry and exit

**The reader enters** knowing that load paths are explanatory representations and that tissues can change mechanical roles, but may still imagine a path as a single line or use *leakage*, *compensation*, and *misalignment* as self-explanatory metaphors.

**The reader exits** able to specify the task, system boundary, candidate carriers, available handoffs, observed change, proposed substitute, local exposure, phase, and plausible alternatives in a routing account—and able to reject an account that cannot identify those elements.

## Central question

What exactly changes when demand no longer follows an available structural relation, and how can interruption, bypass, overload, leakage, substitution, and rerouting be distinguished without pretending that one hidden route has already been measured?

## Working answer

Mechanical continuity is the task-specific availability of successive structures, contacts, controls, and material roles needed to receive, transform, transfer, and resolve a defined demand. A handoff changes carrier, role, direction, or phase. Interruption, bypass, overload, leakage, substitution, and rerouting name different proposed changes in that organization. They become mechanical claims only when the relevant quantity, route, alternate path, exposure, and observation are stated; otherwise they remain project-native relational hypotheses.

## Narrative arc

The chapter opens with an ordinary task whose success conceals several simultaneous and sequential handoffs. It first defines continuity as availability rather than anatomical sameness. It then applies the changing hard/soft division of labor to handoff and translates the project's leakage intuition into candidate mechanical quantities. Next it separates interruption, bypass, and overload before showing how substitution preserves function while altering later demand. It closes with a falsifiability requirement: a routing account must identify alternative paths and allow the possibility that no meaningful redistribution occurred.

## Argument sequence

### Opening — an ordinary task succeeds through several routes

Use `BGB-X-0001` as a phase-by-phase hypothetical observation: lift an object from one support, carry it, turn, and place it on another. At different moments, ground contact, skeletal geometry, muscle force, tendon transfer, joint contact, pressure-mediated support, contralateral balance, and object contact change their roles. The task succeeds, but success alone does not reveal which relations carried which quantities.

The opening should include one variation—an added hand support, changed object position, or altered foot contact—that preserves the endpoint while visibly changing available paths. It must not imply that the original or modified strategy is superior.

### 11.1 Continuity means that successive roles remain mechanically available

**Job:** Replace the cable image of continuity with a task-specific availability relation.

A route is continuous when the selected system can receive, transform, transfer, and resolve a defined demand through the required phase without an avoidable bottleneck or obligatory substitute. Several routes may share the demand. The dominant carrier can change over time, and a structure can participate in several roles at once.

Continuity must be defined at a useful scale. Whole-body visual smoothness does not establish tissue-level continuity, and discontinuous-looking movement does not prove an adverse internal handoff. The route representation must state what enters, what leaves, and where support or opposition ultimately occurs.

**Exit:** A route can remain continuous while its carriers and roles change, so those changes need the more specific concept of handoff.

### 11.2 Handoff changes how demand is being managed

**Job:** Describe transfer across changing mechanical roles without permanent tissue classification.

Follow candidate transitions among active gathering, tensile transfer, skeletal contact, ligamentous guidance, form-receiving accommodation, sliding excursion, and pressure response. A handoff may redirect force, change moment, distribute contact, store or return energy, or temporarily stiffen an assembly. None of these roles belongs exclusively to one tissue class.

State the project provenance once, accurately: the hard/soft division is motivated by TCM hard/soft and Yin/Yang concepts, while Book I uses it as an explicit mechanical polarity open to formal and empirical revision. A successful handoff does not eliminate deformation or soft-tissue loading; it preserves the changing freedom each participant needs for the task.

**Exit:** Once roles are explicit, “leakage” can be translated from a metaphor into candidate exposures.

### 11.3 Leakage and misrouting are hypotheses about unwanted exposure

**Job:** Preserve the authorial intuition while making it testable.

Translate “load leaks into a yielding structure” into possible observations: greater or sustained deformation, increased active tension, changed pressure, restricted glide, altered contact, changed moment, or demand received by a structure with less capacity for that direction and dose. Every formulation must identify why the exposure is considered poorly matched—by material behavior, required freedom, task, recovery, or measured consequence—not simply because the structure is soft, anterior, lateral, or visibly displaced.

Include a null case in which a form-receiving structure deforms or bears substantial load normally and returns without loss of function. Include a second case in which a shape-preserving role is performed by an active or pressure-mediated assembly rather than bone alone.

**Exit:** A changed exposure is not enough to tell whether the original route was absent, avoided, or merely overloaded.

### 11.4 Interruption, bypass, and overload are different events

**Job:** Prevent three distinct route states from being narrated as one generic failure.

An interrupted route is unavailable for a task or phase. A bypassed route may remain available but unused. An overloaded route remains engaged but receives a dose exceeding relevant present capacity. Geometry, pain, contact, control, stiffness, fatigue, task choice, fear, environmental support, or constraint may contribute, but none can be assumed from the label.

Use a three-column comparison with matched task and endpoint. Ask what observation would distinguish each state: contact or force distribution, motion, timing, activation, pressure, excursion, task modulation, or recovery. Permit mixed states and uncertainty.

**Exit:** When the endpoint is preserved despite altered availability, another relation must be considered—but not presumed—to be substituting.

### 11.5 Substitution can preserve the task while changing its cost

**Job:** Show why successful performance does not prove unchanged routing.

Another muscle, joint, fascial region, pressure strategy, limb, support, or neighboring segment may assume a missing or reduced role. The substitute may be efficient, neutral, temporarily costly, more deformable, better suited, or vulnerable only under a particular dose. A person can also choose a new route that improves the task.

Keep immediate success separate from energetic cost, local exposure, reserve, and delayed consequence. Chapter 15 owns the temporal pathway. Here the job is only to show that endpoint equivalence can coexist with different internal organizations.

**Exit:** A substitution story is credible only if it identifies both the proposed original and alternative routes.

### 11.6 A routing account must identify alternative paths

**Job:** Turn the vocabulary into an inspectable method rather than a rhetorical system.

Require every account to record: task and phase; system boundary; incoming demand; candidate carriers and contacts; proposed available route; observed change; proposed substitute; local mechanical exposure; dose and capacity; and plausible alternatives. Evidence-state labels must distinguish measurement, calculation, model, inference, hypothesis, and embodied observation.

Name disfavoring results: no meaningful redistribution under controlled variation; the proposed route is not anatomically or mechanically available; the substitute does not change when the presumed interruption is removed; or another explanation better predicts the measured exposure. Multiple routes and unresolved distributions remain valid outcomes.

**Exit:** The chapter has now created the grammar needed to examine how a redistributed demand becomes concentrated locally in Chapter 12.

### Conclusion and handoff

Revisit the object transfer and describe two successful versions with different candidate handoffs. The chapter does not decide which is globally preferred. It establishes that route language becomes informative only when it names the system, demand, carriers, phase, substitute, and exposure.

Chapter 12 inherits one precise problem: even when the whole-body demand is modest and the task succeeds, geometry and material boundaries can redistribute that demand and concentrate it at a smaller interface. That phenomenon must be modeled rather than inferred from the word *misrouting*.

### `BGB-M-0004` — Load path and force-line family

1. **Object represented:** Distribution and transfer of a defined demand, plus controlled force-line senses.
2. **System boundary and scale:** Task, phase, contacts, and selected internal scale explicitly bounded.
3. **Variables and states:** External force or moment, inferred carriers, contact, activation, pressure, deformation, and embodied observation, each labeled by status.
4. **Assumptions:** Candidate carriers, available anatomy, task phase, and the representation's resolution.
5. **What the model explains:** Why a route can branch, change, or remain partly unknown.
6. **What it cannot establish:** A unique hidden path, preferred configuration, diagnosis, or agreement between felt and measured force lines.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Revise if a network, field, probabilistic, or control representation better fits the question.
8. **Registry action:** Reuse `BGB-M-0004`.

### `BGB-M-0005` — Hard/soft division of mechanical labor

1. **Object represented:** Relative shape-preserving and form-receiving roles in a stated direction and task.
2. **System boundary and scale:** Interaction or assembly, not permanent tissue class.
3. **Variables and states:** Shape change, force transfer, contact, excursion, active stiffness, pressure, and task phase.
4. **Assumptions:** Relevant direction, timescale, activation, pressure, and material state.
5. **What the model explains:** Why preserving one structure's freedom may require demand to be resolved elsewhere.
6. **What it cannot establish:** Exact identity with TCM terms, zero soft-tissue load, posterior superiority, or one ideal route.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Narrow or replace if roles cannot be operationalized or add no value beyond established terms.
8. **Registry action:** Reuse `BGB-M-0005`; state that its provenance is motivated by TCM rather than definitionally identical.

### Transfer-state graph and route-availability matrix

1. **Object represented:** Available, interrupted, bypassed, overloaded, substituted, and uncertain route states.
2. **System boundary and scale:** One defined task and phase, with named contacts and candidate carriers.
3. **Variables and states:** Route availability, distribution, local exposure, endpoint success, and time.
4. **Assumptions:** States are analytical distinctions, not necessarily exclusive biological categories.
5. **What the model explains:** Why similar endpoints can arise from different candidate organizations.
6. **What it cannot establish:** Which state occurred without measurement or whether one route is preferable.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Revise if states cannot be distinguished or distributed systems require continuous rather than categorical description.

## Principal objections and responses

### “Load does not literally choose or want a route.”

Agreed. “Wants” is a project-native heuristic about relative stiffness, geometry, contacts, active control, and available paths. Technical prose must identify those terms. The phrase can orient the model but cannot replace it.

### “Continuity and leakage are unfalsifiable metaphors.”

They would be if left unqualified. The chapter requires a task, boundary, carrier, quantity, proposed alternate route, and disfavoring result. An account that cannot supply them remains an authorial intuition rather than a mechanical claim.

### “Soft tissues routinely bear and transmit large loads.”

Yes. The hard/soft model concerns relative roles, not tissue exclusion. Tendon, ligament, fascia, muscle, cartilage, and pressurized assemblies can perform shape-preserving or force-transmitting roles; form-receiving structures also carry load normally.

### “Calling a route ventral or lateral makes anatomy into a moral map.”

Ventral, dorsal, medial, and lateral are spatial descriptions. Suitability depends on task, dose, capacity, and required freedom. Book I neither declares ventral loading harmful nor derives posterior preference.
