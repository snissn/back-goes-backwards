## Chapter job

This chapter defines the specific object Book I proposes to add to established medical and biomechanical reasoning: a longitudinal account of how a defined demand entered a person–environment system, was partitioned among available paths, changed through compensation or state transition, reached a local tissue or interface, and altered the conditions of later function or recovery. Chapter 17 established that medicine already reasons mechanically and often manages local mechanical endpoints. Chapter 18 must now distinguish the proposed history from those endpoints without claiming that the history is unique, routinely recoverable, or already clinically useful.

The chapter should make the proposal ambitious and falsifiable at the same time. Longitudinal routing may prove useful for exposure research, recurrence, functional reserve, device or surgical context, prevention research, recovery, or communication across disciplines. It may also add little in a given presentation, duplicate mature biomechanics or rehabilitation constructs, or be too underdetermined to reconstruct. The null and overlap branches are part of the chapter's thesis, not concessions added afterward.

**This chapter must:**

- define longitudinal routing as a relationship among dated observations, measurements, models, compensations, and local exposures;
- distinguish a current endpoint from the several histories compatible with it;
- carry forward the six-family adverse-organization taxonomy as pattern language rather than diagnosis;
- show how compensation and cumulative demand can precede symptoms or a discrete injury without making that sequence inevitable;
- state the intended medical and research applications as hypotheses requiring incremental-value evidence;
- locate the object among biomechanics, rehabilitation, medicine, occupational history, development, and social/environmental context; and
- identify observations that would show that routing history adds little or cannot be recovered.

**This chapter must not:**

- claim novelty by ignoring existing biomechanics, physical therapy, ergonomics, occupational medicine, or exposure science;
- treat a reconstructed history as longitudinally observed;
- turn the Part III taxonomy into diagnostic categories or a screening system;
- infer upstream cause from a present lesion, symptom, posture, or response to unloading;
- reuse the recurring object-transfer task, whose principal narrative work ended in Part II; or
- derive a protocol, corrective route, or preferred configuration.

## Reader entry and exit

**The reader enters** accepting that local mechanical endpoints can be real and medically consequential while still asking what a body-wide history would add beyond existing care and rehabilitation.

**The reader exits** able to define a longitudinal routing hypothesis, label its observed and reconstructed components, compare several compatible histories, apply the six-family taxonomy without diagnosing, and name evidence that would support, narrow, duplicate, or disfavor the proposed added object.

## Central question

What additional explanatory or research object is created by following mechanical demand across structures and time, and what would show that the object adds nothing beyond existing local, biomechanical, or rehabilitation accounts?

## Working answer

Longitudinal load routing is a structured reconstruction of task and environment, configuration and available paths, demand and internal distribution, local exposure, compensation, state transition, and later function or findings. It can connect observations held by different disciplines and generate testable hypotheses about history, reserve, recurrence, or recovery. It does not recover a unique cause by definition. Its value must be demonstrated against existing frameworks, and a valid result may be that the relevant history is unrecoverable, nonunique, clinically unnecessary, or low priority.

## Narrative arc

The chapter opens with one present local endpoint and several plausible histories rather than with a preferred route. It first separates endpoint from dated exposure history and then introduces the six-family taxonomy as a common descriptive vocabulary. It follows compensation and cumulative demand through time while preserving recovery and nonprogression. It next states where routing history might matter before or beyond rehabilitation, then maps how several disciplines can contribute different nodes without any one of them owning the whole chain. It closes by defining the framework as a question-generating and research object whose value must survive comparison, measurement, and the possibility of no added value.

## Argument sequence

### Opening — one endpoint, several compatible histories

Use a neutral, hypothetical local endpoint: a focal tissue finding, a repeatedly irritated interface, or a task-limited region with a documented present state. Show three compatible histories: a discrete local event; cumulative repeated exposure with compensation; and a primarily biological or contextual process that later changed mechanics. The histories must be equally visible and explicitly non-diagnostic.

The opening should use the evidence-state grammar. The endpoint may be `OBS` or `MEAS`; portions of the history may be dated observations; internal distributions may be `CALC` or `MOD`; missing intervals and route relations remain `INF` or `HYP`. No history wins by narrative smoothness.

### 18.1 From a local endpoint to a history of demand

**Job:** Define what a routing history records and why a present state underdetermines it.

Walk backward only far enough to expose the inverse problem. The same tissue or interface state may occur within different tasks, geometries, active strategies, dose histories, injuries, biological conditions, and recovery contexts. A reconstruction should identify the candidate initiating or recurring exposure, observed response or transition, and current measurement as separate times.

Then reverse direction and write the history prospectively: task/environment → configuration/paths → demand/distribution → local exposure → response through time → function/finding/symptom. Each arrow receives its own proposition and alternative. The purpose is not to make the chain complete but to reveal what must be known.

**Exit:** Histories need a shared descriptive vocabulary that does not pretend to be diagnosis.

### 18.2 The adverse-organization taxonomy supplies a common language

**Job:** Carry the Part III six-family taxonomy into medical inquiry without reteaching it.

Briefly crosswalk global redistribution, local concentration, segmental instability, timing or handoff failure, constraint-maintained deformation or lost excursion, and compensatory rerouting to the nodes and arrows they can describe. The families can overlap, change with time, or remain absent. Scale transition describes where a proposed effect travels rather than adding a seventh failure label.

The taxonomy's immediate value is comparability: different regions and specialties can describe a relation without asserting identical anatomy, biology, or clinical consequence. Its validity and reliability remain research obligations.

**Exit:** Once patterns can be named across time, the project can state its cumulative-demand hypothesis without making delayed failure inevitable.

### 18.3 Cumulative demand can matter before symptoms or a discrete injury

**Job:** Preserve the compensation-before-symptom hypothesis with recovery and null branches.

Use Chapter 15's conditional sequence: altered route or task state → successful compensation → changed next dose → possible narrowing of reserve or local exposure → possible later finding or symptom. Mark recovery, useful adaptation, stable compensation, unrelated symptom development, and no meaningful change after every step.

Distinguish absence of a discrete injury from evidence of cumulative injury. Imaging that is absent, delayed, common, or nonspecific cannot validate a hidden history. The chapter should ask what prospective or repeated measures would improve the reconstruction.

**Exit:** A history can be medically interesting before rehabilitation only if it changes a defined question or outcome.

### 18.4 The intended relevance is upstream of and wider than rehabilitation

**Job:** State intended applications without presenting clinical utility as established.

Candidate applications include exposure research, recurrence, functional reserve, device and surgical context, recovery conditions, environmental design, prevention research, and interpretation of changing function. In ambiguous chronic presentations, routing may organize context while remaining a low-priority contributor or consequence.

For every application, name the comparator: existing history taking, ergonomics, biomechanical assessment, physical therapy, occupational medicine, or standard risk modeling. “Upstream” should mean earlier or broader in the question being asked, not higher in professional authority or causal hierarchy.

**Exit:** No one discipline contains every node, which makes the proposed object potentially shared—but also vulnerable to duplication and incoherence.

### 18.5 A shared object of study across disciplines

**Job:** Map complementary contributions without erasing expertise or claiming ownership.

Biomechanics can model distribution and exposure; rehabilitation can assess movement, function, capacity, adaptation, and repeated response; medical specialties can define tissue states, diagnoses, exclusions, and local endpoints; occupational and environmental inquiry can describe tasks and constraints; developmental, psychological, social, and patient-reported histories can explain state and available options.

Use one chain with each discipline attached to the nodes it can legitimately observe or model. Overlap is expected. Conflicting observations and missing ownership should remain visible. Chapter 22, not this chapter, decides how information enters a clinical encounter.

**Exit:** A shared object is valuable only if it produces better questions or evidence than existing fragmented or discipline-specific accounts.

### 18.6 A framework for questions, not a treatment system

**Job:** Define the immediate product and its no-added-value conditions.

The framework's first output is a structured hypothesis: what was observed, what route is proposed, what exposure is missing, what alternative history fits, and what measurement could discriminate. It does not yield an exercise, screening score, correction sequence, or universal intervention.

State disfavoring results directly: routing records cannot be made reliable; candidate histories remain too nonunique; added variables do not improve prediction, explanation, communication, or outcome; the relevant local endpoint is already adequately explained and managed; or the approach reproduces existing practice without meaningful integration. These outcomes narrow the program rather than invalidate the fact that mechanics exists.

**Exit:** The next unresolved arrow is biological: even a well-described local exposure does not establish what a living tissue does in response.

### Conclusion and handoff

Return to the opening endpoint and leave all compatible histories visible. Show what a longitudinal routing record can contribute—temporal structure, path alternatives, compensation, exposure hypotheses, and cross-disciplinary questions—and what it cannot—unique cause, tissue response, diagnosis, or treatment.

Chapter 19 inherits a precisely bounded local-exposure node. It must show how a defined mechanical history interacts with tissue state, dose, recovery, mechanotransduction, remodeling, fatigue, sensitization, injury, and repair before any claim reaches function, finding, or symptom.

## Model obligations

### Longitudinal routing record

1. **Object represented:** Dated task, configuration, route, distribution, local exposure, compensation, state transition, and current endpoint.
2. **System boundary and scale:** Person–environment history with selected regional and tissue scales; boundaries redrawn explicitly at each level.
3. **Variables and states:** Nodes from the shared causal grammar, labeled `OBS`, `MEAS`, `CALC`, `MOD`, `INF`, `HYP`, or `EMB`; reconstructed is a temporal qualifier.
4. **Assumptions:** Missing intervals, recall limits, model nonuniqueness, changing biology, treatment effects, and alternate histories.
5. **What the model explains:** Which pieces of a proposed history are known, reconstructed, contested, or absent.
6. **What it cannot establish:** Unique causation, complete exposure, tissue response, clinical value, or treatment.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Abandon or narrow when records are unreliable, non-discriminating, or add no value against comparators.
8. **Registry action:** to create; inherit `BGB-M-0002`, `BGB-M-0003`, and `BGB-M-0004` rather than duplicating them.

### Same endpoint, compatible histories model

1. **Object represented:** Several possible sequences converging on one current endpoint.
2. **System boundary and scale:** One bounded local endpoint linked to task, biology, and whole-person context.
3. **Variables and states:** Dated event, repeated exposure, biological primary, compensation, current state, and missing observations.
4. **Assumptions:** Histories are candidates, not exhaustive or equally probable without evidence.
5. **What the model explains:** Why present state does not retrospectively prove route or cause.
6. **What it cannot establish:** That upstream reconstruction is always necessary or possible.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Revise when a documented acute event or validated causal criterion identifies the history more strongly.
8. **Registry action:** to create.

### Cross-disciplinary contribution map

1. **Object represented:** Which disciplines observe, measure, model, interpret, or manage each chain node.
2. **System boundary and scale:** Research and care ecosystem, not the body itself.
3. **Variables and states:** Information type, evidence state, handoff, overlap, uncertainty, and decision use.
4. **Assumptions:** Professional roles vary across systems and jurisdictions.
5. **What the model explains:** Why the routing object is integrative and where duplication may occur.
6. **What it cannot establish:** New professional authority, workflow benefit, or ownership.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Replace when existing integrated frameworks already perform the proposed job.
8. **Registry action:** to create.

## Evidence and source obligations

### Foundational or definitional support

- Existing longitudinal exposure, cumulative-load, kinetic-chain, compensation, rehabilitation, ergonomics, occupational, and systems-biomechanics frameworks.
- Causal and inverse-problem literature explaining why current endpoints can underdetermine prior histories.
- Clear definitions and candidate reliability measures for every taxonomy family used across regions.

### Mechanism or model support

- Prospective and repeated-measure methods capable of recording task, configuration, distribution, local exposure, state transition, and recovery.
- Reconstruction methods that expose assumptions and missing time rather than presenting a synthetic history as observation.
- Comparisons with established clinical, biomechanical, and rehabilitation assessment models.

### Biological or medical bridge

- No tissue response is established in this chapter. All exposure-to-response claims are handed to Chapter 19.
- Medical relevance requires a named endpoint, population, comparator, timeframe, and incremental-value measure.
- Disease, risk, recurrence, prevention, and recovery applications require condition-specific evidence and medical-coauthor review.

### Alternatives and negative evidence

- Clear local events with little need for body-wide reconstruction.
- Nonunique histories and low reliability.
- Existing disciplines already capturing the same information.
- Biological or contextual primaries that better explain the endpoint.
- Detailed records that do not improve explanation, prediction, communication, or outcome.

### Provenance and salvage

- Preserve the direct project ambition to make routing a medical question before and beyond rehabilitation.
- Preserve the full six-family and cumulative-demand program as a proposed research language.
- Treat claims of a “missing medical foundation,” inevitable delayed failure, or global mechanical origin as overstrong formulations, not reasons to delete the protected idea.
- Generated planning material supplies chapter jobs and acceptance tests, not evidence.

### Required review

- Biomechanics, rehabilitation, ergonomics, occupational medicine, and exposure-method review.
- Causal-inference and longitudinal-method review.
- Medical-coauthor review of every proposed application.
- Interprofessional review to prevent novelty and ownership overclaim.

## Principal objections and responses

### “This is already biomechanics or physical therapy.”

Much of it may be. The chapter requires explicit comparison and permits narrowing or loss of novelty. The proposed addition survives only where a longitudinal, cross-boundary account performs a demonstrable job not already handled adequately.

### “A chronic routing history cannot be reconstructed reliably.”

Often it cannot be reconstructed uniquely. The model records missing intervals, supports partial chains, and treats unrecoverable history as a legitimate result. Prospective research may be more important than retrospective confidence.

### “A larger story is not automatically a better medical story.”

Agreed. Incremental value must be defined against a comparator. A local endpoint may be sufficient for care, and a body-wide account may add cost, noise, or false certainty.

### “The taxonomy can fit anything.”

Each family requires discriminating observations, alternatives, and null states. If reliability or predictive value remains poor, the taxonomy must be narrowed, revised, or replaced.
