## Chapter job

This chapter completes Part I by placing Chapter 2's quantities and Chapter 3's roles inside a changing three-dimensional configuration. It shows how position, orientation, contact, moment arms, curvature, active force, pressure, material state, and boundary conditions affect the paths available for carrying a demand. *Configuration* becomes more than visible posture, and *load path* remains a bounded explanatory representation rather than a hidden anatomical pipe.

The chapter also owns an essential ontology boundary. Several useful descriptions may each involve three directions, but they are not thereby the same object:

1. a local or global spatial frame used to express position, vectors, and tensors;
2. the project's reduced axial, opening/rotational, and expansive generalized modes with their conjugate loads;
3. a possible material-symmetry frame used in an anisotropic or orthotropic constitutive model; and
4. principal-stress directions derived from a stress tensor at a point.

Book I teaches the first description, names the others, and prevents their conflation. It does not derive the generalized modes from spatial orthogonality, material symmetry, or principal stress; it does not make whole-body orthotropy foundational; and it does not introduce the TCM sign map. The generalized-mode preview remains one contained seam into Book II.

**This chapter must:**

- move the reader from planar snapshots to a genuine three-dimensional event;
- distinguish room, anatomical, segment, task, surface, and tissue-local frames;
- show how geometry and boundary conditions change moments, contact, and path availability;
- define configuration as a mechanical state richer than posture;
- define load paths as scale-, task-, and evidence-dependent representations;
- distinguish spatial frames, generalized modes, material axes, and principal-stress descriptions; and
- hand Part II a framework for asking which anatomical structures perform the roles.

**This chapter must not:**

- imply that anatomical planes are a complete coordinate system for every structure;
- treat a visible position as a unique internal mechanical state;
- infer local stress, strain, tissue response, or harm from geometry alone;
- call a generalized opening moment a circumferential spatial vector;
- identify optional orthotropic axes with generalized modes or principal stresses;
- claim that the existence of three orthogonal directions validates the project's TCM map; or
- develop preferred signs, Six-Division correspondence, torus geometry, bandhas, or corrective practice.

## Reader entry and exit

**The reader enters** likely treating configuration as posture, anatomical planes as a complete mechanical description, and any three named body directions as variants of the same coordinate system.

**The reader exits** able to choose a frame, state what object its axes describe, distinguish geometry from force and material response, represent multiple changing load paths, and explain why generalized modes, material axes, and principal-stress directions cannot be inferred merely from a three-direction spatial basis.

## Central question

How does three-dimensional configuration change what an external demand becomes inside a living body, and which distinct spatial, material, stress, and generalized-mode descriptions are needed to represent that process without category errors?

## Working answer

External demand becomes internally meaningful through a configuration comprising geometry, orientation, contact, active force, pressure, material state, stress and strain fields, and available motion. These quantities are expressed relative to declared global or local frames, and demand may be carried through several changing paths. A spatial basis describes directions in space; a generalized mode describes a family of configuration changes and a conjugate load; a material frame describes constitutive directionality; and a principal-stress frame is derived from the local stress tensor. Their possible relationships are research questions, not identities established by the number three.

## Controlled terms and distinctions

| Object | Working use | What determines it | What it is not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anatomical plane and axis | Conventional descriptive reference for position or movement | Standard anatomical convention and named body region | A complete force, stress, or material description |
| Global spatial frame | Room-, gravity-, or task-fixed basis for position and vector components | Chosen reference and sign convention | An intrinsic preferred body organization |
| Local spatial frame | Anatomy-, centerline-, surface-, or segment-fixed basis that may vary with position | Declared landmarks, centerline, surface, or task | A generalized mode or material law |
| Configuration | Geometry plus contact, orientation, active forces, pressure, material state, fields, and available motion at a stated time | System boundary, state variables, reference state, and task | Visible posture alone |
| Load path | An explanatory representation of how demand is distributed and resolved through selected carriers | Boundary, force balance, contacts, carriers, scale, task, and evidence state | A necessarily unique line or anatomical tube |
| Generalized mode | A reduced family of configuration changes paired with a conjugate force or moment through an explicit model | Chosen generalized coordinate and virtual-work or equivalent formulation | An ordinary spatial vector component |
| Material-symmetry frame | Axes or planes used to describe direction-dependent constitutive behavior | Material structure and selected constitutive approximation | A spatial frame chosen for convenience or a principal-stress frame |
| Principal-stress frame | Eigenvector directions of the symmetric Cauchy stress tensor at a defined point and state, when uniquely defined | The current stress tensor | A permanent anatomical, material, or preferred direction |

Principal-strain directions require their own declared strain measure and need not be presumed identical to principal-stress or material axes. Book I may name this additional caution but does not need to teach tensor spectral analysis.

## Argument sequence

### Opening — one transfer seen from three places

Return to `BGB-X-0001`. Show the object transfer in a room or gravity frame, in a frame attached to a selected segment or joint, and in a local frame following a curved or deforming structure. The external event is unchanged, but the useful components, moment arms, contacts, and descriptive axes change.

Use the opening to expose a category error: the fact that a force has three spatial components does not make those components the same as three families of anatomical movement, three material directions, or three traditional categories.

### 4.1 From anatomical planes to a three-dimensional event

Review sagittal, frontal, and transverse planes as useful conventions, then show why a real task can combine translation, rotation, curvature change, contact migration, pressure change, and active force. A movement visible mainly in one plane may have consequential out-of-plane components, but three-dimensional completeness does not mean that every small component is important.

Differentiate position, orientation, displacement, velocity, force, and moment. A curved arrow alone does not establish a torque without a stated axis and point; an observed rotation does not establish the moment that produced or resisted it. Preserve Chapter 2's quantity discipline.

Use `BGB-X-0002` if its joint is selected: the same external force can create different moments when the line of action or joint position changes, while contact distribution still requires geometry and additional internal information.

**Exit:** Anatomical planes organize observation, but a mechanical account must identify the actual spatial objects and relations involved.

### 4.2 Global, local, and moving frames

Define a global or task-fixed frame, then local frames attached to segments, joint surfaces, tissue directions, or a curved centerline. “Up,” “out,” “axial,” and “rotational” acquire meaning only relative to the stated frame and anatomy. A frame can translate and rotate; a curvilinear basis can change direction from point to point.

Introduce a local cylindrical or curvilinear frame only as an example: longitudinal tangent `e_z`, outward-normal or radial `e_r` where a centerline and cross-section justify it, and circumferential tangent `e_theta`. State that outward normal and radial coincide only in suitably regular geometry. The body need not be treated as a cylinder.

Explain transformation qualitatively: a physical vector or tensor does not become a different physical object merely because its components change under a new basis. By contrast, changing the model boundary, reference configuration, or generalized coordinate can change the question being asked.

**Exit:** A frame is a declared language for spatial components, not a discovered body ontology.

### 4.3 Geometry changes mechanical consequence

Develop center of mass, line of action, moment arm, contact point, joint congruence, curvature, lever relation, rotational inertia, and boundary condition as mechanisms by which the same nominal external demand can produce different internal requirements. Keep kinematics, kinetics, and constitutive response distinct.

Use two configurations of the object-transfer task with the same object and endpoint. Show that different support locations and segment relations change external moments and candidate internal paths. Do not rank the configurations globally. A configuration may reduce one demand while increasing another, and two people can use different viable solutions.

Introduce geometry as necessary but insufficient. Internal muscle forces, joint contact, pressure, tissue properties, fatigue, and control may differ even when visible alignment appears similar. Conversely, visible variation may coexist with similar task performance and tolerable exposure.

**Exit:** Geometry changes the mechanical problem, but appearance does not determine the full internal solution or its biological meaning.

### 4.4 Configuration is more than posture

Define configuration as the state required to model the event: anatomical geometry and orientation; contacts and constraints; active muscle forces; baseline tension; internal pressure; tissue or material state; stress and strain fields; and available movement. State the reference time or phase.

Contrast three cases with similar visible posture but different internal states: rested versus fatigued support, active versus externally supported holding, or a pressure-bearing assembly before and during a phase change. Then contrast two visibly different configurations that remain viable for the same task.

This definition prevents *posture* from carrying claims about path, exposure, capacity, or health. Posture can be one observation inside configuration, not a verdict about it.

**Exit:** The book can now discuss route availability and role distribution without pretending they are visible directly from shape.

### 4.5 A load path is an organized explanation of transfer

Apply `BGB-M-0004`. A path may run through skeletal contact, active muscle, tensile structures, pressure-bearing assemblies, external support, or several carriers in parallel. It may branch, change by phase, and appear as a network or field rather than a line. The choice of representation must match the evidence and question.

For the object transfer, distinguish an external force's line of action from an inferred internal path. The external line is geometrically defined by a force. The internal route requires a boundary, carriers, balance, scale, and evidence state. A path drawn through visually continuous anatomy is not established by appearance.

Connect to Chapter 3: hard and soft roles occur within paths, but *hard role* and *load path* are not synonyms. A path may include form-receiving tissues; a pressure system may contribute to structural support; an assembly may change roles as the path changes.

**Exit:** Paths make routing inspectable while preserving multiplicity, inference, and uncertainty.

### 4.6 Four different three-direction descriptions

Present a controlled four-column comparison, not a unified-body diagram:

1. **Spatial frame:** a chosen basis such as room axes or local `(e_z,e_r,e_theta)` for expressing vectors and tensors.
2. **Generalized modes:** the project's axial, opening/rotational, and expansive families, represented by generalized coordinates and conjugate loads. The opening coordinate is a relative angular relation; the expansive coordinate may concern outward-normal displacement, width, area, perimeter, or another declared measure.
3. **Material frame:** optional axes of a selected anisotropic or orthotropic constitutive model, established by tissue structure and measurements.
4. **Principal-stress frame:** local eigenvector directions of the current stress tensor, which can change with loading and may not coincide with material or anatomical axes.

Use two compact checks. First, `e_theta` is a circumferential tangent vector, while an opening mode is an angular relation and its conjugate load a moment; they are not the same object. Second, rib rotation can contribute to thoracic expansion while rotation and expansion remain distinct observables. The same logic applies to a pelvic “scissors” analogy: relative opening among paired structures need not mean a literal large opening of the pelvic ring or the same quantity as width change.

State the contained Book II preview: a complex body may be reduced to several generalized coordinates that need not be Cartesian vector components. Book II must define the axial, opening, and expansive variables, their conjugate loads, signs, coupling, transformations, and predictions. Book III later develops the Yin/Yang and channel correspondences. Nothing in the spatial, material, or principal-stress descriptions proves those later maps.

**Exit:** The project retains all four descriptions as legitimate research tools while refusing to use their shared dimensionality as a derivation.

### Conclusion and handoff

Reconstruct the object transfer as one three-dimensional event described through declared boundaries and frames. Geometry, contact, active force, pressure, material state, and available motion create a configuration; several paths may share the demand; hard and soft roles occur within those paths; the internal organization is not uniquely visible from posture.

End Part I with an anatomical question: which structures and systems establish, guide, tension, cushion, contain, pressurize, and change these paths in an actual living body? Chapter 5 begins with the skeleton and joints, while Chapter 6 adds the active, tensile, compliant, sliding, and pressure-bearing systems that make skeletal possibilities function.

## Principal objections and responses

### “This is too technical for an opening foundation.”

The chapter uses one recurring task and visual distinctions rather than a tensor course. The technical detail is limited to what prevents later category errors: frame, object, quantity, boundary, and evidence state.

### “Three-dimensional biomechanics is already standard.”

Agreed. Book I relies on standard spatial reasoning. The project-specific contribution begins only when that reasoning is used to control configuration and routing questions; standard 3D mechanics is not presented as a novelty.

### “Any three orthogonal directions support the proposed three-mode map.”

No. A spatial basis, generalized-coordinate set, material-symmetry frame, and principal-stress frame answer different questions. Shared component count does not establish equivalence or correspondence.

### “Orthotropic tissues validate orthotropy of the whole body.”

Local orthotropic models may be useful for selected tissues under defined regimes. They neither make the whole body a homogeneous orthotropic solid nor derive the generalized modes.

### “Configuration is just a more elaborate word for posture.”

Configuration includes active forces, pressure, contacts, material state, stress and strain fields, and available motion. Similar posture can conceal different configurations, and different postures can remain mechanically viable.

### “Load path is only metaphor.”

A path is a model whose boundary, carriers, quantities, balance, evidence state, and alternatives must be stated. When those cannot be justified, a diffuse field, multiple routes, or no privileged path is the more accurate representation.

### “The Book II preview smuggles preferred configuration into Book I.”

The preview states only that generalized coordinates can differ from Cartesian components and names the later research object. It supplies no preferred signs, efficiency metric, traditional mapping, or proof.
