# Chapter 2 — A Working Language of Load

**Unit:** `BGB-U-0002`

**Status:** Accepted `R2`; controlled first draft authorized; publication review open

**Series role:** Book I foundation; inherited by every later book

## Chapter job

Give the reader a compact mechanical grammar that can be used throughout the series without turning the chapter into an engineering survey. The chapter should make five habits automatic:

1. choose the system and boundary before naming what it carries;
2. distinguish external demand from internal force, stress, strain, pressure, and deformation;
3. state direction, scale, time, and carrier;
4. distinguish an external line of action, a modeled load path, and an embodied or inferred force line rather than turning any of them into a literal hidden cable; and
5. translate broad language such as “load” or “strain” into the more exact quantity needed by the argument.

The chapter succeeds when later prose can use these distinctions briefly because the reader already understands them.

Within the vertical slice, this chapter supplies the representational rules that Chapters 13 and 24 must obey. It does not establish the hard/soft model or a clinical mechanism; it makes both inspectable by fixing what their arrows, boundaries, quantities, and inferences can mean.

## Reader entry and exit

**The reader enters** accepting that living bodies are material systems but may still use *load*, *force*, *pressure*, *stress*, and *strain* interchangeably. They may also imagine that a load path is a fixed anatomical line.

**The reader exits** able to ask: What is the system? What crosses its boundary? Which quantity is being described? In what frame and direction? Over what time? What internal exposure is observed, calculated, modeled, or hypothesized, and through which carrier or representation?

## Central question

What must “load” mean for a body-wide mechanical narrative to remain inspectable from external task to local tissue exposure?

## Working answer

*Load* is a useful umbrella for mechanical demand, not a single scalar or tissue state. A defensible account starts with a chosen system and external contacts, then distinguishes the forces, moments, pressures, internal resultants, stresses, strains, deformations, work, energy, and time-dependent capacities relevant to that system. A load path is a bounded account of distribution and transfer; its geometry and carriers must be stated rather than assumed.

## Argument sequence

### Opening — one object, several mechanical descriptions

Use the recurring task of lifting an object from one support, carrying it, and placing it on another. Describe the same event from several legitimate boundaries:

- person and object together;
- person alone;
- one foot and the ground;
- a limb or joint;
- a selected tissue region; and
- a compliant conduit within that region.

The external event has not changed, but the relevant forces, moments, pressures, and internal exposures have. This makes the need for system definition intuitive before any formal vocabulary appears.

### 2.1 Choose the system before describing the force

Introduce free-body reasoning in plain language. Identify the boundary, environment, contact points, gravity, support, acceleration, and interaction forces. Explain that an internal interaction for one chosen system becomes an external force when the boundary is redrawn.

The section should establish that “the body carries twenty kilograms” and “a tendon carries a tensile force” are not statements at the same level. Moving between them requires geometry, equilibrium or dynamics, contact, and a model of internal distribution.

### 2.2 Force, moment, torque, and pressure

Define force as a vector with magnitude, direction, and point or region of application. Introduce moment as the turning tendency of force about a selected point or axis and show why moment arm matters. Treat *torque* as context-dependent terminology rather than a second unrelated phenomenon.

Distinguish a resultant force from distributed contact. Introduce pressure as a local scalar field that produces normal traction on a boundary; where *contact pressure* is used for a solid interface, define it as the normal component of the distributed contact traction. Its mechanical or physiological role depends on boundaries, gradients, material response, and—where a fluid is involved—flow. Do not imply that pressure is automatically supportive or harmful.

### 2.3 Stress, strain, and deformation

Move across the system boundary into a material. Stress describes internal force intensity and orientation; strain describes relative deformation. Introduce tension, compression, shear, bending, torsion, and multiaxial states as recurring components rather than mutually exclusive labels.

Make three distinctions explicit:

- external force is not local stress;
- visible displacement is not a complete strain field; and
- stress and strain are related through material behavior and boundary conditions, not interchangeable synonyms.

Strain rate belongs here because a living tissue may respond differently to the same nominal deformation applied at different rates.

### 2.4 Stiffness, compliance, strength, tolerance, and capacity

Define stiffness as the relation between demand and deformation for a stated system and direction; define compliance as the corresponding tendency to yield. Neither term should become a permanent tissue identity.

Separate material strength or failure threshold from functional capacity. In living systems, capacity may include active control, fatigue, prior exposure, pain, tissue state, recovery, adaptation, and task familiarity. The chapter should avoid implying that a single laboratory material constant determines what a person can tolerate.

### 2.5 Mechanical dose and history

Replace “too much load” with a multidimensional dose: magnitude, direction, rate, repetition, duration, variability, sequence, rest, and recovery. A high peak, low repeated exposure, sustained deformation, and rapidly varying impact can be mechanically different even when one summary number matches.

This section should prepare Chapters 10 and 19 without making biological-response claims that belong there. The point is that a mechanical history must be described before its biological significance can be evaluated.

### 2.6 Work, energy, transfer, and load paths

Introduce work, power, energy storage, return, and dissipation only to the depth later chapters require. Avoid treating energetic efficiency as already defined for the whole body.

Use a three-level comparison:

- An **external line of action** is the geometric line associated with a stated force in a stated frame. It does not describe internal distribution.
- A **load path** is a model of how a chosen demand is distributed, transferred, and ultimately resolved through a selected system. It may be a line, branching network, distributed field, or time-varying relation. It must name candidate carriers such as skeletal contact, joint resultants, tensile structures, active muscle, pressure-mediated support, or a combination.
- A **force line** is controlled project vocabulary. It may refer to the ordinary external line of action, an inferred internal multicarrier relation, or an embodied experience of continuity that motivates a hypothesis. Its intended sense must be stated.

These objects may be related, but the chapter must not fuse them. For an inferred or embodied force line to become a mechanical claim, the writer must name its scale, candidate carriers, quantity, task, and provenance; otherwise it remains explicitly relational or experiential. This preserves the project's Yin or form-receiving sense of internal connection for later development without pretending that it is already one measured cable or resultant.

### Conclusion and handoff

Return to the object-transfer task and describe it once more using the new grammar. Close with the idea that precise quantities do not yet tell us which structure ought to perform which job. Chapter 3 therefore asks how a living body divides mechanical labor between relatively shape-preserving and relatively form-receiving roles.

## Load-bearing distinctions

- System boundary versus anatomical region.
- External demand versus internal distribution.
- Force and moment versus pressure.
- Stress versus strain versus deformation.
- Stiffness/compliance versus strength/capacity.
- Instantaneous state versus dose and history.
- Spatial line of action versus inferred load path versus embodied force line.
- Engineering description versus biological or medical consequence.

These distinctions should be carried into the [claim-evidence ledger](../claim-evidence-ledger.md) rather than multiplied into claims for every definition.

### Primary example

The recurring object transfer. It should appear at the opening, at one or two conceptual transitions, and at the conclusion.

### Short supporting examples

- A backpack whose unchanged weight creates a different trunk moment with a changed distance from the body.
- A hip or knee contact problem in which external load does not specify local contact stress.
- A tendon under tensile force to distinguish force from stress and deformation.
- A vessel or pressure-bearing cavity to distinguish wall loading, pressure, deformation, and flow.

Each example should perform one definitional job. None should become a premature clinical narrative.

## Evidence and source obligations

- Use authoritative continuum/statics/dynamics sources for definitions and units.
- Use biomechanics sources to show how system boundary, joint resultants, contact, and tissue exposure are actually handled in living systems.
- Source nonlinear, anisotropic, viscoelastic, poroelastic, active, and history-dependent qualifications where they enter the prose.
- Do not use a citation about one tissue or loading regime as universal evidence for all living materials.
- Treat engineering analogies as models whose limits are stated, not evidence that a medical conclusion follows.
- Use the [definitions and notation pack](../evidence/chapter-02-definitions-and-notation.md) as the controlling technical sheet for object type, units, frames, reference configurations, assumptions, and category-error checks.

This chapter is medically low-exposure, but terminological errors here would propagate through the entire series. Formal and visual review are therefore as important as citation completeness.

The evidence pass should produce a compact definition-and-units sheet rather than a literature catalogue. Each later biological qualification should be sourced where it is used; this chapter needs only enough constitutive complexity to prevent a simple mechanical diagram from being mistaken for a universal tissue model.

## Principal objections and responses

### “This reduces the body to an engineered object.”

The chapter defines a language for one dimension of a living system. Active control, remodeling, biology, pain, behavior, and context alter the model; they are not excluded by using mechanics precisely.

### “The vocabulary is too technical for an opening Part.”

Use the recurring task and diagrams to carry the explanation. Include only terms that do real work later, and move specialized constitutive detail into notes, figures, or the model registry.

### “A load path suggests one correct route.”

Define it as a system- and question-dependent representation. Multiple structures and carriers may share or redistribute demand, and later books investigate—not assume—whether a preferred global organization exists.

### “A force line is either a real force or merely subjective.”

The phrase is being used for three different but legitimate project objects. An external line of action has an ordinary mechanical definition; an inferred internal relation is a model; an embodied line is an observation capable of generating a model. Keeping those statuses distinct preserves the observation without granting it evidence it does not yet have.

### “A diagram will make an inferred internal route look measured.”

Use distinct visual notation for external forces, calculated resultants, modeled internal distributions, and authorial or embodied hypotheses.
