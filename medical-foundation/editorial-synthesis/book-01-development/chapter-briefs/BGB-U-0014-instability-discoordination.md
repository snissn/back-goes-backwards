## Chapter job

This chapter separates two failure families that can look similar at the endpoint but require different observations. *Segmental or task-specific instability* concerns failure to maintain or recover a usable relationship under a defined demand. *Discoordination or handoff failure* concerns timing, sequencing, and phase relations among participants that may each be structurally capable. Both can generate repeated correction, altered contact, extra work, or protective bracing without a dramatic lesion.

The chapter must keep protection, useful variability, guarding, instability, and discoordination distinct. It also preserves a project-native feedback hypothesis: altered position or tissue state may change sensory information; uncertainty may provoke co-contraction or bracing; sustained bracing may change movement options and sensory input; that changed input may reinforce further protection. Every arrow remains conditional. The loop may be useful, transient, pain-mediated, or absent, and it must not be turned into a universal autonomic or disease narrative.

**This chapter must:**

- define instability by a task-relevant relationship rather than by movement amount alone;
- define discoordination through timing, sequence, and relation rather than weakness alone;
- show how repeated correction can become a dose;
- model the proposed proprioception–bracing loop with alternatives at each link;
- distinguish protective stabilization, normal variability, guarding, fatigue, fear, habit, and inadequate control; and
- require dynamic and repeated-task observations rather than relying on static shape.

**This chapter must not:**

- equate mobility, flexibility, asymmetry, noise, or pain with instability;
- infer discoordination from one visually unfamiliar strategy;
- treat increased muscle activity as automatically inefficient or harmful;
- present the feedback loop as demonstrated body-wide causality;
- diagnose a joint or motor-control disorder; or
- prescribe stabilization, cueing, retraining, or corrective exercise.

## Reader entry and exit

**The reader enters** able to describe a route and a maintained constraint but may still use *unstable*, *uncoordinated*, *guarded*, and *compensating* as interchangeable judgments about movement.

**The reader exits** able to ask whether a defined relationship was lost, whether it was recovered, whether capable participants arrived in the wrong sequence, whether correction repeated, whether bracing was protective, and which dynamic observations would distinguish those possibilities.

## Central question

How can a living structure generate repeated mechanical demand through loss of control or altered timing even when its materials remain intact and the task is ultimately completed?

## Working answer

Instability occurs when a selected segment or assembly cannot adequately maintain or recover the relationships required by a defined task. Discoordination occurs when otherwise available structures, contacts, or actions are mistimed or poorly sequenced. Either may require repeated correction or protective bracing, creating a time-dependent exposure. Their meaning depends on task success, adaptability, pain, fatigue, sensory state, and comparison with normal variability; neither can be inferred from movement amount or static shape alone.

## Narrative arc

The chapter opens with a task that ends successfully but contains repeated catching or correction. It first defines instability around a required relation and then separates timing failure from material or route unavailability. It converts small corrective events into a dose using Chapter 10's temporal language. The proposed sensory-control/bracing loop is then developed as a branching hypothesis rather than an inevitable cascade. A comparison section distinguishes protection and variability from failure, and the chapter closes with the measurement requirement that shape and timing be observed through perturbation, repetition, and recovery.

## Argument sequence

### Opening — the task succeeds, but correction repeats

Use a hypothetical low-stakes balance, step, reaching, or load-transfer task in which a segment briefly loses a relation, catches, and recovers. The endpoint is successful. Compare one isolated correction with repeated corrections across many trials. The scene is not a diagnosis and should not make the unfamiliar strategy look defective.

The opening establishes why endpoint success and static imaging can miss the defining event. It also creates the distinction the chapter will test: was the relation itself insufficiently controlled, or did capable participants arrive in an altered sequence?

### 14.1 Instability is task-specific loss of sufficient control

**Job:** Define instability without equating it with mobility or structural damage.

Identify the relation a task requires: contact, translation, rotation, pressure boundary, alignment within a tolerance, or recovery after perturbation. Instability may involve excessive movement for the task, insufficient stiffness, delayed response, recurrent loss of contact, or inability to return. The same joint can be stable for one demand and insufficiently controlled for another.

Anatomical laxity, visible motion, or variability may coexist with excellent control. Conversely, a relatively still segment can be actively braced because control feels uncertain. The term must therefore name task, scale, perturbation, and outcome.

**Exit:** A relation can also fail despite adequate materials and potential stability if its participants are mistimed.

### 14.2 Discoordination is a problem of timing and relation

**Job:** Separate sequence and phase from route interruption, weakness, and fixed constraint.

Describe how muscle activation, joint motion, contact, pressure, or neighboring segments may arrive too early, too late, or in a sequence that leaves another structure temporarily unprepared. Rib–scapular, limb–trunk, or foot–ankle examples can illustrate the pattern, but they must remain bounded and measurable.

Discoordination is not established by aesthetic smoothness or comparison with one ideal technique. It becomes consequential only where timing predicts a defined exposure, repeated correction, loss of performance, or reduced adaptability.

**Exit:** Both instability and discoordination may produce small corrective events whose importance lies in repetition and timing.

### 14.3 Repeated correction can become its own dose

**Job:** Apply the Chapter 10 exposure framework to micro-motion and control work.

Follow loss of contact, sudden catching, repositioning, deceleration, recurrent muscle bursts, or force variability as repeated events. A small event repeated often may produce meaningful work, strain rate, activation, or fatigue even when no single trial looks dramatic. Equally, repeated correction may remain harmless or improve skill.

The chapter must identify the actual quantity being repeated. “Microtrauma” cannot substitute for measured correction. If biological consequence is named, it is routed to Chapter 19.

**Exit:** Repeated correction can alter sensory input and provoke protection, creating the possibility of a feedback relation.

### 14.4 Proprioception and bracing may form a feedback loop

**Job:** Preserve the proposed loop while keeping every arrow open to evidence and reversal.

Lay out separate candidate links: changed position, movement, tissue state, or pain may change sensory information; uncertain or threatening control may provoke co-contraction or bracing; bracing may reduce options, alter effort, modify contact, or change sensory input; the changed state may reinforce, resolve, or have no effect on protection. The loop can begin at several points and may be therapeutically or functionally useful during an acute phase.

Include alternatives at each arrow: pain without instability, bracing without proprioceptive deficit, sensory change secondary to inflammation, fear or instruction altering control, and stable performance despite atypical activation.

**Exit:** Similar visible patterns can represent protection, learning, fatigue, or failure, so comparison is essential.

### 14.5 Protection, variability, guarding, and failure can look alike

**Job:** Prevent the model from pathologizing adaptive control.

Compare intentional stabilization, exploratory variability, pain-related guarding, fatigue drift, habitual strategy, and inadequate control. Ask whether the strategy preserves the task, adapts when demand changes, consumes increasing effort, recovers, and predicts local exposure. Greater activation can be necessary and efficient; less movement can be the correct response to a perturbation.

Include disabled, aging, trained, and anatomically variable bodies as possible sources of capable alternate control rather than deviations from a single norm.

**Exit:** Static observation cannot distinguish these states; the measurement program must capture timing, perturbation, repetition, and recovery.

### 14.6 Measurement must capture timing as well as shape

**Job:** Specify what could support or disfavor the paired failure families.

Candidate observations include perturbation response, joint or segment kinematics, contact, muscle activation, force variability, phase timing, sensory discrimination, repeated-task drift, fatigue, recovery, and performance under task variation. No single measure proves the model. The required set depends on whether the claim concerns relation, timing, exposure, or response.

Disfavor instability when the relation remains controlled across relevant perturbations. Disfavor discoordination when timing differences do not predict exposure or task outcome. Disfavor the feedback loop when proposed sensory and bracing changes do not covary in the predicted direction or when a competing model explains them better.

**Exit:** Once a protective strategy persists, it becomes a compensation whose effects must be followed through time—Chapter 15's job.

### Conclusion and handoff

Revisit the opening and show at least three legitimate interpretations: task-specific instability, altered timing among capable participants, or useful protective correction. The endpoint alone cannot choose among them. Repetition and chronology determine whether the corrective work becomes an important exposure.

Chapter 15 inherits the successful solution rather than the failure label. Bracing, changed timing, or an alternate route may preserve something valuable. The next question is what happens when that solution becomes the baseline for subsequent tasks.

## Model obligations

### Instability/discoordination paired-state model

1. **Object represented:** Loss and recovery of a required relation versus altered timing among capable participants.
2. **System boundary and scale:** One joint, segment, coordinated region, or task phase.
3. **Variables and states:** Kinematics, contact, force, activation, timing, perturbation, correction, and outcome.
4. **Assumptions:** Defined task tolerance, measurement reliability, and a comparison adequate to natural variability.
5. **What the model explains:** Why similar endpoints may arise from distinct control problems.
6. **What it cannot establish:** Tissue damage, symptom origin, fear, sensory deficit, diagnosis, or treatment.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Revise if relation and timing cannot be operationally separated or if a continuous control model is better.
8. **Registry action:** to create.

### Repeated-correction exposure model

1. **Object represented:** Time series of perturbation, corrective action, and return.
2. **System boundary and scale:** Defined task and selected segment or interface.
3. **Variables and states:** Event magnitude, rate, frequency, work, activation, local strain or contact, and recovery.
4. **Assumptions:** The selected event is reliably identified and relevant to the proposed exposure.
5. **What the model explains:** Why final posture and peak movement can miss cumulative corrective demand.
6. **What it cannot establish:** Microtrauma, fatigue, or degeneration without separate evidence.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Reject when corrections do not accumulate or differ meaningfully from normal variability.
8. **Registry action:** to create; reuse `BGB-M-0003` as the temporal descriptor.

### Sensory-control/bracing loop

1. **Object represented:** Candidate reciprocal relation among state, sensory information, protection, and mechanical options.
2. **System boundary and scale:** Local or regional control loop; no body-wide autonomic generalization.
3. **Variables and states:** Position/motion, sensory measures, pain/threat context, activation, stiffness, effort, contact, and task outcome.
4. **Assumptions:** Direction and time order are not established by correlation.
5. **What the model explains:** How a useful protection can become self-maintaining or alter future options.
6. **What it cannot establish:** A universal cause of bracing, pain, dysautonomia, or chronic disease.
7. **Failure or replacement condition:** Revise if arrows lack independent support or intervention on one term does not produce predicted changes.
8. **Registry action:** to create.

## Principal objections and responses

### “Instability is too vague to function as a taxonomy.”

The chapter narrows it to failure to maintain or recover a named relationship under a defined task and perturbation. If that relation cannot be specified, the label is not used.

### “Discoordination is just visual judgment.”

Not here. It requires timing or sequence observations and a relation to a defined exposure or task outcome. Atypical appearance alone is not evidence.

### “Bracing can be exactly what stability requires.”

Agreed. The model treats bracing first as a potentially useful solution. Cost, reduced options, or feedback persistence are conditional claims requiring their own measurements.

### “The feedback loop can explain anything after the fact.”

Each arrow has an independent observation, competing direction, and disfavoring result. If those cannot be specified, the loop remains a research prompt rather than an explanation.
