# Provisional Multi-Book Architecture

**Status:** Draft 0.1

**Date:** 2026-07-09

**Purpose:** Provide a provisional series architecture for reorganizing the repository as a source archive and developing a new sequence of books from the aligned project model.

## 1. Editorial premise

The project contains more than one book-sized argument. Its broadest and most defensible medical foundation, its preferred-configuration hypothesis, its TCM and embodied-tradition correspondences, its breath and whole-system geometry, and its practical applications should not all be required to succeed or fail together.

The proposed architecture therefore develops the work in layers:

1. establish the body as a living biomechanical system and load routing as a useful dimension of clinical reasoning;
2. develop the preferred global configuration and posterior skeletal-routing hypothesis;
3. formalize the three signed dimensions and their correspondence with TCM and other embodied traditions;
4. investigate breath, pressure, phase, torque, and whole-system geometry;
5. translate the mature model into assessment, teaching, and practice.

Each layer may refer forward to the larger program, but it should depend only on the layers before it. A reader may accept the first book without accepting the preferred-configuration hypothesis, and may accept the mechanical hypothesis without accepting the traditional correspondence or later geometric models.

The five books below are editorial containers, not a commitment to five published volumes. Later containers may be merged, split, reordered, or retained as research notebooks depending on how much evidence, formal clarity, and useful prose they ultimately support.

## 2. Series at a glance

The titles are working labels only. Final naming and subtitles should follow the architecture rather than determine it.

| Book | Working label | Primary job | Central proposition | Relationship to the wider model |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | **Medical Foundations** | Establish the broadly useful medical and biomechanical foundation | The body must manage forces, moments, pressures, deformation, and accumulated mechanical demand; clinical reasoning improves when load history, distribution, and body-wide routing are considered alongside biological, psychological, and social factors. | Does not depend on a unique preferred configuration, TCM mapping, breath geometry, or practice system. |
| II | **Preferred Configuration** *(possible title: The Back Goes Backwards)* | State and develop the project's distinctive mechanical hypothesis | Human anatomy may have a preferred body-wide organization characterized by coherent downward, outward, externally rotational, and posteriorly supported load routing. | Makes the all-Yang configuration load-bearing as a hypothesis and begins its formal and anatomical evaluation. |
| III | **The Body's Coordinates** | Develop the signed three-dimensional map and its traditional correspondences | Axial, rotational, and lateral/radial organization may provide a mechanical coordinate system that corresponds meaningfully with Yin/Yang directions, the Six Divisions, channels, and related embodied observations. | Treats TCM and other traditions as sources of structured observation and hypotheses, not as proof of the mechanical model. |
| IV | **The Breath-Structured Body** | Develop the dynamic and higher-specificity whole-system model | Breath, pressure, phase, torque, tissue continuity, and cyclic geometry may help explain how body-wide configurations are established, maintained, and changed. | Houses the torus, paired-loop, tensegrity, hyoid, metastability, and topology research programs without forcing one representation too early. |
| V | **Embodiment and Practice** | Translate the matured framework into responsible use | A sufficiently developed model may inform observation, teaching, movement, training, rehabilitation, and future clinical practice. | Becomes prescriptive only after the preceding theory, evidence, and safety questions have been developed and reviewed. |

## 3. Book I — Medical Foundations

### Editorial role

This is the first and immediate book. It should be the least objectionable, most useful, and most independently complete expression of the project.

Its job is not to prove the entire research program. Its job is to make a strong case that mechanical organization belongs in medical reasoning at a level more integrated than isolated ergonomics, injury mechanics, or downstream rehabilitation.

### Provisional thesis

> The human body is a living biomechanical system. Its tissues and structures continuously receive, distribute, transform, transmit, and dissipate forces and moments while managing pressure and deformation over time. Health and function require these demands to remain within viable, context-dependent ranges and to retain workable routes through the system. Clinical reasoning improves when load history, load distribution, and body-wide load routing are considered alongside biological, psychological, and social factors.

### Reader promise

By the end of the book, a clinically or mechanically literate reader should be able to:

- reason about the body in terms of force, moment, stress, strain, pressure, capacity, time, and recovery;
- distinguish external demand, structural routing, local tissue response, and experienced symptoms;
- recognize concentration, constraint, instability, and compensation as recurring mechanical patterns;
- see how a whole-system mechanical lens can change questions and interpretations without replacing existing medicine;
- understand the broader preferred-configuration program without needing to accept it.

### Provisional structure

#### Part I — A living body under load

**1. The mechanical dimension of life**

Introduce the body as an active, materially embodied system. Establish that biological regulation occurs within mechanical constraints and situate mechanical organization alongside the other determinants of health and disease.

**2. A working language of load**

Define force, moment and torque, pressure, stress, strain, strain rate, deformation, stiffness, compliance, and load path. Explain why “load” is an umbrella term rather than a single scalar, and why magnitude, direction, rate, duration, repetition, and variability matter.

**3. How living structure carries demand**

Introduce a first-order skeletal-primary model while accounting for muscle, tendon, ligament, fascia, cartilage, fluid, pressure-bearing spaces, and active control. Describe tissues by the roles they perform—contractile, tensile, compressive, stabilizing, compliant, containing, buffering, pressure-bearing, and load-sensitive—without assuming one role per tissue.

#### Part II — Routing, capacity, and failure

**4. Load paths through a changing body**

Move from local forces to chains and networks of transmission. Distinguish a route from the direction of force along it, explain force-and-moment transfer across joints and segments, and show why movement changes routing without eliminating structural continuity.

**5. Mechanical homeostasis, capacity, and time**

Develop viable dynamic ranges rather than one fixed posture or set point. Relate current capacity to load history, development, aging, tissue state, recovery, biological regulation, and task. Address adaptation as one possible response among tolerance, compensation, sensitization, injury, remodeling, and failure.

**6. Failure signatures and compensation**

Develop a compact pattern language: concentration, constraint, instability, and compensatory rerouting or buffering. Explain how compensation can be locally useful, globally costly, silent, reversible, persistent, or eventually insufficient.

#### Part III — The mechanical lens in medicine

**7. Where mechanics already lives in medicine**

Begin from common ground: pressure, pumps, ventilation, fixation, decompression, conduit patency, joint loading, and structural support already matter in medical reasoning and intervention. The proposed extension is greater integration across tissues, regions, and time.

**8. What load-routing reasoning adds**

Show the difference between identifying a local lesion and asking how demand arrived there, why it concentrated there, what other structures absorbed it, and how the pattern changed over time. Present the framework as an additional level of causal and contextual modeling.

**9. Degrees of mechanical contribution**

Separate mechanical primary causes, material contributors, symptom modifiers, consequences, and incidental findings. Show how mechanical explanations interact with inflammatory, infectious, genetic, metabolic, neurological, psychological, social, and environmental explanations rather than competing with them as a single alternative.

#### Part IV — Worked patterns

The book should use three or four worked examples rather than an encyclopedic disease list. Each example should trace external and internal demand, geometry, likely load paths, tissue response, time, symptoms, uncertainty, and credible alternative explanations. The final conditions should be chosen during the evidence audit; the present candidates are intentionally provisional.

**10. Geometry and force concentration**

Candidate contexts include scoliosis, age-related changes in spinal geometry, or a joint-degeneration pattern. The example should show how a body-wide change in geometry can relocate stress without making geometry the sole explanation for outcome.

**11. Constraint and loss of space**

Candidate contexts include a nerve or vascular corridor such as thoracic outlet syndrome, a local entrapment, or spinal narrowing. The example should distinguish a mechanically intelligible constraint from certainty about diagnosis, origin, or treatment.

**12. Instability, repeated demand, and compensation**

Candidate contexts include segmental instability, recurrent ankle injury, or another pattern in which control, tissue capacity, and repeated loading interact. The emphasis is the failure signature rather than the disease label.

**13. A mixed chronic presentation**

Use a case such as nonspecific low-back pain or an age-related decline in structural capacity to demonstrate a genuinely multicausal presentation. Show how mechanical history can remain useful when it is neither exclusive nor sufficient.

#### Part V — Integration and research

**14. Mechanical questions in clinical reasoning**

Offer a non-protocol framework for asking about load history, task, geometry, variability, symptom modulation, capacity, and recovery. Explain what observations and measurements could strengthen or weaken a mechanical interpretation.

**15. Prevention, education, and a research program**

Identify implications for professional education, longitudinal observation, prevention research, measurement, imaging interpretation, and interdisciplinary study. End with testable questions and bounded proposals for change.

**16. The broader hypothesis**

Disclose the later program directly: the project hypothesizes a preferred body-wide organization involving coherent downward, outward, external/opening, and posteriorly supported routing. Explain that Books II–V investigate this possibility and that Book I's argument does not depend on it being correct.

### What Book I does not need to settle

- whether the all-Yang state is instantaneous, averaged, dominant under gravity, or an attractor;
- whether the whole body is usefully approximated as orthotropic;
- whether one posterior route is evolutionarily optimal;
- whether the TCM channel map corresponds to measurable mechanical axes;
- whether breath is necessary to establish the preferred configuration;
- whether load-routing regime transitions have a topological formalization;
- how to diagnose or correct an individual configuration;
- which drills, bandhas, breathing practices, or movement systems should be prescribed.

## 4. Book II — Preferred Configuration

### Editorial role

This book develops the project's distinctive anatomical and mechanical hypothesis. “The Back Goes Backwards” is likely more accurate here than as the title of Book I because posterior routing becomes a proposition to investigate rather than an implication the foundational reader must accept in advance.

### Provisional thesis

> At a useful first approximation, the body may possess a preferred global organization in which structural demand is routed through coherent skeletal support and the three principal modes are biased toward downward axial, external/opening rotational, and outward lateral organization. Posterior loading and “the back goes backwards” describe prominent anatomical manifestations of this all-Yang configuration rather than a command that every movement must travel backward.

### Provisional structure

#### Part I — Defining the hypothesis

1. From general load routing to preferred routing
2. Bilateral symmetry, skeletal-primary support, and local orthotropy as first-order assumptions
3. Configuration, movement within a configuration, and load-routing regime transitions
4. Preferred organization versus one correct static posture

#### Part II — The anatomy of posterior support

5. The pelvis as a coupled opening and closing structure
6. Spine, rib cage, and the local longitudinal frame
7. Scapulae, shoulders, arms, and posterior suspension
8. Hips, legs, feet, and transmission to the ground
9. Shape-preserving, tensile, compliant, and pressure-bearing roles across anterior and posterior regions

#### Part III — The three signed modes

10. Axial organization: downward and upward
11. Rotational organization: external/opening and internal/closing
12. Lateral organization: outward and inward
13. Coupling among force, torque, pressure, gravity, and geometry
14. The all-Yang configuration as an instantaneous state, bias, or attractor

#### Part IV — Testing “the back goes backwards”

15. Posterior loading as an observable consequence rather than a primitive coordinate
16. Candidate measures of alignment, stress, strain, force transmission, effort, and capacity
17. Competing explanations and cases in which the hypothesis should not predict an advantage
18. Falsifiable predictions and the research needed to distinguish the model from conventional load-capacity and movement-variability accounts

### Material retained for later books

Book II may name the Yin/Yang signs because they are native to the hypothesis, but the full channel map belongs to Book III. Breath may appear as a modulator, but the breath-driven geometry belongs to Book IV. Corrective drills and individual assessment belong to Book V.

## 5. Book III — The Body's Coordinates

### Editorial role

This book develops the three-dimensional correspondence in its own terms and gives traditional material enough historical and conceptual room to be treated seriously rather than used as decorative validation.

### Provisional thesis

> The body's axial, rotational, and lateral/radial modes can be treated as signed dimensions. The project's proposed mapping to Yin/Yang, the Six Divisions, paired channels, and selected movement traditions may preserve a structured observational account of whole-body organization that can be translated into mechanical hypotheses.

### Core mapping

| Dimension | Yang direction | Yin direction | Yin division and channels | Yang division and channels |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Axial | Downward/caudal | Upward/cranial | Shaoyin — Heart, Kidney | Taiyang — Small Intestine, Bladder |
| Rotational | External/opening | Internal/closing | Taiyin — Lung, Spleen | Yangming — Large Intestine, Stomach |
| Lateral/radial | Outward/external | Inward/internal | Jueyin — Pericardium, Liver | Shaoyang — Sanjiao, Gallbladder |

### Provisional structure

#### Part I — Translation without erasure

1. What traditional maps claim, record, and leave ambiguous
2. Yin and Yang as signed directions in this project
3. Hard and soft as functional polarities rather than literal tissue classes
4. How historical observation, mechanical analogy, and biological mechanism differ

#### Part II — The Six Divisions in three dimensions

5. Axial Shaoyin–Taiyang organization
6. Rotational Taiyin–Yangming organization
7. Lateral Jueyin–Shaoyang organization
8. Hand and foot channels, bilateral pairing, and local versus global direction
9. Comparing the proposed map with classical sources and competing internal notes

#### Part III — Embodied coordinate systems

10. Meridians as candidate routes, regions, relationships, or attention maps
11. Bandhas as dimensional locks or coordination strategies
12. Yoga, martial arts, and other traditions as comparative observations
13. Where energetic language can and cannot be translated mechanically

#### Part IV — A testable correspondence

14. Anatomical candidates for each division and direction
15. Measurements that could support, refine, or reject the mapping
16. Alternative mappings and failure conditions
17. A research program for textual scholarship, anatomy, biomechanics, and embodied practice

## 6. Book IV — The Breath-Structured Body

### Editorial role

This book holds the dynamic synthesis. It can compare several mathematical and visual representations without assuming in advance that the torus, tensegrity, orthotropic, shell, network, field, or rod model is the one final ontology of the body.

### Provisional thesis

> Breathing is a cyclic mechanical event involving pressure, volume, tissue tension, skeletal motion, muscular activity, and phase. It may participate in the establishment and transition of whole-body load-routing configurations. A higher-order model should distinguish respiratory phase, the route of breath-related action, force and torque direction, pressure propagation, tissue response, and attention rather than treating them as one phenomenon.

### Provisional structure

#### Part I — The mechanics of a breathing structure

1. Pressure, volume, flow, force, and deformation
2. Rib cage, diaphragm, abdomen, pelvis, spine, airway, and suspended structures
3. Inhalation and exhalation as phases rather than fixed Yin/Yang conclusions
4. Breath route versus force vector, pressure gradient, tissue motion, and directed attention

#### Part II — Coupling breath to configuration

5. Axial, rotational, and lateral changes across a breath cycle
6. Reciprocal and synergistic coupling across paired sides
7. Breath as modulator, organizer, or necessary driver: competing hypotheses
8. The hyoid as an anatomical node, not a fourth spatial dimension
9. Bandhas, pressure management, and phase coordination

#### Part III — Candidate whole-system geometries

10. Tensegrity as an illustrative and limited model
11. Local orthotropic frames and curved anatomical coordinates
12. Tall-torus, shell/core, and paired-track representations
13. Loop torque, anterior/posterior tracks, and left/right tracks
14. Intrinsic longitudinal organization and the external gravitational axis

#### Part IV — Persistence and transition

15. Path dependence, hysteresis, metastability, and energy barriers
16. Load-path networks, zero crossings, separatrices, and reconnection
17. When topology is literal, restricted, or only metaphorical
18. Configuration transitions across breath, gait, movement, and longer-term structural change

#### Part V — Formal and experimental program

19. Choosing state variables and scales
20. Candidate mathematical models and their non-equivalence
21. Imaging, pressure, motion, force, and physiological measurements
22. Predictions that distinguish the models

## 7. Book V — Embodiment and Practice

### Editorial role

This is a conditional practice and translation volume. It should be developed with explicit clinical, safety, and professional-scope review, including the planned medical coauthor pass. It may ultimately become more than one artifact: a general-reader practice book, a professional manual, and/or a research protocol collection.

### Provisional thesis

> As the mechanical and coordinate models are developed and tested, they can guide more coherent observation, cueing, movement exploration, training progression, rehabilitation hypotheses, and research, with applications individualized and bounded by professional scope.

### Provisional structure

#### Part I — From model to observation

1. What can be observed directly and what must be inferred
2. Load history, task, capacity, variability, and recovery
3. Configuration, compensation, and symptom modulation
4. Screening, measurement, uncertainty, and referral

#### Part II — Learning the three modes

5. Axial organization in ordinary movement
6. External/opening rotational organization
7. Outward lateral organization
8. Integrating posterior support with anterior function
9. Moving through Yin and Yang while retaining a viable global organization

#### Part III — Breath and coordination

10. Breath awareness before breath control
11. Pressure, phase, and whole-body coordination
12. Bandhas and traditional methods in context
13. Progressions, regressions, contraindications, and stopping rules

#### Part IV — Teaching and professional use

14. Language, metaphors, tactile feedback, and visual models
15. Movement education, yoga, martial arts, athletics, and rehabilitation
16. Individual variation, age, injury history, disability, and disease
17. Case documentation without turning the model into a diagnosis

#### Part V — Testing practice

18. Single-subject observation and structured self-experimentation
19. Case series and comparative interventions
20. Outcome selection, adverse events, and null results
21. Updating or abandoning practices when the model fails

## 9. Development order

The immediate sequence should be:

1. revise the Book I contract around the thesis and independence rules in this outline;
2. perform a source-disposition audit for Book I at the subsection level;
3. construct a Book I claim-and-evidence map;
4. select and research the three or four worked examples;
5. produce a detailed Book I chapter brief and only then begin new prose;
6. maintain Books II–V as inclusive future-work maps while Book I is developed;
7. return to the later books in dependency order, revising this architecture as the models mature.

## 10. Architecture decisions still open

- Whether the public series contains three, four, or five books.
- Whether Books II and III are independent volumes or one book with mechanical and traditional halves.
- Whether Book IV becomes a general-audience synthesis, a technical monograph, or several research papers.
- Whether Book V becomes a single practice book or separate public, professional, and research materials.
- Whether “The Back Goes Backwards” is the title of Book II, the series, or only the name of the broader project.
- How much historical material belongs in Book III versus a separate scholarly companion.
- Which later volume should contain the deepest mathematical formalization.
- Which existing examples survive evidence review and which are replaced.

These decisions do not block Book I. They should remain open until the foundational manuscript and the higher-specificity models reveal their actual size and strength.
