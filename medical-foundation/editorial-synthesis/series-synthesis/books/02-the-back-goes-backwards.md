# *The Back Goes Backwards*, Book II: The Back Goes Backwards

**Status:** Provisional detailed outline for structural review

## Editorial role

Book II makes the project's distinctive anatomical and mechanical hypothesis central. It is sequential and assumes Book I has been read. It inherits Book I's language of load, hard and soft roles, pressure-mediated support, posterior anatomy, and adverse exposure by reference, then asks whether human structure has a preferred body-wide organization and whether posterior participation is one observable manifestation of it. Brief seam recaps should orient the reader without rebuilding the foundation.

The preferred configuration remains a research hypothesis at this stage. The outline preserves its strongest coherent form, its anatomical and traditional motivations, its possible applications, and the observations that could support, refine, delimit, or disfavor it.

## Provisional thesis

> At a useful first approximation, human structure may possess a preferred body-wide organization—one that manages mechanical demand with greater whole-system efficiency through coherent skeletal, tensile, active, and pressure-mediated support; preserves the yielding freedom of soft-role structures; and biases three generalized modes toward their Yang signs: downward axial resolution, external or opening rotation, and outward expansion. Posterior support—and “the back goes backwards”—may be its clearest anatomical manifestation rather than a command that every movement or force travel backward.

## Book introduction and narrative

### The opening problem

Book I established that a living body must continuously carry and redistribute mechanical demand. It also established the posterior chain as a substantial anatomical support system and introduced hard and soft as changing mechanical roles. None of those claims requires one ideal posture or one globally preferred load path. Book II begins where that completed foundation leaves an open question: among the many configurations through which a person can stand, move, absorb force, and recover, does human anatomy favor a more coherent body-wide organization?

The question is stronger than asking whether a particular movement is possible or whether posterior structures are active. It proposes that axial, expansive, and opening/rotational modes may be signed and coupled across regions; that one combined organization may more efficiently establish a capable skeletal, tensile, active, and pressure-mediated route; and that posterior support may be one anatomical consequence. The book develops this possibility in its own terms before asking whether observation and measurement justify it.

### What is inherited and what is new

The argument inherits five pieces of ground from Book I: external demand becomes local exposure through configuration-dependent paths; hard and soft name functional roles rather than tissue classes; yielding structures can carry substantial load while still needing freedom to conform, glide, contain, and recover; the posterior chain is anatomically real and mechanically important in many tasks; and mechanical meaning remains conditional on task, dose, capacity, biology, history, and context.

Book II adds a separable proposition stack. First, skeletal-primary routing and bilateral reference symmetry provide useful first-order assumptions at whole-body scale, while orthotropic and other constitutive models remain optional regional approximations. Second, regional organization may be reduced to axial, expansive, and opening/rotational modes with two proposed signs each. Third, the Yang signs—downward axial resolution, external or opening rotation, and outward expansion—may participate in a coherent global organization. Fourth, posterior participation may follow as a necessary but insufficient manifestation within the eventual domain of that organization. A finding may support one proposition without establishing the others, and each must retain its own alternatives and tests.

### What “preferred” is allowed to mean

“Preferred” names the hypothesized ideal—or high-efficiency region—of a real but not-yet-formalized whole-system mechanical relation. Stability, resilience, continuity of skeletal and pressure-mediated support, strain distribution, freedom of soft-role structures, active cost, reserve, recovery, developmental typicality, and return tendency are candidate terms, constraints, proxies, or manifestations of departure from that ideal rather than rival definitions of preference. The relevant optimum may depend on anatomy, task, phase, environment, and available capacity and may appear as a state, trajectory, regime, or attractor rather than one pose.

Ordinary Yin-directed movement remains necessary. A step, reach, breath, recovery, or perturbation can pass through both signs while the system retains a longer-term bias or return tendency. Likewise, a person may be well adapted to a configuration that is not globally preferred by the model, and a proposed preferred organization may offer no advantage for a particular body, task, life stage, or outcome.

### The recurring derivation-and-testing chain

The same chain organizes the book:

> inherited load-routing foundation → chosen system and local frame → first-order structural assumptions → signed regional organization → coupling across modes and regions → candidate global state or bias → possible posterior manifestation → predicted mechanical consequence → measurement and comparison with alternatives

This chain prevents the title from proving itself. Visible posterior participation cannot by itself establish an all-Yang configuration, and an all-Yang configuration cannot be inferred simply by renaming posterior anatomy. The account must show how signed geometry changes contact, force, moment, pressure, tensile relations, and load partition; it must also identify posterior loading without the preferred state and preferred organization without an obvious backward visual signature.

### The intended ending

Book II succeeds if it turns an authorial intuition into a clearer and more discriminating research object. The conclusion need not announce one immutable configuration. It may support a task-specific bias, divide the hypothesis into regional forms, preserve an attractor interpretation for further work, or identify observations that would disfavor the whole proposal. What must emerge is an explicit account of what the model claims, what posterior loading contributes to it, what conventional explanations can already explain, and what future evidence could genuinely change the answer.

Book III then takes up a different question: whether the mechanical signs developed here map meaningfully to Yin/Yang, the Six Divisions, paired channels, bandhas, and other embodied traditions. Book II may use Yin and Yang as native names for its proposed signs, but neither its anatomy nor its conclusions depend on that later correspondence being historically or mechanically validated.

## Part I — Defining the hypothesis

**Part introduction.** Book I established a general mechanical foundation: living bodies carry material demand; that demand can be described through force, moment, pressure, stress, strain, dose, and capacity; different structures can perform relatively hard or soft mechanical roles; and changing configurations make some load paths available while narrowing or redistributing others. It also established the posterior chain as a substantial anatomical and functional system. None of those propositions requires one preferred route, one ideal posture, or one signed body-wide organization. Part I of Book II begins where that foundation ends and asks the stronger question directly: does human structure have a preferred global routing regime, and can that preference be expressed through three coupled generalized mechanical modes?

**Part arc.** Chapter 1 separates the inherited foundation from the new claim stack and defines preferred through a provisional whole-system efficiency criterion. Chapter 2 separates local spatial frames from generalized modes, conjugate loads, and organizational signs. Chapter 3 states the deliberately simplified structural assumptions that make a first model possible and places orthotropic and other constitutive descriptions among optional regional models. Chapter 4 defines configuration, continuity, and the possibility of a transition between routing regimes. Chapter 5 then prevents the hypothesis from collapsing into one correct static posture by placing efficiency inside task, phase, variability, and time.

**Part outcome.** By the end of Part I, the reader should be able to state exactly which ideas Book II inherits, which it newly proposes, how spatial frames differ from generalized mechanical modes, which simplifying assumptions its first model uses, and how a preferred organization could remain dynamic. The reader should also be able to identify observations that would refine or disfavor each claim without first accepting the later anatomical derivation.

**Chapter summary.** Book II begins from the established usefulness of tracing mechanical demand and advances a stronger possibility: human anatomy may support some body-wide routing organizations more efficiently than others. Skeletal-primary support, the hard/soft division of labor, an all-Yang organizational bias, and posterior loading are treated as related but separable propositions. “Preferred” provisionally names a real whole-system mechanical efficiency relation whose variables and domain remain to be formalized; the familiar language of stability, strain distribution, cost, reserve, resilience, and recovery supplies candidate terms and observable consequences rather than unrelated definitions.

**Chapter introduction.** A load path can be described without declaring it optimal. Book I used that neutral stance to follow how demand is distributed, how local exposure arises, and how compensation changes the next demand. This chapter now asks whether the neutral framework contains a further anatomical pattern: across changing tasks, might there be an organization toward which the body tends, in which structural demand is resolved coherently and yielding structures retain greater freedom to conform, glide, expand, and recover?

#### 1.1 What Book I establishes

The inherited foundation is substantial but bounded. A living body has a mechanical history; external demand becomes tissue exposure through configuration and routing; dose and capacity shape consequence; and hard and soft name task- and scale-dependent roles rather than fixed tissue identities. Posterior structures form a major support and transfer system, but their existence and importance do not by themselves establish a preferred global route.

#### 1.2 The stronger question introduced by Book II

Book II asks whether human anatomy has a preferred routing regime rather than merely many possible paths. In its strongest provisional form, the hypothesis proposes that skeletal, tensile, active, compliant, and pressure-bearing structures can enter a coherent global organization with a directional bias. The claim concerns an organization of demand across a changing body, not a command that every force, tissue, or movement follow one literal line.

#### 1.3 Four propositions that must remain separable

The first proposition is that, at a useful whole-body approximation, gross structural demand is primarily resolved through the skeleton and the structures that organize skeletal contact. The second is that this allows a productive hard/soft division of labor in which relatively form-receiving structures remain more available to yield. The third is that the preferred organization may bias three generalized modes toward downward axial resolution, external or opening rotation, and outward expansion—an all-Yang combination in the project's present vocabulary. The fourth is the working expectation that mechanically meaningful posterior participation is necessary within a fully expressed version of that organization but insufficient to establish it by itself. Evidence for any one proposition does not automatically establish the others.

#### 1.4 Preferred as whole-system mechanical efficiency

Preferred provisionally names the organization that best satisfies a real but not-yet-formalized whole-system mechanical efficiency criterion under specified anatomical, task, environmental, and temporal constraints. Lower local strain, broader load distribution, continuity of skeletal and pressure-mediated support, preserved soft-role excursion, stability, active or metabolic cost, reserve, recovery, resilience, developmental typicality, statistical frequency, and return tendency are possible terms, constraints, proxies, or signatures of departure from that optimum. They may trade off or matter at different scales; the eventual model must say how they are weighted rather than treating any one familiar outcome as the definition.

#### 1.5 Preference requires a defined comparison

Every preferred claim needs a system boundary, task, timescale, state, comparator, and outcome. A regional organization may be advantageous during quiet stance yet irrelevant during swimming, useful during one phase of gait yet not another, or efficient for a particular morphology while costly for another. Preference cannot be inferred from visual familiarity alone; it must be expressed as a difference between specified alternatives.

#### 1.6 Alternatives and predicted non-advantages

Conventional explanations based on strength, skill, morphology, tissue capacity, training history, pain, fatigue, environment, and ordinary movement variability may account for an observation without a preferred global regime. Asymmetry, internal rotation, upward movement, or inward organization may be adaptive or required in a particular phase. The model becomes more credible when it predicts where its proposed organization offers no advantage, where another route should dominate, and where individual anatomy changes the comparison.

#### 1.7 A claim ladder for the rest of the book

The investigation proceeds from definitions to assumptions, from assumptions to regional anatomy, from anatomy to three signed modes, and from those modes to the posterior consequence. Each step carries its own burden: the coordinate system must be coherent, regional mappings must be anatomically defensible, proposed coupling must be mechanically consistent, and the combined model must generate observations beyond its component descriptions. This ladder allows a partial result to survive even if a stronger body-wide claim is revised.

**Chapter conclusion.** Preferred routing is a family of testable propositions rather than one indivisible assertion. Book I supplies the neutral mechanics; Book II adds skeletal-primary resolution, a signed directional bias, and posterior manifestation as increasingly specific hypotheses. Chapter 2 now builds the coordinate language needed to state those hypotheses without confusing movement, force, material direction, and organizational sign.

**Survival work:** Formalize the candidate whole-system efficiency relation: its terms, weights, constraints, scale, task domain, time horizon, tradeoffs, and observable proxies.

### 2. Spatial frames and generalized mechanical modes

**Chapter summary.** The model requires two related but different descriptions. Spatial frames locate geometry and transform directions across a curved, bilateral body. Three generalized modes reduce recurring patterns of longitudinal organization, outward expansion, and relative opening or closing into coordinates whose associated loads can be compared. Configuration, motion, conjugate load, internal field, and organizational sign remain distinct even when they concern the same mode. Cylindrical and curvilinear frames remain useful visual analogies rather than the ontology of the model.

**Chapter introduction.** Words such as up, out, axial, rotation, and lateral feel self-evident until they are applied across a curved spine, two sides of a pelvis, a rotating limb, and a body moving through space. The same arrow can describe where a point moved, where a force acts, how a tissue is oriented, or how an organizational tendency is signed. This chapter gives each use a place so that a later anatomical observation cannot silently change categories as it becomes a body-wide claim.

#### 2.1 Choose the system and reference configuration first

A coordinate description begins by naming what is being modeled: the whole body, a region, a segment, a joint, or a tissue. It also requires a reference configuration against which position, orientation, deformation, and change are described. A frame that is useful for quiet standing may not be the right frame for a pelvic ring, curved vertebral column, scapula, or foot during gait.

#### 2.2 Global, gravity-relative, anatomical, and local frames

The room supplies a global frame, and gravity supplies a physically important direction within it. Anatomical planes provide a conventional body-relative vocabulary. Local frames follow structures whose axes curve, rotate, or differ between the left and right sides. A body-wide model must state when it uses each frame and how a direction is transformed when it passes from one region to another.

#### 2.3 Spatial frames versus generalized modes

A spatial coordinate locates or parameterizes geometry; an axis defines a reference for direction or rotation; and a vector has magnitude and direction in a chosen frame. A generalized mode instead reduces a recurring relation that may involve many points, vectors, contacts, and structures. The proposed axial, opening/rotational, and expansive/lateral modes are therefore not the three Cartesian axes, the anatomical planes, or three independent forces. Local frames describe where the body and its loads are; generalized modes describe selected ways in which its organization changes.

#### 2.4 Configuration, motion, load, and organizational sign

For each mode `i`, use `q_i` for a configuration coordinate or mode amplitude, `q-dot_i` for its direction and rate of change, `Q_i` for the mechanically conjugate generalized load, and `s_i` for the project's proposed Yin/Yang organizational sign. The virtual-work relation `delta W = sum_i Q_i delta q_i` supplies a compact dimensional check without claiming that one scalar captures a full anatomical field. The organizational sign may depend on configuration, loading, routing, and phase; it is not automatically the sign of `q_i`, `q-dot_i`, or `Q_i`. An externally rotated hip can be stationary, rotating further outward, or moving inward under either internal or external moment, and each statement differs from a whole-body opening bias.

#### 2.5 Bilateral transformations and relational signs

Positive expansion is locally outward on both sides even though the corresponding left and right vectors may oppose one another in a global frame. Positive bilateral opening is likewise relational: mirrored external rotations or an increasing aperture may share one opening sign while using opposite global rotation vectors. Every diagram must therefore declare its side, local frame, generalized coordinate, quantity, and phase. The shared sign describes a bilateral relation, not one universal arrow.

#### 2.6 Position, force, moment, stress, and material direction

Position and orientation describe a state; translation and rotation describe change; force and moment describe interactions; stress describes an internal field; and material directions describe structural organization or constitutive symmetry. Principal-stress directions arise from a particular stress state and need not coincide with anatomical axes, material axes, or generalized modes. Center of mass, line of action, moment arm, and rotational inertia belong to this mechanical account but cannot substitute for one another. One positive hypothesis for later testing is that preferred organization may bring several of these relations into greater coherence—or reduce mechanically costly conflict or shear among them—without requiring the frames to coincide.

#### 2.7 A curvilinear visual grammar

A leading visual model may use a local longitudinal tangent, one or more outward surface normals, and angular relations around region-specific axes. A cylindrical `(z,r,theta)` construction is useful where anatomy approximates it, but it must not make circumferential direction identical to opening or radial distance identical to whole-region expansion. Pelvic and vertebral examples should demonstrate both the convenience and the failure limits of the analogy. Every arrow or arc must identify its frame, quantity, sign, region, side, and phase.

**Chapter conclusion.** Separating spatial frames from generalized modes makes the hypothesis stateable without pretending that anatomy is geometrically simple. Local and global directions can be transformed, while configuration, movement, conjugate load, internal field, material orientation, and organizational sign remain distinct. Chapter 3 now states which structural assumptions the first model adopts and which constitutive descriptions remain optional.

**Survival work:** Produce a consistent coordinate vocabulary and visual grammar that prevents movement, translation, material symmetry, stress direction, and organizational sign from being silently conflated.

### 3. First-order structural assumptions and candidate constitutive models

**Chapter summary.** The first model adopts bilateral reference symmetry, skeletal-primary routing, and functional hard and soft roles as deliberately simplified whole-body assumptions. Orthotropic, transversely isotropic, poroelastic, viscoelastic, active-material, shell, network, contact, and pressure-supported descriptions remain candidate regional models chosen for a particular tissue, scale, and question. Biological inhomogeneity, asymmetry, active control, pressure, time dependence, and individual variation are not errors in the model; they are higher-order structure that may refine or replace its first approximation.

**Chapter introduction.** A useful first model is not a miniature copy of every anatomical detail. It chooses a small number of relationships that may explain a larger pattern, then states where those choices stop working. The assumptions in this chapter are intended to make the preferred-configuration hypothesis analyzable, not to declare the body a passive, homogeneous, perfectly symmetric engineering material.

#### 3.1 The purpose of a first-order model

The first-order model asks how gross mechanical demand is organized across a bilaterally patterned skeleton surrounded and actuated by tissues with different mechanical roles. It temporarily suppresses some complexity so that proposed routes and signs can be defined and compared. An omitted feature must return whenever it changes the prediction, and the model must be replaceable by a richer one rather than protected from contradiction by its simplicity.

#### 3.2 Bilateral symmetry as a reference, not a literal body state

Human anatomy supplies paired limbs, broadly mirrored regions, and a central axis that make bilateral symmetry a reasonable reference geometry. Real bodies contain developmental asymmetry, organ asymmetry, handedness, injuries, learned preferences, and task-specific differences. The reference allows left and right transformations to be defined; it does not make asymmetry pathological or require equal loading at every moment.

#### 3.3 Skeletal-primary routing

At whole-body scale, the skeleton and its articulations provide the clearest relatively shape-preserving route from external contact through the body. Skeletal-primary does not mean bone acts alone or bears every force directly. Muscle, tendon, ligament, fascia, cartilage, pressure, neural control, and environmental support establish, stabilize, redirect, and sometimes replace parts of the route. The assumption is that gross structural demand is most coherently resolved through organized skeletal contact rather than persistently imposed on structures whose function depends on remaining more deformable.

#### 3.4 Hard and soft as functional roles

A hard role preserves or imposes enough geometry to transmit, redirect, or resolve demand at the scale being modeled. A soft role receives form, conforms, glides, expands, contains, cushions, or responds to pressure. Bone is the clearest hard-role example and a vessel a clear soft-role example, yet active muscle, tendon, fascia, a pressurized cavity, or a coordinated assembly may become relatively hard in one direction while remaining soft in another. The assumption concerns division of labor, not permanent tissue identity.

#### 3.5 Candidate regional constitutive approximations

An orthotropic material model has three mutually orthogonal material symmetry directions with potentially different properties along each. Regional bone, tendon, muscle, or organized connective tissue may sometimes justify such an approximation, while other questions may be better served by transversely isotropic, poroelastic, viscoelastic, active-material, shell, network, contact, or pressure-supported models. Orthotropy remains a serious research candidate, not a foundational derivation: its material axes neither generate nor validate the three generalized modes, although later work may ask how they interact with those modes, skeletal geometry, and measured stress fields.

#### 3.6 Higher-order structure that the first model suppresses

Living tissues are heterogeneous, anisotropic at several scales, nonlinear, viscoelastic, poroelastic, actively controlled, remodeling, and state-dependent. Fluids and pressure systems contribute to support; contact conditions change; sensory control and muscle recruitment alter stiffness; and injury, age, disease, training, and fatigue change material response. These features may refine a first-order prediction, dominate a region, or show that orthotropic or skeletal-primary language is inappropriate for the question being asked.

#### 3.7 Domains of validity and alternative models

Every assumption needs a spatial scale, time scale, loading regime, anatomical region, and intended output. Transversely isotropic models may fit some tissues better than orthotropic ones; poroelastic or viscoelastic models may be necessary for time-dependent response; active-material and neuromuscular models may dominate movement; and network, shell, beam, contact, or pressure models may better explain a particular structure. Model comparison is part of the hypothesis rather than a concession outside it.

**Chapter conclusion.** The preferred-configuration program begins with explicit approximations: a bilaterally referenced body, a skeleton-centered gross route, and a changing hard/soft division of labor. Regional constitutive models—including orthotropy where useful—enter only when a tissue, scale, and question call for them. This hierarchy makes the next question possible: what constitutes one mechanical configuration, and what would count as a meaningful change in its routing regime?

**Survival work:** Define the scale and domain of every approximation and compare orthotropic, transversely isotropic, poroelastic, viscoelastic, active-material, and other plausible regional models.

### 4. Configuration, continuity, and routing-regime transitions

**Chapter summary.** Configuration includes geometry, joint and tissue relationships, contact, active forces, pressure, tissue state, stress and strain fields, and the resulting organization of load paths. Ordinary movement can change every visible position while preserving a recognizable routing regime. A routing-regime transition would instead involve a meaningful change in carrier, side, handedness, continuity, principal direction, or hard/soft role; topological language remains an intuition until such changes are formalized through observable criteria.

**Chapter introduction.** Two bodies can occupy similar poses while carrying demand differently, and one body can move through a wide range while preserving a stable division of labor. Configuration must therefore mean more than visible alignment. This chapter develops a state description rich enough to include geometry and internal organization, then asks what kind of discontinuity or reorganization would justify saying that the body has entered a different routing regime.

#### 4.1 Configuration is a combined mechanical state

A configuration contains positions and orientations, but also contact relationships, active tensions, pressure, tissue properties, constraints, and internal stress and strain. It includes which routes are available and how strongly they participate. Because many of these variables are hidden from ordinary observation, a visible pose is evidence about configuration rather than a complete measurement of it.

#### 4.2 Load paths and carrier systems

A load path is an explanation of how demand enters, is transformed, shared, and resolved through a chosen system. The carrier may be skeletal contact, active or passive tension, pressure, external support, or a coordinated combination. Multiple paths can coexist and exchange dominance across a task. Continuity refers to the availability of the successive roles needed for transfer, not to one uninterrupted anatomical cable.

#### 4.3 Ordinary movement within one regime

Movement within a regime can include flexion, extension, rotation, translation, asymmetry, changing pressure, and alternating muscle activity. What persists is not a static geometry but a recognizable organization of carriers and relations. A gait cycle, for example, can reverse local motions and shift demand from side to side while maintaining a broader pattern of support and return.

#### 4.4 Candidate routing-regime transitions

A transition may occur when the dominant carrier changes, demand crosses from one side or region to another in a new way, a route becomes interrupted or bypassed, handedness reverses, a principal direction reorganizes, or a structure changes from a yielding role to a persistent shape-preserving substitute. No one candidate defines the concept universally. Each must be connected to the task and to a measurable change in distribution or exposure.

#### 4.5 Continuity, crossing, and loss of anatomical relation

The motivating intuition is clearest when a large geometrical change alters which structures are positioned to carry one another. A substantial lateral spinal shift, for example, may place a structure that normally relates to one side of the axis across a different supporting region. The important question is not whether an anatomical landmark crossed an imaginary line, but whether force transmission, contact, tension, or compensation reorganized in a way that creates a different continuous path.

#### 4.6 Invariants, order parameters, and return behavior

An invariant would remain stable through ordinary movement within a regime; an order parameter would summarize a feature that changes when the regime changes. Candidate quantities include load partition, dominant carrier, signed multi-region coordination, side-to-side transfer, continuity of a principal route, or persistence of hard/soft roles. Hysteresis, delayed return, or dependence on prior loading may distinguish a durable transition from momentary variation, but these ideas require data rather than metaphor alone.

#### 4.7 What topological language contributes—and does not

Topology motivates attention to continuity and qualitative change that can survive ordinary geometric deformation. The biological proposal does not yet establish a literal topological invariant, singularity, or theorem. Routing-regime transition is the broader working term: it permits kinematic, kinetic, field-based, network, or state-space definitions and can be studied through observable changes without prematurely choosing one final mathematical formalism.

**Chapter conclusion.** A configuration is the organized mechanical state beneath a pose, and a routing-regime transition is a candidate qualitative change in how that state carries demand. This framework preserves the intuition of continuity without overstating its mathematical completion. Chapter 5 now asks how one regime could be preferred while movement, variability, and Yin-directed phases remain necessary parts of ordinary life.

**Survival work:** Identify invariants, order parameters, continuity criteria, and observable transition signatures without requiring a literal topological theorem before the concept can be studied.

### 5. Preferred organization versus one correct static posture

**Chapter summary.** A preferred routing regime need not be one frozen pose, one universal neutral alignment, or an instruction to eliminate Yin-directed movement. Preference may be instantaneous, task-relative, gravity-biased, statistically dominant, cyclic, or attractor-like across movement. Variability, asymmetry, and alternative organizations may remain adaptive, while any proposed advantage must be defined for a particular task, person, state, and timescale.

**Chapter introduction.** The phrase preferred configuration easily collapses into a familiar but weaker claim: there is one correct way to stand, and departures from it are defects. That is not the model developed here. A living body must turn, fold, reach, absorb, yield, reverse, and adapt. If a preference exists, it must be compatible with that repertoire and visible in how the system organizes and recovers across movement rather than in one photograph.

#### 5.1 A routing regime is not a posture command

Posture describes an observable arrangement at a moment, while a routing regime describes how demand is being carried through that arrangement. Similar postures can use different carriers, and different postures can preserve a common organization. The hypothesis therefore cannot be reduced to holding the spine, pelvis, shoulders, or feet at one angle throughout the day.

#### 5.2 Preference can be phase-dependent

Walking, breathing, lifting, throwing, and recovering balance contain phases in which local directions reverse and different tissues take the lead. A preferred organization may appear only during load acceptance, propulsion, support, or return, or it may describe how the phases fit together over a cycle. A Yin-directed motion can be necessary within a cycle whose load-bearing organization has a broader Yang bias.

#### 5.3 Task, gravity, and environment change the comparison

Standing under gravity, swimming, climbing, lying down, carrying an asymmetric object, and using an external support create different boundary conditions. A regime that organizes ground reaction efficiently in gait may have no special status in another task. Environmental constraint, footwear, tools, seating, injury protection, and cultural practice can also make an alternative organization more useful.

#### 5.4 Variability and asymmetry can be adaptive

Healthy function often requires the ability to distribute demand across several strategies rather than repeat one solution. Anatomical asymmetry, handedness, sport specialization, disability, pregnancy, and permanent structural differences may make symmetric or statistically typical behavior inappropriate. Preference should therefore be tested as available organization and return capacity, not enforced sameness.

#### 5.5 Several dynamic representations remain possible

The preferred organization could be a momentary state under a defined load, a positive average bias across time, a recurring phase relation, a basin toward which the system returns after perturbation, or a family of task-specific solutions sharing certain relationships. These representations make different predictions. Book II keeps them available until regional anatomy and measurement can distinguish them.

#### 5.6 Advantages and non-advantages require separate outcomes

Candidate advantages include lower local strain, more skeletal load partition, lower sustained muscle activity, preserved excursion of compliant structures, greater balance reserve, reduced correction, or faster recovery. Improvement in one outcome does not imply improvement in all. The model should also predict tasks, people, tissues, and timescales in which the proposed organization is neutral, costly, inaccessible, or less useful than an alternative.

#### 5.7 A preference that can be disfavored

Evidence would weigh against a proposed preference if clearly defined alternative organizations repeatedly produce equal or better outcomes, if the signed pattern fails to correspond to load distribution, if apparent advantages disappear after accounting for strength, skill, morphology, pain, or training, or if no stable regional mapping can be constructed. Refinement may narrow the domain rather than erase the entire program, but revision rules must be stated before a favored observation is interpreted.

**Chapter conclusion.** The preferred-configuration hypothesis concerns a dynamic organization of demand, not a moralized ideal posture. It allows reversal, asymmetry, and variation while requiring explicit advantages, boundary conditions, and null cases. Part II now turns from definitions to anatomy, beginning with the pelvis as a coupled structure in which axial support, rotational opening and closing, and lateral spread can first be examined together.

**Survival work:** Define candidate advantages and non-advantages across tasks, individuals, and time, including cases where variability, asymmetry, or another organization is adaptive.

**Part summary.** Part I has separated Book II's stronger hypothesis from Book I's general mechanical foundation. Preferred routing now names a provisional whole-system efficiency relation whose candidate terms and consequences can be studied separately. The mechanical grammar distinguishes local and global spatial frames from generalized modes and keeps configuration, movement, conjugate load, stress field, material direction, and organizational sign separate. The scissors analogy makes this coupling visible and connects rotation to stance, gait, axial support, lateral spread, and ground reaction.

**Chapter introduction.** The pelvis is the clearest place to begin because it sits at a mechanical junction. Demand rising from either foot meets body weight and trunk motion; the two sides must cooperate without becoming one immobile block; and the ring must simultaneously support, transmit, contain, and permit motion. A change described casually as the pelvis “opening” may involve several small rotations, translations, tensions, and pressure relationships rather than one simple joint action. This chapter builds the strongest coherent version of that regional intuition before Part III assigns formal signs to it.

#### 6.1 A ring built from coupled parts

Begin with the pelvic ring as an assembly of two innominates, the sacrum, the pubic symphysis, the sacroiliac joints, the hip joints, and the ligamentous and muscular systems that join them. Its apparent solidity is produced by coordinated constraint rather than by a single continuous bone. Loads can enter asymmetrically through one femur, descend through the trunk, or arise from muscular action, yet the assembly must distribute them across both sides. This coupled architecture makes regional organization more informative than the position of any one landmark.

#### 6.2 The scissors analogy and what it is intended to show

Use two scissor blades as a visual analogy for coordinated opening and closing across the paired hemipelves and lower limbs. The useful feature is not the size of the motion but the relationship: movement or torque on one side changes the geometry and available paths of the paired system. “Opening” can therefore name an externally organized relationship even when motion at the sacroiliac joint is small and distributed through hips, pubic symphysis, soft tissues, and stance. The analogy should be drawn with anatomical joints and constraints visible so that it clarifies coupling without replacing anatomy.

#### 6.3 Hip rotation is part of the pelvic pattern

External and internal rotation at the hips provide a readily observable component of the proposed opening and closing modes, but hip angle alone does not define the state of the pelvic ring. Femoral version, acetabular orientation, stance width, knee and foot position, muscular action, and task all alter what the same visible rotation means. The regional model asks whether coordinated external organization can widen and stabilize available support, and whether coordinated internal organization can narrow or redirect it, while allowing either direction to be useful during ordinary movement.

#### 6.4 Sacrum, hemipelves, and longitudinal support

Follow demand between the spine, sacral base, two innominates, and femoral heads. The sacrum acts neither as a passive keystone in every circumstance nor as an isolated mover; it participates in a changing force-and-moment balance established by the whole ring. The preferred-organization hypothesis proposes a relationship between pelvic opening and a viable longitudinal route through the trunk and legs. That proposition requires distinguishing the direction in which demand is resolved from whether the sacrum or pelvis is momentarily translating, tilting, nutating, or counternutating.

#### 6.5 Ground reaction completes the pelvic system

The pelvis cannot be interpreted apart from the surfaces supporting the body. In quiet stance, ground reactions from two feet, gravity, joint contact, and active muscle forces produce a coupled equilibrium. In single-leg stance, the support polygon changes and the hip musculature must manage a substantial frontal-plane moment. These familiar mechanics provide a disciplined entry into the broader hypothesis: an outward or opening organization may be expressed through how the body establishes and shares support, not simply through a wider-looking pose.

#### 6.6 Opening and closing through the phases of gait

Walking requires alternating acceptance, support, propulsion, and swing, with pelvic and femoral rotations changing through the cycle. Both opening and closing are therefore normal. The relevant question is whether those local phases occur within a more persistent organization that reliably receives load, transfers it across the ring, and returns it to the ground. This distinction prevents a preferred direction from becoming a ban on the opposite movement. It also creates measurable questions about phase, timing, symmetry, range, joint moments, and recovery toward a repeatable support state.

#### 6.7 Rotation, lateral spread, and containment

Pelvic rotation is coupled to lateral organization. Changes in the relationship among femoral heads, iliac wings, abdominal wall, pelvic floor, and rib cage can alter the base through which longitudinal demand is carried and the spaces within which yielding tissues conform. The model's stronger intuition is that an opening or externally organized hard-body route can support containment without requiring the contained soft body to become the primary brace. This relationship remains a regional proposition to be compared with pressure, muscular, and conventional stability accounts.

#### 6.8 From an anatomical picture to a testable regional model

Close by identifying what the analogy must eventually predict: relative rotations and translations of the pelvis and femora; force and moment sharing between sides; paths of muscular and ligamentous tension; sacral and spinal coupling; pressure changes; and differences among stance, gait, lifting, and rest. No single marker can stand in for the whole pattern. Part III will ask whether these quantities can be expressed through a consistent rotational sign and how that sign relates to the axial and lateral modes.

**Chapter conclusion.** The pelvis makes the project's central method concrete. A memorable directional pattern can organize a real anatomical question, but the pattern lives in coupled relationships rather than in one joint angle or a large literal opening. The proposed externally organized pelvic mode now has enough anatomical content to be compared with the curving longitudinal support of the spine and rib cage.

Downward routing and upward support, compression and suspension, extension and curve geometry, and posterior tension are treated as related mechanical questions whose exact equivalence remains to be determined.

**Chapter introduction.** “Axial” sounds simple until it is applied to a curved, segmented, moving column. The cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and sacral regions face different directions, meet different constraints, and change shape with breathing and movement. A force directed downward in the room is not everywhere aligned with a vertebra's local axis; an elongated spine may still be highly compressed; and extension can either improve or worsen a particular distribution. This chapter replaces a single vertical line with a sequence of local frames while retaining the intuition that the trunk has a coherent longitudinal organization.

#### 7.1 The spinal axis is local and curving

Define a longitudinal reference that follows the centerline and changing orientation of the vertebral column. At each level, an axial direction can be distinguished from local radial and circumferential directions even though the entire path curves through space. This construction permits comparison across regions without pretending that every vertebra shares one global orientation. It also gives later claims about “down,” “back,” compression, and extension a frame in which they can be interpreted rather than relying on the page or the room.

#### 7.2 A segmented column carries through joints and tissues

Trace a load path through vertebral bodies, discs, facet joints, ligaments, deep segmental muscles, long posterior muscles, abdominal structures, and thoracolumbar fascia. The proportion carried by each route changes with geometry, activity, speed, and external task. The spine's hard role is therefore an organized behavior of multiple structures, not the exclusive action of bone. Likewise, discs and ligaments may deform while still transmitting substantial demand, and active muscle may temporarily make a compliant region more shape-preserving.

#### 7.3 Downward routing is not the same as downward motion

Introduce the proposed axial Yang direction as downward or caudal while separating several phenomena that casual language blends together. Body weight can be routed downward while the head moves upward; ground reaction can act upward while internal demand resolves through a descending skeletal path; a vertebral segment can translate in either direction while experiencing compression or tension. The regional hypothesis concerns the organization and resolution of demand, not a command for every structure to move caudally.

#### 7.4 Upward support and downward resolution can be reciprocal

Standing is commonly experienced and coached as rising or lengthening upward, while the body also transfers weight toward the ground. These descriptions need not be rivals. An upward support response may be the active counterpart of a viable downward route, just as a column supports a structure above by resolving demand below. The chapter keeps both descriptions visible and asks what is being measured in each: position, acceleration, muscle action, contact force, internal stress, pressure, perceived effort, or directional organization.

#### 7.5 Curves, flexion, extension, and available paths

Examine cervical lordosis, thoracic kyphosis, lumbar lordosis, and the way spinal curves change moment arms, contact, stiffness, and shock management. Extension is important to the series because posterior structures participate strongly in upright support, but extension is not automatically decompression or preferred organization. Flexion is not automatically collapse. The useful question is whether a particular curve and movement preserve a coherent path through the task, or concentrate demand in a region whose geometry or capacity is poorly matched to it.

#### 7.6 The ribs make the thoracic frame dynamic

Add ribs, costovertebral joints, sternum, diaphragm, intercostals, abdominal wall, lungs, and pressure changes to the thoracic model. Breathing changes dimensions and stiffness in all directions, while arm and trunk tasks use the rib cage as a moving base. Thoracic organization therefore cannot be inferred from vertebral alignment alone. Rib motion may participate in axial, opening/rotational, and expansive/lateral patterns, and the same breath phase can interact differently with support depending on task, posture, and active control.

#### 7.7 Posterior tension, shock management, and protected spaces

Examine the posterior ligamentous and muscular systems as tension-bearing contributors that guide movement, resist moments, store or dissipate energy, and help maintain the changing geometry around the spinal canal and intervertebral foramina. The proposed posterior preference becomes mechanically interesting when it predicts a capable route rather than simply more posterior muscle activity. A successful arrangement should manage repeated demand while preserving neural and soft-tissue freedom; an unsuccessful one may concentrate strain or require yielding structures to act as persistent restraints.

#### 7.8 What a longitudinal model must distinguish

Gather the candidate observations: vertebral and rib kinematics, curvature, local force direction, joint contact, disc and ligament deformation, muscle activation, pressure, ground reaction, balance, and task performance. Their relationships may reveal a coherent longitudinal mode, but none is its automatic proxy. Part III must formalize local-to-global transformations and decide whether axial sign belongs to force, motion, configuration, dominant route, or a coupled state that includes several of them.

**Chapter conclusion.** The spine and rib cage support a strong longitudinal intuition, but only when axial organization follows their living, curved geometry. Downward routing, upward support, posterior tension, extension, breathing, and protected space can now be studied as connected propositions without being collapsed into one word. The next chapter asks how the highly mobile shoulder girdle can join this axial framework while transmitting demand to and from the arms.

Describe flexion and extension together with tibiofemoral rotation, patellar mechanics, frontal-plane moments, meniscal contact, ligament tension, and muscle action. Treat the foot as an articulated, deformable structure whose arches, joints, fascia, muscles, and contact surface accommodate terrain and reorganize for support and propulsion. Bone geometry, muscle capacity, neural control, balance, and habitual environment develop together. A preferred organization may emerge gradually, be learned through interaction, reflect maturation, or vary across cultures and individuals. Developmental observations can therefore clarify whether the model concerns an inherited anatomical possibility, a common acquired strategy, or an attractor produced by both.

### 11. The hard/soft division of labor across body regions

**Chapter summary.** Across the pelvis, trunk, shoulder girdle, and limbs, capable support depends on a changing division between relatively shape-preserving and relatively form-receiving roles. Hard and soft, posterior and anterior, exterior and interior, deep and peripheral, skeletal and visceral, and Yang and Yin form a family of correspondences without becoming automatic synonyms.

**Chapter introduction.** The compact intuition inherited from Book I—“Load wants to travel through the hard body so that the soft body can remain soft”—now returns at regional scale. #### 11.1 Hard and soft name roles, not permanent materials

Restate the polarity precisely. A hard role is relatively shape-preserving, path-establishing, force-transmitting, or geometrically resolving at the scale and direction under discussion. A soft role is relatively form-receiving and shape-conforming: it yields to neighboring geometry and remains available for glide, containment, expansion, flow, buffering, and local deformation. Bone often occupies the clearest hard role and viscera an obvious soft role, but muscle, tendon, ligament, fascia, cartilage, fluid spaces, and joints can change roles with task and state.

#### 11.2 Skeletal-primary routing is the first-order proposition

State the mechanical hypothesis in its simplest regional form: gross demand is most coherently resolved when the skeletal system and its tensile and active supports establish the principal body-wide route. This is an approximation, not a claim that only bone carries force. Muscles, connective tissues, pressure systems, and contact interfaces create and modify the route. “Primary” concerns organizational responsibility for preserving a viable path while other structures accommodate, refine, and respond to it.

#### 11.3 Posterior and anterior are complementary but not interchangeable

The posterior body contains large, continuous support-and-transfer systems: spinal extensors, trapezius, latissimus dorsi, gluteals, hamstrings, calf–Achilles structures, fasciae, ligaments, and the posterior aspects of the skeleton. Their scale and continuity make posterior support a serious anatomical proposition. Anterior structures remain essential for movement, containment, pressure regulation, braking, and control. The stronger hypothesis is not that the anterior body should be unloaded, but that persistent gross support should not be displaced into anterior or internal structures poorly positioned to provide it.

#### 11.4 Exterior, interior, deep, and peripheral relationships

Develop the proposition that an organized exterior can establish boundaries and paths within which the interior remains mobile and form-receiving. Ribs, pelvis, spine, body wall, and limb girdles surround or border organs, vessels, nerves, and pressure spaces without isolating them mechanically. The related intuition that deep or central structures are soft and peripheral structures are hard is useful in some regions but cannot become a literal rule: the skeleton is deep in many places, deep muscles can establish firm control, superficial fascia can transmit tension, and pressure systems can support from within. The model retains the relational insight—that one layer can establish form for another—while specifying the actual anatomy, direction, and task. When external support is capable, internal tissues can move with breathing, flow, digestion, and ordinary deformation; when support is redirected, a yielding structure may instead be asked to maintain shape or resist a sustained moment.

#### 11.5 Yielding structures need freedom, not absence of load

Clarify the meaning of “remain soft.” Vessels, nerves, viscera, muscle, fascia, cartilage, and fluid-filled spaces are mechanically active and continuously loaded. Their soft role is not zero stress or deformation; it is the capacity to receive form, glide, change volume, transmit pressure, and recover without becoming the persistent substitute for a missing structural route. This distinction explains why a narrowed or displaced soft structure may be understood partly through the continuing hard-body demand that maintains the deformation, not only through the reduced space itself.

#### 11.6 Substitution changes the division of labor

Describe the candidate failure pattern in which a form-receiving tissue, pressure system, or local muscle becomes responsible for holding geometry or arresting a body-wide moment over time. The immediate compensation may preserve function, stability, or comfort and can therefore be intelligent rather than erroneous. Its possible cost is a changed distribution of strain, pressure, excursion, effort, or recovery. The model must distinguish transient useful substitution from a persistent reorganization that creates adverse exposure.

#### 11.7 Yang and Yin organize the polarity without exhausting it

Introduce Yang as the working sign for the proposed downward, external or opening, and outward directions, and Yin for upward, internal or closing, and inward directions. The hard, posterior, exterior, and Yang terms may converge in the preferred configuration, but they refer to different kinds of relationship: mechanical role, anatomical region, spatial relation, and directional sign. Their correspondence may be an identity in one model, a tendency in another, a task-dependent coupling, or a traditional analogy whose translation remains to be tested.

#### 11.8 A regional synthesis ready for formalization

Gather the strongest coherent proposition. Pelvic opening, longitudinal spinal support, posterior shoulder suspension, lower-limb transmission, and adaptable ground contact may participate in a body-wide organization that routes gross demand through capable skeletal, tensile, and active structures while preserving the freedom of yielding tissues. The regional chapters make this proposition anatomically intelligible. Part III must now decide whether a consistent three-mode representation can express it, where the signs change with local frames and phase, and which correspondences survive formal comparison.

**Chapter conclusion.** The hard/soft division of labor gives the regional anatomy a common purpose without forcing every contrast into one category. Posterior, external, skeletal, and Yang organization can be investigated as mutually supporting features of preferred support while remaining separately definable. With that regional picture established, Part III turns from anatomy-first description to the exact meaning of axial, opening/rotational, and expansive/lateral signs and their coupling.

**Survival work:** Formalize correspondence types—identity, tendency, regional association, functional analogy, or task-dependent coupling—and test rather than assume their equivalence.

### 12. Axial organization: downward and upward

**Chapter summary.** The axial generalized mode reduces longitudinal organization or deformation to a configuration coordinate `q_a` referenced to a locally curving skeletal route. Its conjugate load `Q_a` is an axial force or resultant when the coordinate is length-like. The model assigns Yang to downward or caudal resolution and Yin to upward or cranial organization, but the organizational sign `s_a` is not automatically the sign of position, movement, force, compression, or elongation. Downward routing and upward support can therefore describe complementary aspects of one supported event.

**Chapter introduction.** The axial mode appears simple because gravity supplies an obvious vertical direction. In the body, however, the spinal column curves, the limbs branch, the feet meet the ground, the rib cage changes shape, muscles create internal forces, and pressure systems transmit demand in several directions at once. Begin with quiet standing: body weight acts downward, the ground exerts an upward reaction, tissues carry compression and tension, and the person remains upright. The chapter's task is to define which part of that event receives the axial sign and to keep the other mechanical descriptions visible.

#### 12.1 Coordinate and sign convention

Define `q_a` as a reduced coordinate for longitudinal organization or deformation measured relative to a stated reference configuration and local longitudinal tangent. Its conjugate `Q_a` is an axial generalized force or resultant when `q_a` is length-like. Positive or Yang organization is downward or caudal resolution; negative or Yin organization is upward or cranial. The spatial tangent must be transformed as the route curves, while `q_a`, `q-dot_a`, `Q_a`, and `s_a` remain separately reported. A vertebra moving upward may participate in downward load resolution, and a downward-moving segment may be poorly connected to the load-bearing route.

#### 12.2 Regional anatomy

Follow the axial mode from head and cranio-cervical junction through the curving spine, rib cage, sacrum, pelvis, lower limbs, feet, and ground contact. At each region, identify what counts as the local longitudinal direction and how it connects to the next segment. The vertebral frame supplies the clearest image: axial through the local column, radial away from its center, and circumferential around it. The pelvis and feet then show why the intrinsic bodily path cannot simply be equated with the room's vertical axis or with gravity's line of action.

#### 12.3 Movement and action inventory

Separate axial organization from the movements commonly associated with it. Flexion and extension alter spinal geometry; elongation and shortening alter distance; rising and lowering change global position; gait alternates loading and unloading; breathing moves the ribs and changes internal volume. Compression, suspension, recoil, and shock absorption may accompany any of these actions without sharing one sign. Both upward and downward motion are ordinary. The candidate axial organization concerns whether those motions preserve a coherent route through which structural demand can be received, redirected, and resolved.

#### 12.4 Force, moment, pressure, and transmission

Use free-body reasoning to show why standing contains downward weight, upward ground reaction, joint contact, muscular tension, bending moments, shear, and pressure at the same time. “Downward routing” should mean that gross demand is organized toward and through successive supports until it is resolved at contact, not that all forces point downward or that the ground's upward reaction disappears. Joint moments, curved geometry, and off-axis contact can convert nominally axial demand into bending or shear. Pressure in the thorax and abdomen can contribute to support while remaining mechanically distinct from skeletal contact and tensile transmission.

#### 12.5 Posterior support and hard/soft roles

Examine how vertebral geometry, pelvic and lower-limb alignment, active muscle, tendons, ligaments, and posterior tension can cooperate to preserve a shape-resolving axial route. The hard role is whatever maintains enough geometry to transmit and resolve demand; the soft role remains free to yield, conform, glide, expand, and change pressure. Posterior participation may help create upward bodily support precisely by routing demand downward through a capable frame. This is a proposed division of labor, not a claim that posterior tissue is always hard, anterior tissue always soft, or compression should be maximized.

#### 12.6 Analogies and directional language

Compare three bounded analogies: a column carrying compression, a spring storing and returning energy, and a suspended structure stabilized by tension. None alone represents a living spine, but together they explain why “downward load,” “upward support,” “axial elongation,” and “suspension” can describe different aspects of one event. This book uses Yang for downward axial organization. Language that calls extension, lift, or upright support upward remains useful when it is attached to movement or outcome rather than silently substituted for the routing sign. Yin and Yang here name a proposed directional polarity, not fixed substances or a verdict about health.

#### 12.7 Candidate observations

Candidate observations include the relation among head, rib cage, pelvis, feet, and support surface; the preservation or loss of local spinal curves; changes in joint contact and posterior participation; effort required to remain upright; shock management during gait or landing; and compensations such as forward-head organization, rounded shoulders, lumbar flattening, or persistent hip and knee flexion. These patterns may indicate many things besides an axial routing change, including morphology, habit, fatigue, pain behavior, task strategy, or disease. Their value is to generate discriminating measurements, not to function as diagnoses by appearance.

#### 12.8 Alternatives, measurements, and counterexamples

Measure local orientation and motion with segmental kinematics, external demand with force plates and task loads, joint demand with inverse dynamics, active contribution with appropriately interpreted muscle measures, and internal distribution with imaging or computational models where justified. Compare downward-resolution accounts with ordinary balance control, strength, morphology, movement variability, and task-skill explanations. Seek counterexamples: tasks in which upward-directed force, local Yin motion, asymmetry, or reduced posterior participation is efficient and adaptive. The axial hypothesis becomes meaningful only if load resolution can be distinguished from vertical appearance or motion.

**Chapter conclusion.** Axial Yang is a sign assigned to downward resolution through a local load-bearing organization, not a synonym for compression, descent, extension, or posture. Upright support can emerge from that downward resolution while ordinary movement cycles in both directions. Chapter 13 applies the same disciplined template to rotation, where the difference among angular position, motion, torque, regional opening, and global sign becomes even more important.

**Survival work:** Establish sign conventions, local-frame transformations, regional examples, and observations that distinguish downward load resolution from vertical position or movement alone.

### 13. Rotational organization: external or opening and internal or closing

**Chapter summary.** The opening/rotational generalized mode reduces a region-specific angular or relational aperture to `q_o`; its conjugate load `Q_o` is a moment or torque. The model assigns Yang to external or opening organization and Yin to internal or closing organization. Angular configuration, angular motion, torque, and organizational sign remain separate. The pelvis supplies the leading opening-and-closing image, while the spine, shoulders, forearms, hips, knees, and feet show why no one universal axis can define the entire body-wide mode.

**Chapter introduction.** Rotation is often described as if every segment turns around one central pole. Human motion is more complicated: joint axes shift, the pelvis couples to the hips and sacrum, vertebrae rotate with side bending, the scapula moves on the rib cage, forearms pronate and supinate, and the feet translate ground contact into tibial and femoral motion. Begin with walking, where reciprocal rotations coexist with forward progression and alternating support. The chapter asks what it would mean for these local events to participate in an externally opening organization without requiring them all to rotate the same way at the same instant.

#### 13.1 Coordinate and sign convention

Define `q_o` as a region-specific angular coordinate or relational aperture. Its axis, landmarks, reference configuration, and allowable transformations must be stated for the anatomy involved; a cylindrical `theta` is one regional representation, not a universal definition. The conjugate load `Q_o` is a moment or torque. Positive or Yang organization is external or opening; negative or Yin organization is internal or closing. Mirrored left and right external rotations may use opposite global rotation vectors while sharing one bilateral opening sign. A region can exert an external-rotation torque while moving internally, or occupy an externally rotated position while carrying little rotational demand.

#### 13.2 Regional anatomy

Develop a linked inventory across the hemipelves, sacrum, hips, spine, rib cage, scapulae, glenohumeral joints, elbows, radioulnar joints, knees, ankles, and feet. The pelvic “scissors” model describes paired hemipelvic, hip, and lower-limb organization opening externally or closing internally relative to the longitudinal frame; it does not claim that the pelvic ring swings open through a large literal hinge. Shoulder and forearm examples show that similar sign language can arise from different anatomical mechanisms. Each region therefore needs its own axis, allowable motion, contact geometry, and route to neighboring regions.

#### 13.3 Movement and action inventory

Include trunk twist, vertebral rotation, pelvic rotation, hip internal and external rotation, shoulder rotation, scapular motion, pronation and supination, foot progression, pivoting, throwing, reaching, and the reciprocal rotations of gait. Internal and external actions are both indispensable. The question is not whether Yang motion replaces Yin motion, but whether the system preserves a coherent opening bias or return tendency while movement cycles through both. Distinguish a local joint action from coupled opening across the pelvis or shoulder girdle and from a global spiral that emerges through sequencing.

#### 13.4 Force, moment, pressure, and transmission

Trace rotational demand through applied torque, off-center ground reaction, joint moments, muscle moment arms, torsional stiffness, contact friction, and paired forces. A visible twist does not establish how torque is distributed, and an apparent lack of rotation may conceal large opposing moments. During stance and gait, rotation can be generated at the foot, controlled through the leg and hip, exchanged across the pelvis, and continued or countered by the trunk and arms. Internal pressure and changing geometry may modify torsional behavior, but pressure should not be treated as torque without an explicit mechanism.

#### 13.5 Posterior support and hard/soft roles

Ask how skeletal geometry, capsuloligamentous restraint, active muscle, tendon, fascia, and contact cooperate to preserve a rotational route. Posterior structures may transmit or control torque, yet the role cannot be assigned merely by location. A muscle can create rotation, resist it, yield under it, or stiffen a joint so that torque passes elsewhere. A capable hard route preserves usable geometry while soft structures remain available for glide, recoil, volume change, and local adaptation. Rotational “leakage” becomes a testable claim only when the substitute carrier, deformation, and task demand are specified.

#### 13.6 Analogies and directional language

Use the pelvis's opening scissors, a helical spring, and paired spirals as visual aids while stating where each analogy fails. Preserve the sternocleidomastoid and quadriceps as candidate rotational-lock observations: neck extension and rotational tension in upward-facing backbends, and a subtle external femoral spiral associated with quadriceps, pelvic, and lumbar organization in wheel-like extension tasks. These are observation contexts rather than exercises, proofs, or fixed muscle identities. “Lock” names a possible coordination or constraint strategy whose anatomy, torque, timing, and necessity remain to be determined.

#### 13.7 Candidate observations

Candidate observations include pelvic opening and closing under stance, shoulder–hip counter-rotation in gait, trunk rotation during reaching or locomotion, pronation and supination, rotational range and control, asymmetry, and the sequencing of force across regions. A focused case may examine restricted trunk rotation, pelvic rigidity, limited shoulder–hip integration, or repeated lumbar demand in chronic low-back pain. The observation does not establish that rotational deficiency caused pain; it tests whether the proposed organization predicts load partition, effort, variability, or task performance beyond ordinary mobility and strength accounts.

#### 13.8 Alternatives, measurements, and counterexamples

Combine three-dimensional kinematics with external forces, segmental moments, joint-specific axes, muscle activity interpreted in context, and task outcomes. Compare the preferred-opening model with morphology, sport adaptation, handedness, learned skill, pain avoidance, joint pathology, and ordinary mobility or capacity explanations. Look for tasks in which internal or closing organization improves performance, safety, or stability, and for people who function well without the predicted external bias. A successful model must specify when local rotations should be reciprocal, synergistic, phase-dependent, absent, or irrelevant.

**Chapter conclusion.** Rotational Yang is an externally opening organizational sign, not a demand that every joint remain externally rotated. Its plausibility depends on whether locally different rotations and torques can form a coherent, measurable route across tasks. Chapter 14 repeats the analysis for lateral or expansive organization, where outward force, outward motion, abduction, width, and side shift must likewise be kept distinct.

**Survival work:** Separate angular position, rotational motion, torque, coupled regional opening, and body-wide sign, then specify measurements and counterexamples for each.

Yang is outward and Yin is inward. Expansion must be distinguished from left-right translation, width, anatomical abduction or adduction, lateral bending, and outward force. **Chapter introduction.** “Lateral” can mean away from the midline, toward one side, perpendicular to a local surface, or movement in the frontal plane. The project retains lateral as the TCM and historical family name while using expansive to identify the mechanical mode more clearly. Begin with single-leg stance: the center of mass shifts, ground reaction changes, the pelvis and trunk counterbalance, the stance hip produces a moment, the foot adapts, and the rib cage and shoulder girdle reorganize. The chapter asks whether an outward-expansive relation names a measurable feature of support rather than merely a wider-looking posture.

#### 14.1 Coordinate and sign convention

Define `q_e` as a reduced measure of outward-normal expansion or inward contraction for a stated region and reference configuration. Depending on the system it may represent displacement, separation, width, area, volume-related shape change, or another derived amplitude; nonuniform deformation may require several coordinates or a field. Its conjugate `Q_e` is defined through virtual work and may take the form of force, traction, membrane resultant, or a pressure-related resultant. Positive or Yang organization is locally outward on both sides even when the left and right global vectors oppose; negative or Yin organization is inward. Lateral translation, `q_e`, `q-dot_e`, `Q_e`, abduction, side bending, and `s_e` remain distinct.

#### 14.2 Regional anatomy

Examine radial organization at the pelvis and hips, femur and knee, tibia and ankle, foot and arch, rib cage, scapula, shoulder, and upper limb. The pelvis and rib cage show outward expansion around a curving central frame; the limbs show how joint surfaces and segment orientation transform that relation before demand reaches the ground or hand. At the knee and ankle, local anatomy makes a simple “outward is lateral” rule inadequate. Each region needs a defined center, axis, joint relation, and connection to the adjacent rotational and axial modes.

#### 14.3 Movement and action inventory

Include abduction and adduction, side bending, lateral shift, stance-width change, pelvic drop and recovery, rib expansion and recoil, scapular protraction and retraction, foot inversion and eversion, and the side-to-side transfers of gait. These actions can combine inward movement in one region with outward organization elsewhere. Ordinary movement must cross and recross the radial signs. The proposed Yang bias concerns how support spreads and resolves through the load-bearing frame, not a command to widen every joint, abduct every limb, or prevent inward motion.

#### 14.4 Force, moment, pressure, and transmission

Trace lateral demand through ground-reaction location, frontal-plane moments, contact geometry, active force, tensile restraint, pressure, and changes in the base of support. Balance is not simply a position over the feet; it is an ongoing management of moments and acceleration. Outward force can coexist with inward motion, and an outward-looking position can be maintained by inward force. Rib and abdominal pressure may contribute to width and support, while joint moments and muscle forces determine how that organization reaches the pelvis, limbs, scapulae, and ground.

#### 14.5 Posterior support and hard/soft roles

Ask whether outward organization helps capable skeletal, tensile, and active structures preserve width and contact while compliant structures remain free to change shape. Posterior and lateral systems may cooperate, but neither region is automatically Yang or hard. At the hip, knee, ankle, shoulder, or rib cage, a structure may provide a hard role in one direction and a soft role in another. Persistent inward collapse, outward bracing, or excessive stiffness becomes relevant only when it changes load partition, excursion, effort, capacity, or task performance.

#### 14.6 Analogies and directional language

Use radial spokes, an expanding ring, and paired side supports as limited visual aids. Preserve the serratus anterior–latissimus dorsi coupling candidate: in side-support tasks, serratus may anchor the loaded scapula while latissimus forms a tensile bridge between upper limb and pelvis; hip sag, rib collapse, or scapular instability provide possible contrary observations. A movement-action convention may sometimes label abduction and adduction differently from this book's radial sign convention. Those descriptions can coexist only when the coordinate, action, and proposed functional consequence are made explicit; Book II reserves Yang for outward organizational sign.

Candidate observations include stance width, side shift, pelvic control, trunk lean, rib and scapular relations, knee valgus, ankle inversion or eversion behavior, arch deformation, balance recovery, and recurrent ankle instability. A focused case may examine hip-abductor contribution, peroneal and gluteus medius activity, lateral trunk strategy, proprioception, and the distribution of demand after an ankle injury. Valgus, pronation, narrow stance, or inward motion is not inherently pathological. The relevant question is whether the pattern is persistent, capacity-mismatched, costly, or predictive in the stated task.

#### 14.8 Alternatives, measurements, and counterexamples

Measure three-dimensional joint and segment motion, ground-reaction location, center-of-pressure excursion, joint moments, contact, balance performance, fatigue, and task-specific capacity. Compare radial-organization predictions with foot morphology, injury history, strength, sensory control, footwear, surface, sport technique, pain, and ordinary variability. Seek counterexamples in which inward organization is efficient or protective and outward organization adds no advantage. The model must also state whether left and right sides should act synergistically, reciprocally, or differently across phases of gait and other tasks.

**Chapter conclusion.** Lateral or expansive Yang is an outward organizational sign defined through local bilateral relations, not a synonym for abduction, width, side shift, or outward force. Its usefulness depends on whether a reduced expansion coordinate and its conjugate load explain support and transmission more precisely than those existing descriptions. Chapter 15 now reconnects axial, opening, and expansive organization and asks how three signed modes can arise from one coupled mechanical event.

**Survival work:** Define useful regional expansion coordinates, their conjugate loads, local-to-global transformations, higher-resolution field alternatives, and observations that distinguish them from width, abduction, force direction, and persistent organizational bias.

### 15. Coupling among force, torque, pressure, gravity, and geometry

**Chapter summary.** Axial, opening/rotational, and expansive/lateral modes are reduced descriptions of coupled bodily events, not vector components or independent controls. Their relationships arise through constraints, force and moment pairs, gravity, ground reaction, contact, changing moment arms, tissue stiffness, active tension, pressure, geometry, and sequencing across regions. Virtual work supplies a common relation among generalized coordinates and their conjugate loads without claiming that the modes are uncoupled. Spiral continuity, redirection, interruption, and load leakage remain candidate whole-body phenomena, but no equation or visual analogy establishes them by itself.

**Chapter introduction.** Return to walking or lifting after the three modes have been separated. The body translates, rotates, changes width, meets the ground, alters internal pressure, and recruits active support at once. Decomposition makes this event legible, but the components are not independent in the anatomy. A force applied off-axis creates a moment; rotation changes the line of action; pressure changes stiffness and contact; and a change at the foot alters what reaches the pelvis and trunk. The purpose of coupling is to reconstruct these relations without collapsing them into an intuitive equation.

#### 15.1 Generalized modes are reductions, not vector components

Clarify what reduction does. Let `q=(q_a,q_o,q_e)` describe axial, opening, and expansive configuration; let `Q=(Q_a,Q_o,Q_e)` contain their conjugate generalized loads. The relation `delta W = Q_a delta q_a + Q_o delta q_o + Q_e delta q_e` checks how each chosen coordinate exchanges work under the stated constraints. It does not turn the modes into Cartesian components, prove that they are independent, or imply three separate anatomical actuators. One structure can contribute to several modes, and one mode can arise through several structures.

#### 15.2 Force and moment pairs across contact

Develop coupling from ordinary mechanics. Gravity and ground reaction act at different locations; muscle forces have moment arms; joint contact and friction constrain motion; paired forces create bending or torque; and changing geometry changes every resultant. At the segment level, torque is indispensable even when a continuum model represents it through distributed stress. Following these relations from foot to leg, pelvis, spine, rib cage, shoulder, and arm shows how axial support, rotation, and lateral balance can emerge together without requiring a single continuous cable or force vector.

#### 15.3 Pressure, stiffness, changing geometry, and modal coupling

Internal pressure can alter support, shape, contact, and apparent stiffness, but it is not a fourth generalized mode and does not replace skeletal, tensile, or active mechanics. Volume change in the thorax or abdomen may modify `q_e`, rib or pelvic opening may alter `q_o`, and both may change the longitudinal route represented by `q_a`. Geometry can therefore couple opening and expansion without making them identical. Tissue tension can redirect pressure effects, while joint geometry can transform a local pressure or force into motion elsewhere. Distinguish the route along which an action is sensed or coordinated from the direction of force, tension, pressure propagation, tissue motion, or fluid flow.

#### 15.4 Sequencing, spiral continuity, and gait

Use gait, stance transitions, reaching, and lifting to show how timing creates or interrupts a coupled route. Local rotations can alternate while a global spiral remains coherent; outward organization on one side can be paired with inward recovery on the other; and axial resolution can persist through changing limb and trunk geometry. “Spiral continuity” should therefore be framed as a hypothesis about coordinated sequencing and transmission. It must be distinguished from anatomical continuity, simultaneous muscle activation, visual resemblance, and clinically meaningful long-range force transfer.

#### 15.5 Interruption, redirection, leakage, and substitution

Define a coupled interruption as loss or bypass of an available route across modes or regions. Redirection may be adaptive; substitution may preserve the task at greater deformation, activation, or reduced excursion; leakage proposes that demand is resolved through a structure poorly matched to its dose. A changed arch, valgus relation, pelvic rotation, shifted spine, rib collapse, or altered scapular support can serve as candidate examples only when the proposed incoming demand, alternative paths, carrier changes, and local exposure are named. The explanation must allow several paths to coexist.

#### 15.6 Candidate whole-system representations

Compare rigid-segment and free-body models, curved rods, shells, local orthotropic materials, pressure-supported systems, distributed networks, tensegrity analogies, and field representations. Preserve a paired-track or tall-torus possibility as a later, higher-specificity model: anterior/posterior torque relations may contribute to axial and rotational organization; left/right relations may contribute to lateral curvature or expansion; convergence and separation may contribute to opening and closing. These are candidate mechanisms, not established anatomy, and they need not be accepted for the signed coordinate model to be testable.

#### 15.7 Dimensional consistency and formalization

Require every formal relation to define variables, frames, units, boundaries, and assumptions. Angular velocity cannot be added to linear velocity without an explicit length scale and model; a rotation matrix transforms orientation or components but does not prove decompression, improved routing, or a medical effect. Candidate coupling may be expressed through constrained multibody dynamics, stress and strain fields, pressure–volume relations, energy, stiffness, control, or network connectivity. The mathematical representation should be selected because it yields discriminating predictions, not because it resembles the verbal hypothesis.

#### 15.8 Observations, alternatives, and measurements

Combine synchronized motion, external force, pressure, muscle activity, contact, task performance, and time-course measurements where feasible. Test whether a proposed cross-mode relation predicts changes not explained by local anatomy, strength, skill, fatigue, pain behavior, or generic coordination. Include null cases in which the modes vary independently, coupling reverses by task, or the same visible configuration is produced by different internal distributions. The goal is not one maximal model but a set of nested models whose added complexity earns its place through measurement.

**Chapter conclusion.** The three modes become mechanically meaningful only through explicit coupling. Force, torque, pressure, gravity, contact, material behavior, and sequencing can link them, while several formal representations remain viable. Chapter 16 gathers these possibilities into the all-Yang hypothesis and asks exactly what kind of state—or tendency—a preferred sign combination could be.

**Survival work:** Build dimensionally consistent coupled models and identify whether proposed coordination is kinematic, kinetic, pressure-mediated, neuromuscular, material, or mixed.

**Chapter summary.** The all-Yang proposal combines downward axial, externally opening rotational, and outward expansive organization as `s=(s_a,s_o,s_e)=(+,+,+)`. This is a tuple in generalized-mode space, not a physical vector. The notation does not yet decide whether the signs describe every local region, a body-wide relation, a bias within the load-bearing frame, one phase of a cycle, a metastable regime, or an attractor toward which the system returns. These interpretations are preserved as competing models and possible forms through which a whole-system efficiency relation could appear.

**Chapter introduction.** A compact sign vector can create an illusion of precision. The body is spatially extended, locally curved, actively controlled, time-dependent, and often asymmetrical. Three plus signs cannot describe it until the location, scale, averaging interval, state variables, and comparison class are known. Begin by stating the hypothesis directly, then resist resolving its ambiguity rhetorically. The chapter's purpose is to turn “all Yang” from an evocative whole-body intuition into several explicit propositions that can be compared and tested.

#### 16.1 The sign combination and its scope

State the candidate organization as `s=(s_a,s_o,s_e)=(+,+,+)`: axial Yang downward, opening/rotational Yang external or opening, and expansive/lateral Yang outward. The tuple records organizational signs, not magnitudes, Cartesian components, or the sign of every configuration, motion, and load. In general `s_i` cannot be inferred solely from `sign(q_i)`, `sign(q-dot_i)`, or `sign(Q_i)`; it must be defined from the relevant configuration, routing, loading, and phase. The notation also does not establish that posterior, hard, external, extension, and Yang are synonyms.

#### 16.2 Simultaneous local-state interpretation

The strongest literal interpretation assigns positive organization to each mode at every relevant local region during the state. This version is clear but demanding: local frames must be transported along curved and branching anatomy, regional signs must be defined, and ordinary reciprocal behavior must be reconciled with simultaneous positivity. It predicts broad spatial coherence and should be disfavored if capable function routinely requires mixed local signs under the very loading conditions in which the model predicts all Yang. Its clarity makes it useful as one comparison even if it proves too strong.

#### 16.3 Global resultant and load-bearing-bias interpretations

A less literal interpretation allows local signs to differ while the integrated body-wide resultant remains positive. Another assigns the signs specifically to a load-bearing frame: local movement can cycle through both directions while the dominant structural route retains downward, opening, and outward bias. These versions require rules for weighting regions, tissue roles, force or stress magnitude, and task relevance. They may fit ordinary movement better than simultaneous local positivity, but they risk becoming unfalsifiable unless the aggregation and threshold for a positive global bias are defined in advance.

#### 16.4 Phase-dependent and cyclic interpretations

The all-Yang combination may describe one recurring phase within gait, breath, lifting, striking, recovery, or another cycle rather than a continuous state. A complementary Yin phase could gather, recoil, or reposition the system before Yang organization reappears. This preserves the importance of both signs and treats continuity as successful transition rather than static maintenance. It also creates testable questions about timing: which event begins the phase, how signs propagate across regions, whether the sequence is invariant or task-specific, and whether one phase reliably prepares the next.

#### 16.5 Attractor, metastability, and path dependence

An attractor interpretation proposes that perturbation and ordinary motion can move the body away from all Yang while control and mechanics tend to return it toward that organization. A metastable interpretation allows several persistent routing regimes separated by effort, coordination, or energy barriers; history then influences which regime is occupied. “Topological change” may eventually describe reconnection of the inferred load-path network, but a change in anatomy's literal topology is not required. These models need state variables, order parameters, transition criteria, and observations of recovery, hysteresis, or path dependence.

#### 16.6 Posterior manifestation and the division of labor

Ask how an all-Yang organization could produce posterior loading as a visible anatomical consequence. Downward resolution, external opening, and outward spread may together recruit posterior skeletal, tensile, and active support while allowing yielding structures to remain available for shape change. Yet posterior loading can occur without all three signs, and all-Yang organization might sometimes appear without a simple posterior marker. The chapter therefore preserves the title hypothesis while preventing circularity: posterior participation must be derived from regional mechanics and measured independently of the sign assignment.

#### 16.7 Candidate efficiency terms, non-advantages, and counterexamples

Candidate terms or signatures of the efficiency relation include lower localized strain, more coherent skeletal and pressure-mediated partition, preserved soft-role excursion, more stable contact, reduced compensatory effort, improved shock management, greater reserve, and more reliable recovery after perturbation. These may trade off and must not be assumed to improve together. Define tasks and populations in which no advantage is expected, and cases in which asymmetry, inward organization, internal rotation, upward routing, or another regime is adaptive. Pain, morphology, injury, pregnancy, disability, aging, environment, skill, and chosen task may change both the accessible optimum and the relevant outcome.

#### 16.8 Measurements and decisions among the models

Build a measurement program that can discriminate among interpretations: local kinematics and kinetics for simultaneous signs; weighted regional measures for a global resultant; load-partition measures for a load-bearing bias; synchronized time series for phase models; and perturbation, recovery, and hysteresis for attractor or metastability accounts. Predefine competing explanations, null predictions, thresholds, and revision rules. The most useful representation may differ by scale or task, but that conclusion should follow from evidence rather than allowing the hypothesis to change meaning whenever a prediction fails.

**Chapter conclusion.** All Yang is now a family of explicit hypotheses rather than a slogan: simultaneous local state, global resultant, load-bearing bias, phase, metastable regime, or attractor. Each preserves the proposed downward, opening, and outward organization while making different predictions. Part IV can therefore test whether posterior loading is actually a consequence of any of these forms, how the proposal changes across tasks and lives, and what observations would support, delimit, or disfavor it.

**Survival work:** Choose or compare mathematical representations, define the relevant time and spatial scales, and derive observations that distinguish the alternatives.

Their coupling may arise through contact, geometry, moment arms, active and tensile support, pressure, material behavior, and sequencing, but every proposed link requires an explicit model.

Part IV therefore turns from definition to testing. ## Part IV — Testing *The Back Goes Backwards*

**Part introduction.** Parts I–III have defined the preferred-configuration hypothesis, made it concrete in regional anatomy, and formalized its three proposed signed modes. Part IV asks whether that work earns the title. The task is no longer to show that posterior structures exist or that whole-body coordination can be described in three dimensions. #### 17.6 Hard routing and preserved soft-body freedom

Connect the derivation to the compact intuition, “Load wants to travel through the hard body so that the soft body can remain soft.” Ask whether posteriorly supported organization allows gross demand to be resolved through relatively shape-preserving skeletal, tensioned, and active routes while vessels, nerves, viscera, sliding interfaces, compliant tissues, and pressure-bearing spaces retain excursion and the ability to conform. State the strongest bounded proposition: in defined tasks and frames, the proposed all-Yang organization should predict a repeatable redistribution toward coherent posterior skeletal, tensile, and active support, accompanied by specified changes in compliant-structure excursion or load partition. #### 21.3 Anatomical and mechanical predictions

#### 21.4 Observational and longitudinal predictions

Ask whether candidate signs cluster within people and tasks, display repeatable phase or bias structure, change with fatigue and recovery, predict later compensation or reserve, and return after perturbation. Longitudinal data can distinguish a stable trait, trainable strategy, temporary state, consequence of pain, and attractor-like tendency better than a single cross-sectional comparison.

#### 21.5 Experimental task and perturbation studies

Vary support surface, carried load, direction, speed, fatigue, visual input, contact, instruction, and task goal while measuring regional signs and internal consequences. Perturb one proposed mode and test whether the other modes and posterior route change as predicted. Instruction-induced changes require controls for attention, novelty, expectation, and generalized co-contraction.

#### 21.6 Comparative, developmental, and evolutionary studies

Translate evolutionary motivation into discriminating predictions across development, morphology, locomotor tasks, and species. Determine whether the proposed relations follow bipedal mechanics, posterior vertebrate anatomy, ecology, training, or a more general structural principle. Comparative evidence can constrain the hypothesis; a plausible evolutionary story cannot substitute for that comparison.

#### 21.7 Bounded clinical studies

Only after variables are reliable should studies ask whether the model contributes to selected symptoms, function, tissue exposure, or recovery. Designs should separate association, prognosis, mediation, and response to an intervention; include adverse and null outcomes; and compare with appropriate existing care or training. A mechanical classification is not automatically a diagnosis, and a successful manipulation does not establish the model through which it was chosen.

#### 21.8 Falsifiers, thresholds, stopping rules, and handoff

Define in advance what would disfavor each proposition, what effect size or predictive improvement would matter, how inconsistent regional results alter a global claim, and when a state, bias, phase, or attractor representation should be abandoned. Preserve narrowing as a legitimate result but prevent endless relabeling after failure.
