# *The Back Goes Backwards*, Book III: The Body's Coordinates

**Status:** Provisional detailed outline for structural review

## Editorial role

Book III assumes the reader has followed Book I's mechanical foundation and Book II's preferred-configuration hypothesis. It asks whether Book II's axial, opening/rotational, and expansive generalized modes correspond meaningfully to Yin/Yang, the Six Divisions, paired channels, gross limb roles, bandhas, named embodied relationships, and selected movement traditions. It reconstructs traditional maps before translating them and preserves multiple mapping layers where the source program has not yet chosen a final reconciliation.

Traditional observation, authorial correspondence, anatomical analogy, formal mechanics, physiological mechanism, and medical implication remain distinguishable. That distinction permits the book to retain the complete hypothesis generously while making the work required of each layer explicit.

The book develops three compatible scales of the project's core correspondence. At the local scale, individual channels and Six-Division pairs are assigned to the three modes. At the gross scale, the legs and feet are weighted toward axial support, the arms and hands toward expansion, and opening/rotation toward coordination between those limb systems. At the embodied-anchor scale, coordination between KI1 at the foot and PC8 at the palm preserves a direct felt observation that may help investigate the relation between the gross axial and expansive systems. These scales do not automatically prove one another, and none requires every channel in a limb to share that limb's gross role.

## Provisional thesis

Axial, opening/rotational, and expansive organization can be treated as three signed generalized mechanical modes within the project. Yin and Yang, the Six Divisions, paired channels, gross limb roles, bandhas, embodied point relationships, and related traditional maps may preserve structured observations about those modes. This book reconstructs each map on its own terms and then develops explicit mechanical correspondences without requiring traditional and biomechanical vocabularies to collapse into one another.

The proposed correspondences are a research program. Their inclusion preserves the full model for study; it does not imply that historical provenance, anatomical mechanism, physiological consequence, or clinical relevance has already been established. Direct authorial embodied observations remain valuable sources of hypotheses, but they are labeled separately from claims recovered from traditional texts or lineages.

Book III cannot be used retroactively as proof that Book II's mechanical model is correct. It may ask how ordinary breathing and pressure interact with the modes, but breath as a necessary driver and whole-system breath geometry belong to Book IV. Testing, correction, and reader-facing practice belong to Book V.

## Book introduction and narrative

### The opening problem

Books I and II have already done the mechanical work this book is allowed to inherit. Book I established load routing, hard and soft roles, pressure-mediated support, posterior anatomy, adverse exposure, and the importance of task, dose, capacity, and context. Book II asked the stronger question of preferred organization and reduced its proposal to three signed generalized modes: axial, opening/rotational, and expansive. Book III does not need tradition to validate either foundation. It asks a different question: do several traditional and embodied maps preserve observations that become more intelligible when placed beside those modes?

The question is neither whether ancient language secretly contained modern biomechanics nor whether a contemporary mechanical model can replace a tradition. It is whether a disciplined translation can preserve what each source actually contributes: historical description, lineage interpretation, direct experience, anatomical analogy, formal hypothesis, candidate mechanism, or possible implication. The book must be generous enough to retain the full research program and exact enough that similarity, provenance, and evidence do not blur into one another.

### What is inherited and what is new

Book III inherits the generalized-mode grammar `q=(q_a,q_o,q_e)`, its conjugate loads `Q`, organizational signs `s`, local-frame transformations, the all-Yang hypothesis, and Book II's unresolved alternatives among state, bias, phase, regime, and attractor. It also inherits the rule that configuration, movement, load, routing, stress, material direction, and organizational sign may differ at the same moment. Those propositions are not reopened merely to make a traditional map fit.

The new work begins with the Six Divisions and paired channels. The project proposes Shaoyin–Taiyang as axial, Taiyin–Yangming as opening/rotational, and Jueyin–Shaoyang as expansive. It then introduces a different-scale projection in which legs and feet are weighted toward axial support, arms and hands toward expansion, and rotation toward coordination between lower and upper systems. A KI1–PC8 felt relationship provides a named embodied anchor between those gross systems. Subtle and structural bandha maps, central and lateral routes, muscle-pair observations, and breath-related questions enlarge the comparison without being forced into one diagram.

### The three mapping scales

The book's central organizational decision is to distinguish three compatible scales:

1. **Local channel-mode correspondence.** Individual channel families are mapped to axial, opening/rotational, and expansive modes through the Six Divisions. Every limb may therefore contain channels assigned to all three modes.
2. **Gross limb-role projection.** The lower limbs and feet are weighted toward axial support and ground resolution; the upper limbs and hands toward reach, span, and expansion; and opening/rotation toward their coordination. This is a coarse whole-system model, not a reassignment of every channel in a limb.
3. **Embodied-anchor hypothesis.** KI1 at the foot and PC8 at the palm preserve a direct authorial observation about felt lower–upper connection in opening and closing. The observation is a source for investigation, not yet a historical inheritance or validated force path.

The bandha maps and cross-tradition routes are additional correspondence layers. They may intersect the three-scale model, but they are not required to duplicate its units, signs, or anatomical carriers.

### The recurring translation chain

Every major correspondence should move through the same sequence:

> source on its own terms → provenance and authority → reported observation or traditional relation → project translation → generalized-mode hypothesis → candidate anatomy or mechanical carrier → measurable prediction → alternatives and failure conditions → bounded physiological or clinical implication

The chain can stop legitimately at any step. A reliable embodied observation need not already identify its carrier; a historically authentic route need not be an anatomical structure; a useful analogy need not establish a mechanism; and a measured mechanical relation need not establish a medical effect. Conversely, uncertainty at a later step does not erase the earlier observation or its value as hypothesis-generating material.

### The intended ending

Book III succeeds if it turns a rich but overlapping archive into a structured comparative research program. It need not conclude that all traditional maps describe one physical system. It may find that some correspondences are historically unsupported yet mechanically generative, that some embodied relations are repeatable without a known carrier, that one mode maps better than another, or that local, gross, and experiential scales require different representations. The important result is a provenance-clean account of what is being compared and what would change the comparison.

The handoff to Book IV is deliberately narrow. Book III may establish candidate links among modes, pressure, bandhas, routes, and phase, but it does not decide that breath drives the system, that the hyoid is a fourth spatial dimension, or that a torus is the body's final geometry. It supplies the candidate variables, source distinctions, and embodied phenomena that Book IV must compare through mechanical and physiological models.

## Part I — Traditional maps as technical archives

**Part introduction.** Book III begins with method because the material is unusually easy to overread in both directions. Traditional descriptions can be treated as inert metaphor, granted automatic authority, or translated so quickly that a modern proposal is projected backward into a source. Part I instead treats traditions as heterogeneous archives of text, practice, symbol, pedagogy, and interpretation whose contents can be studied at several levels.

**Part arc.** Chapter 1 explains why accumulated embodied observation deserves preservation. Chapter 2 separates text, lineage, translation, practice, and present interpretation. Chapter 3 builds the inference ladder that governs every later comparison. Chapter 4 then introduces Yin/Yang and hard/soft as relational vocabularies whose possible connection is worth developing without making them synonyms.

**Part outcome.** By the end of Part I, the reader should be able to identify who or what is making a claim, at which interpretive level, with what authority, and what additional work would be required to translate it mechanically. The reader should also understand why preserving a traditional or embodied formulation does not require accepting its strongest possible medical or metaphysical interpretation.

### 1. Why preserve traditional observation

**Chapter summary.** Yoga, martial arts, Chinese medicine, and other embodied traditions can be studied as archives of intergenerational observation, experimentation, instruction, and interpretation. Their longevity does not certify every explanation, but neither does unfamiliar vocabulary make their practical distinctions empty. The chapter establishes preservation, comparison, and reconstruction—not automatic endorsement, replacement, or dismissal—as the book's method.

**Chapter introduction.** Human beings investigated stance, breath, pain, fatigue, coordination, attention, and skilled action long before contemporary laboratories could measure them. What survives is not a neutral dataset. It is practice encoded in texts, lineages, rituals, metaphors, point maps, sequences, and correction language. A technical archive approach asks what was observed, how it was transmitted, what problems it addressed, and what later interpreters added.

#### 1.1 An archive rather than an oracle

Define an archive as material whose preservation makes inquiry possible, not as a source that answers every present question. Age, repetition, geographic spread, or lineage authority may justify study while remaining different from anatomical validation or causal evidence. The book should neither ask a tradition to become modern science nor exempt a translated claim from comparison.

#### 1.2 The recurring human problems behind the maps

Identify the practical domains that make comparison plausible: upright support, locomotion, balance, impact, breath, voice, exertion, recovery, pain, internal sensation, skilled force, and coordination across the limbs. These shared problems explain why traditions may contain mechanically relevant distinctions without establishing that their taxonomies describe the same entities.

#### 1.3 Observation preserved through instruction

Examine how a cue, form, channel route, point, bandha, paired quality, or named phase can retain an observation even when its explanatory language changes. Instructional usefulness, repeatability within a lineage, and convergence among practitioners are evidence about a teaching structure; they are not yet evidence for one tissue path or physiological mechanism.

#### 1.4 Variation, loss, and productive contradiction

Treat differences among texts, schools, eras, teachers, and practitioners as part of the archive. A contradiction may reflect changed purpose, scale, translation, audience, or embodied emphasis rather than simple error. Some variants will remain irreconcilable, and preserving them may be more informative than manufacturing one canonical synthesis.

#### 1.5 What contemporary inquiry can ask

Use the archive to generate bounded questions: whether a distinction can be described consistently; whether practitioners can reproduce it; whether anatomy supplies a plausible relation; whether a mechanical variable changes; and whether that change matters beyond the task. Historical, phenomenological, biomechanical, and physiological studies may answer different portions of the same question.

#### 1.6 Authority, appropriation, and interpretive restraint

Name source communities, translations, lineages, and present interpreters rather than treating “tradition” as a single anonymous authority. Avoid attributing the project's generalized-mode map to traditions that did not state it. Ethical use includes acknowledging living practice, recording disagreement, and making clear when the author is developing a new synthesis.

**Chapter conclusion.** Traditional material enters the book because it preserves organized human attention to recurring embodied problems. It enters as evidence with a type and history, not as automatic proof. Chapter 2 therefore builds a source grammar capable of distinguishing the several ways a map can be inherited, practiced, translated, and newly interpreted.

**Survival work:** Establish the provenance, internal diversity, historical setting, and appropriate limits of every traditional source used.

### 2. Reading a traditional map on its own terms

**Chapter summary.** A traditional map can arrive through a dated text, commentary, lineage instruction, symbolic diagram, learned practice, modern clinical school, or present-day synthesis. These sources have different authority and may describe different objects. The chapter gives each a provenance role so the book can preserve tensions among versions rather than forcing a single timeless system.

**Chapter introduction.** “Traditional teaching says” is too coarse for the comparisons this book needs. A phrase may be early or late, central or marginal, descriptive or prescriptive, literal or pedagogical, translated several times, or known mainly through current practice. Before interpreting a route or pairing mechanically, the book must know what form of source it is handling.

#### 2.1 Text, date, genre, and intended use

Record the earliest located formulation, likely date, genre, audience, and practical function. A medical classic, technical manual, philosophical commentary, practice song, diagram, and modern textbook should not be read through the same assumptions. Where dating or authorship is disputed, the uncertainty belongs in the record.

#### 2.2 Lineages and plural traditions

Distinguish a tradition's broad public taxonomy from lineage-specific instruction and a particular teacher's interpretation. Describe internal debates and historical change. “Yoga,” “Taijiquan,” and “Chinese medicine” each name plural fields; similarity within or across them must be shown rather than presumed.

#### 2.3 Translation is an interpretive act

Track original terms, transliteration, literal range, conventional translation, and project usage. Words such as channel, vessel, qi, prana, lock, Yin, Yang, interior, exterior, opening, and closing carry histories that a neat English equivalent may hide. Retain terms where translation would falsely settle their ontology.

#### 2.4 Lived practice and reported experience

Treat learned sensations, skilled distinctions, correction cues, and form-specific relationships as their own evidence stream. Record who reports the observation, under what instruction, in which task and phase, and with what reproducibility. Experience may be precise without being self-interpreting.

#### 2.5 Symbol, analogy, and teaching compression

Ask whether a map is intended to locate anatomy, organize functional relationships, guide attention, sequence action, classify symptoms, encode cosmology, or do several jobs at once. A symbol may compress a practice relation without corresponding point-for-point to a physical structure. Mechanical translation should preserve that possibility.

#### 2.6 Modern reconstruction and project synthesis

Label contemporary anatomy-based readings, present lineage explanations, and the author's own coordinate proposal as later interpretive acts. A productive modern synthesis should state what it borrows, what it changes, and why. It must not be retroactively inserted into a traditional source as the source's hidden original meaning.

#### 2.7 The minimum provenance record

For every important formulation, retain raw wording, source or speaker, date, translation, lineage or context, confidence, interpretive level, known variants, and the project's normalized statement. Link diagrams and embodied observations to the same record. This allows later research to revise the interpretation without losing the source.

**Chapter conclusion.** Traditional maps become more rather than less interesting when their differences remain visible. With sources separated by role and authority, Chapter 3 can establish how an observation or correspondence may advance toward a formal or medical claim—and where that advance must pause.

**Survival work:** Recover representative primary and scholarly sources and distinguish textual claims, lineage claims, and present-day interpretations.

### 3. Observation, analogy, model, mechanism, and implication

Tags domain:research, domain:mechanics, use:foundation, use:research-program · gates DEF, PROV, ALT, AUD

**Chapter summary.** The book uses a durable ladder from reported observation through analogy, organizing model, formal mechanics, candidate mechanism, physiological implication, and clinical application. Each transition changes the claim and its evidence burden. A correspondence may be valuable at one level without supporting the next, while failure at a later level need not erase a well-described earlier observation.

**Chapter introduction.** An informal embodied formulation can be mistaken for a final scientific conclusion, while publication-level caution can be applied so early that it erases the observation that should have generated the research question. This chapter supplies a progressive structure: preserve the observation, label each interpretive step, and increase rigor as a claim moves toward formal, medical, or reader-facing use.

#### 3.1 Reported observation

Begin with the smallest claim: a person, text, lineage, or group reports a repeatable relation, sensation, pattern, or practical distinction. Record task, instruction, phase, context, and observer. At this level the book need not decide whether the experience reflects mechanics, attention, expectation, training, language, or several factors.

#### 3.2 Descriptive analogy

An analogy such as bow, hose, hinge, scissors, track, lock, channel, or torus can preserve shape, sequence, or relationship. State exactly which feature transfers and where the analogy stops. Visual resemblance alone does not establish identical material behavior, but a disciplined analogy can expose variables worth modeling.

#### 3.3 Organizing framework and correspondence

An organizing model classifies observations so that similarities, differences, and missing cases become visible. The Six-Division-to-mode map belongs here before its mechanics are established. Its usefulness may be evaluated through coherence, coverage, economy, and generation of new questions without treating those virtues as causal proof.

#### 3.4 Formal mechanical model

A formal model names system boundaries, coordinates, configurations, loads, constraints, constitutive assumptions, coupling, dimensions, and predicted observables. It can be simplified, but its equations and diagrams must remain mechanically consistent. Book II's generalized modes supply a starting grammar; Book III must still define any traditional overlay separately.

#### 3.5 Candidate anatomical and physiological mechanism

A mechanism identifies carriers and intermediate changes: skeletal contact, muscle action, tendon or fascial tension, pressure, fluid behavior, neural control, sensory attention, perfusion, or another process. Several carriers may realize one observed relationship. Anatomical plausibility narrows possibilities but does not establish magnitude or consequence.

#### 3.6 Physiological, medical, and practical implication

Moving from mechanism to function, symptoms, diagnosis, prognosis, or intervention requires additional evidence and qualified review. A small change in comfort, excursion, or task performance is not equivalent to disease causation. Reader instruction adds feasibility, contraindication, adverse-event, and audience obligations beyond the claim that a relation exists.

#### 3.7 Rules for moving—and not moving—between levels

Require explicit bridge statements, appropriate evidence, alternatives, and disfavoring observations at every transition. Do not let a citation attached to one level support a stronger neighboring claim. Equally, do not demand clinical evidence before retaining a well-labeled historical formulation or authorial observation in an internal research program.

**Chapter conclusion.** The ladder permits both intellectual generosity and later rigor. The rest of Book III can now preserve traditional and embodied material without making it carry more than its current level allows. Chapter 4 applies that method to the project's most important translation pair: Yin/Yang and hard/soft.

**Survival work:** Define each level, add examples of legitimate and illegitimate transitions, and apply the ladder consistently throughout the book.

### 4. Yin and Yang; hard and soft

**Chapter summary.** Yin and Yang are developed as relational, signed, phase-dependent terms rather than fixed substances or moral categories. Book I's hard and soft roles provide a possible mechanical translation: relatively shape-preserving structures can establish and route demand while form-receiving structures yield, conform, contain, slide, and connect. The relation is generative but not an identity; tissues and systems can exchange roles with direction, task, scale, and time.

**Chapter introduction.** “Load wants to travel through the hard body so that the soft body can remain soft” is one of the project's clearest internal formulations. It names a division of mechanical labor, not a claim that soft tissue is unloaded or that all bone is always hard. Book III asks whether the polarity also illuminates Yin and Yang, including an internal or force-line sense of Yin that ordinary compliance language does not fully capture.

#### 4.1 Yin and Yang as relations and phases

Introduce opposition, complementarity, mutual dependence, transformation, interior–exterior relations, and cyclic phase without pretending that one project definition exhausts traditional usage. A structure or action may be Yin relative to one comparator and Yang relative to another. Signed mode language is therefore a deliberate project use that must retain its reference and scale.

#### 4.2 Hard and soft inherited from Book I

Recap only the seam: a hard role relatively preserves or imposes geometry and helps transmit or resolve gross demand; a soft role receives form and changes with surrounding geometry and load. Bone and vessel provide clear limiting examples, while muscle, tendon, fascia, cartilage, pressure-supported cavities, and coordinated assemblies can combine or exchange roles.

#### 4.3 The proposed mechanical correspondence

Develop the hypothesis that Yang often emphasizes outwardly shape-establishing, load-resolving, or structurally expressive organization, while Yin often emphasizes form-receiving, internally connecting, containing, or yielding organization. Keep this at the level of a proposed translation. The later signed modes give Yang and Yin additional directional meanings that cannot be derived from hard and soft alone.

#### 4.4 Internal force lines as a possible Yin expression

Preserve “force line” in its internal sense: a felt or inferred connection through which tension, compression, pressure, contact, active organization, or attention may relate distant regions. Such a connection can coordinate how demand reaches the hard body while remaining yielding and multicarrier. It is not automatically one anatomical cable, external line of action, or conserved resultant.

#### 4.5 Role exchange and mixed structures

Use breath, stance, a loaded tendon, an active muscle, a pressurized cavity, a sliding nerve, and a constrained vessel to show that relative hardness and softness depend on direction, rate, activation, geometry, and timescale. Yin/Yang language must allow transition and reciprocity rather than turning anatomy into permanent binary classes.

#### 4.6 What the correspondence does not establish

Hard does not always mean posterior, Yang, bone, healthy, or loaded; soft does not always mean anterior, Yin, fascia, unhealthy, or unloaded. A Yang-directed mode can be produced through active and compliant tissues, and a Yin phase can be necessary to receive, store, recover, or change direction. The correspondence should clarify relations rather than moralize them.

#### 4.7 Questions generated by the translation

Ask whether preferred organization changes load partition between form-establishing and form-receiving roles; whether internal force-line reports correlate with measurable coordination; whether the same tissue changes role across phases; and whether traditional Yin/Yang distinctions predict anything beyond familiar stiffness, compliance, activation, and geometry. These questions prepare the signed mode map without presupposing it.

**Chapter conclusion.** Hard/soft and Yin/Yang now form a careful bridge rather than a collapsed vocabulary. The bridge preserves the project's internal insight while leaving historical, mechanical, and medical questions open. Part II can therefore state the proposed Six-Division correspondence in the generalized-mode language already earned by Book II.

**Survival work:** Confirm the intended translation, test it against traditional usage and tissue mechanics, and state where the analogy stops.

**Part summary.** Part I has established a method for reading traditions as heterogeneous technical archives. It distinguishes text, lineage, translation, practice, direct experience, and modern synthesis; separates observation, analogy, model, mechanism, implication, and application; and develops Yin/Yang beside hard/soft without making either vocabulary prove the other.

**Part conclusion and transition.** The result is a source-aware translation discipline. Book III can now make a strong and specific proposal without pretending it was already established by history: the axial, opening/rotational, and expansive modes may correspond to the three Yin/Yang Six-Division pairs. Part II states that map mode by mode and resolves the local-frame, bilateral, and phase questions required before any body-wide correspondence can be coherent.

### 5. From mechanical modes to traditional signs

**Chapter summary.** Book III inherits rather than re-derives the reduced generalized-coordinate model: axial `q_a`, opening/rotational `q_o`, and expansive `q_e`, with conjugate loads and separately assigned organizational signs. The chapter re-establishes only the distinctions needed to translate traditional maps. It then defines how a canonical channel grouping and a project-specific mechanical overlay can occupy one comparison without becoming one historical claim.

**Chapter introduction.** A traditional axis map could become deceptively simple if “down,” “turn,” and “out” were treated as three ordinary arrows. Book II showed why they are not. Axial force, rotational moment, expansive pressure or resultant, spatial basis, material direction, motion, and organization have different units and transformation rules. The translation must begin from that discipline.

#### 5.1 The inherited reduced model

Use `q=(q_a,q_o,q_e)` for reduced axial, opening, and expansive configuration and `Q=(Q_a,Q_o,Q_e)` for their conjugate generalized loads. The exact regional form of each coordinate remains specified by anatomy, reference state, and task. The reduction organizes recurring relations; it does not claim three independent anatomical actuators or three Cartesian forces.

#### 5.2 Configuration, rate, load, and organizational sign

Report `q`, `q-dot`, `Q`, and `s=(s_a,s_o,s_e)` separately. A region can occupy an externally open configuration while experiencing a closing moment, move inward while sustaining outward pressure, or move upward while participating in downward load resolution. The traditional sign proposal must eventually identify which relation is being mapped rather than borrowing the sign of whichever variable is easiest to see.

#### 5.3 Local spatial and material frames

Retain anatomy-fixed and task-fixed bases for expressing vectors and tensors. A local longitudinal tangent, outward normal, and circumferential direction may aid diagrams, but the outward basis vector is not `q_e`, and the circumferential basis vector is not `q_o`. Material-symmetry and principal-stress directions remain other frames that may align, conflict, or change independently.

#### 5.4 The all-Yang sign proposal

State the project tuple as `s=(+,+,+)`: axial Yang downward or caudal, opening Yang external or opening, and expansive Yang outward. It is a tuple in generalized-mode space rather than a physical vector or universal pose. Book III asks whether Six-Division and other maps illuminate the signs; it does not use tradition to settle Book II's competing state, bias, phase, regime, or attractor interpretations.

#### 5.5 Canonical grouping versus mechanical overlay

For every mode, place the traditional division and channel family in one field and the project's mode assignment in another. The first may be historically standard while the second is a contemporary authorial synthesis. A source that supports the channel grouping cannot silently become evidence for the mechanical coordinate.

#### 5.6 Constitutive models remain optional

Orthotropic, transversely isotropic, poroelastic, viscoelastic, shell, rod, and network descriptions may help with selected tissues or regions. None is required by the three-mode proposal, and the existence of three material axes does not prove a Six-Division map. Use a constitutive approximation only after naming the structure, scale, regime, and quantity it improves.

#### 5.7 The common comparison template

Each mode chapter records canonical grouping, sign overlay, coordinate and load, local anatomy, gross-scale relation, traditional or embodied observations, alternatives, and disfavoring results. Repetition here is purposeful: it prevents a vivid example for one pair from becoming generic support for the whole three-mode system.

**Chapter conclusion.** The translation now has a stable mechanical and provenance grammar. Chapter 6 can begin with the axial family, where downward resolution, upward support, stance, suspension, and the body's relation to the ground make complementary signs especially visible.

**Survival work:** Produce explicit generalized-mode and local-frame conventions, free-body and virtual-work examples, bilateral and scale transformations, and tests that distinguish the signs of configuration, motion, load, and routing. Use orthotropic material behavior only as an optional local constitutive approximation where it earns a role.

### 6. Axial organization: Shaoyin and Taiyang

**Chapter summary.** The axial proposal pairs Shaoyin Heart/Kidney with the Yin sign of upward or cranial support and Taiyang Small Intestine/Bladder with the Yang sign of downward or caudal resolution. The chapter examines how those complementary relations could organize support from foot to cranium without equating channel, anatomical structure, and force path.

**Chapter introduction.** Upright life requires downward resolution into support and upward maintenance of form at the same time. The two descriptions are mechanically compatible: ground reaction, pressure, active support, tensile suspension, skeletal contact, and gravity can participate in one event while pointing or acting differently. The axial correspondence asks whether Shaoyin–Taiyang preserves an organized account of that complementarity.

#### 6.1 Canonical division and channels

Present Shaoyin as the Heart and Kidney channels and Taiyang as the Small Intestine and Bladder channels, preserving hand and foot assignments and traditional route distinctions. Document canonical terminology and source variants independently of the project's axial interpretation.

#### 6.2 The proposed Yin and Yang overlay

Assign axial Yin to upward or cranial support and axial Yang to downward or caudal resolution. Explain that “support” and “resolution” name organizational relations rather than mandatory directions of tissue motion. State whether a later example concerns configuration, movement, load, route, or phase.

#### 6.3 The axial coordinate and conjugate load

Define `q_a` relative to a stated longitudinal skeletal route or regional centerline and `Q_a` as the appropriate axial force or resultant when the coordinate is length-like. Compression, elongation, translation, suspension, and downward routing remain different quantities. Curvature requires local tangents rather than one straight global spine axis.

#### 6.4 Local anatomical expressions

Examine feet and ground contact, legs, pelvis and sacrum, vertebral column, thorax, neck, head, and upper-limb suspension. Ask how bone contact, joint geometry, muscle, tendon, pressure, and tensile structures establish downward resolution and upward support at each region. Retain discontinuities and bypasses rather than drawing one uninterrupted cable.

#### 6.5 Relation to the gross limb-role projection

The lower limbs and feet receive an axial emphasis at gross scale, yet the local map also contains axial hand channels—Heart and Small Intestine. Use this difference to show why limb role and channel family are compatible scales rather than competing assignments.
