## #### **Myofascial Trigger Points: Local Consequence, Global Origin**

### Myofascial Trigger Points: Local Consequence, Global Origin

When we consider the body’s intricate tapestry of tension and load, myofascial trigger points (or "knots") often appear as isolated, localized phenomena—tender spots that cause discomfort, restrict movement, or perpetuate pain. But this view — of trigger points as mere local anomalies — misses the deeper truth. These points are not simply random spots of muscle overuse or spillover; rather, they are manifestations of a systemic mechanical disorganization rooted in the body's global load distribution.

**The anatomy of trigger points begins with understanding the fascial continuum and the tensegrity architecture of the body.** Fascia is not just a passive wrapping around muscles; it is an active, tension-bearing network that transmits mechanical forces across vast distances. When the body’s load pathways become misaligned—say, due to anterior loading, poor posture, or habitual compensations—certain regions bear an excessive, often sustained, tension. This overload results in localized contractures and trigger points, serving as the body's attempt to "contain" or resist abnormal stress.

**Think of a suspension bridge:** if one part is overloaded, the tension redistributes along the cables. Over time, the cables closest to the stress points become taut and develop microfocal strains, which can be felt as trigger points. These are not independent “breakdowns,” but rather the body's biomechanical response to a systemic imbalance. The trigger point is the visible tip of a much larger iceberg—an adaptive, yet maladaptive, reorganization of force.

**From a clinical perspective,** this systemic origin explains why treating a trigger point in isolation often yields only temporary relief. If the underlying load pathway remains misaligned—say, the pelvis is rotated, or the thoracic spine is collapsed—the trigger point will inevitably recur. The local knot is a symptom of the body's attempt to maintain structural coherence in the face of ongoing mechanical disharmony.

**Moreover, the trigger point’s behavior is deeply connected to the body's breath and load organization.** During inhalation, posterior chains are engaged, elongating and stabilizing the spine; during exhalation, internal pressure shifts, and soft tissues recoil. If the breath is restricted or misdirected—say, the chest breath remains shallow or front-loaded—the tension in the fascial network becomes uneven. This imbalance fosters the development of trigger points in muscles that are compensating for the lack of systemic coherence.

**An example from embodied practice:** a client with chronic neck pain often exhibits trigger points in the upper trapezius and levator scapulae. But the true origin lies deeper—perhaps a collapsed thoracic spine or a forward head posture driven by anterior load and shallow breathing. The trigger points are the local "hot spots," but they are driven by the global pattern of mechanical overload and dysfunctional load pathways.

**In traditional systems like TCM,** these patterns are mapped as meridian imbalances, often associated with the “governing” or “du” meridian running along the spine and posterior body. The meridians are, in essence, ancient maps of force and tension pathways—bioelectric channels that reflect the underlying biomechanics. When these pathways are constricted or misaligned due to posterior load deficits, trigger points emerge along the fascial and muscular corridors that follow these lines.

**Understanding trigger points as consequences of systemic load misorganization shifts our approach from localized needling or massage to a more comprehensive strategy.** It invites us to ask: *Where is the systemic load pathway compromised?* Is the pelvis rotated? Is the thoracic cage collapsed? Is the breath shallow or anterior?* Correcting these fundamental patterns—restoring posterior load, elongating the spine, and harmonizing breath—dissolves the systemic stress that creates and sustains trigger points.

**In practical terms,** this means that effective treatment involves reestablishing mechanical coherence: guiding the body into posterior loading through mindful movement, breath, and alignment, rather than solely targeting the local knot. When the load pathway rebalances, the trigger points lose their stress source, and softening occurs not as a patch but as a systemic shift.

**To synthesize:** trigger points are the local “symptoms,” but their true roots are systemic. They reflect the body’s mechanical response to unresolved load imbalances, often rooted in anterior loading, poor posture, and dysfunctional breathing. Recognizing this shifts our therapeutic perspective—treat the system, and the local knots will resolve naturally. This understanding opens the door to a more integrated, sustainable approach to healing—one that respects the body’s elegant, systemic design for coherence and resilience.
